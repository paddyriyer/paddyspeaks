Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 76 of 80

Tvam agne dyubhiḥ: Agni born from waters, stone, woods and plants

A Vedic verse to Agni: he flares up quickly and is born from the waters, the stone, the woods and the plants, pure among men.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

You, Agni, with the days; you flare up quickly; you are born from the waters, from the stone, from the woods and the plants; you, lord of men, are born pure among men.

Key concepts अग्नि Agni, fire

7 Explain it to a beginner

This is an old Vedic verse to Agni, the god of fire. It repeats ‘you’ seven times and lists the places fire comes from: it flares up quickly, it is born from the waters, from stone, from the woods and from the plants, and among people it is born pure. The verse names the many places where fire is found or kindled. The only commentator in this edition who explains it, the anonymous one, reads it as continuing the previous line, which asked for protection from death: Agni too is asked to protect. He explains the epithets: Agni quickly dries up the sins of his devotees, and stays pure though he consumes everything. The edition reports this as his reading.

An analogy Like a list of all the places a river is found — in the mountain spring, in the clouds, in the sea: one water, many births.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse say?

It addresses Agni seven times as ‘you’: with the days, quickly flaring, from the waters, out of the stone, from the woods, from the plants; and as lord of men, born pure among men.

Traditional Interpretation

Why is it here, according to the commentator?

The anonymous commentator joins it to the preceding formula: Agni, with all these powers, is asked to protect from death (एवंविधप्रभावस्त्वं मृत्योः सकाशान्मां पाहीति पूर्वेणान्वयः). He explains āśuśukṣaṇi as one who quickly dries up the devotees’ sin, and the purity of fire: वैदिकलौकिकश्मशानिकसर्वपदार्थभुगपि सन्सर्वदा शुचिरेव — though he consumes everything, of the rite, of the world and of the cremation ground, he is always pure.

Traditional Interpretation

Born from the waters?

For the waters the anonymous commentator gives a reason from scripture: fire is prior to water, since water comes from fire, and he cites अग्नेरापः as śruti. He reads ‘stone’ as Meru, on whose peaks the gods dwell. These readings are his.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What is unusual in the wording?

The visarga of aśmanaḥ is written s before pari (aśmanas pari), a Vedic sandhi. The base prints āśuśukṣaṇiḥ with a space, as आशु शुक्षणिः.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • त्वमग्ने tvamagne → त्वम् + अग्ने

    final m before a vowel

    tvam + agne: the m is joined to the vowel.

  • द्युभिस्त्वम् dyubhistvam → द्युभिः + त्वम्

    visarga before t

    dyubhiḥ + tvam → dyubhis tvam: the visarga becomes s before t.

  • अश्मनस्परि aśmanaspari → अश्मनः + परि

    visarga before p (Vedic)

    aśmanaḥ + pari → aśmanas pari: in Vedic texts the visarga before p is written s.

  • त्वमोषधीभ्यस्त्वं tvamoṣadhībhyastvaṃ → त्वम् + ओषधीभ्यः + त्वम्

    final m before a vowel; visarga before t

    tvam + oṣadhībhyaḥ: the m is joined to the vowel; oṣadhībhyaḥ + tvam → oṣadhībhyas tvam.

Morphology

आशुशुक्षणिः
One word, though the base prints it with a space (आशु शुक्षणिः). The anonymous commentator derives it from āśu ‘quickly’ and śuṣ ‘dry up’; the edition reports his derivation.
अश्मनः
Ablative singular of aśman ‘stone’ with the postposition pari, ‘out of the stone’; the visarga is written s before pari.
जायसे
Middle present of √jan, ‘you are born’; the ablatives give where from.

Anvaya

Syntax

A litany of seven ‘you’ clauses addressed to Agni (vocatives agne, nṛpate). One verb, jāyase, ‘you are born’, closes the series and is understood with the ablatives of origin before it (adbhyaḥ, aśmanaḥ pari, vanebhyaḥ, oṣadhībhyaḥ); śuciḥ is its predicate.

Ambiguities

the ablatives
With jāyase they say where Agni is born from. The anonymous commentator reads them otherwise: above the waters, on the stone of Meru (महामेरुरूपस्य पाषाणस्य पर्युपरि तिष्ठसि), in the woods, pervading the plants, taking the cases as exchanged.
construction
The anonymous commentator construes the whole verse with the request of the previous anuvāka, ‘protect me from death’ (मृत्योः सकाशान्मां पाहीति पूर्वेणान्वयः).

Metre

irregular · syllables counted as written: 20 + 20

As printed the two lines count 20 and 20 syllables. No commentary in the edition’s sources names the metre, and the edition does not resolve semivowels to fit one.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Wonder

The verse looks for fire everywhere — in water, stone, wood and plant — and finds one thing in many places. Noticing the same thing at work in very different settings is a way of seeing connections. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Where in everyday life do you find fire or warmth hidden in something that does not look like fire?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The commentator reads the verse as another plea for protection. What does it add to say whom one is asking, and with what powers?

A contemplation exercise Notice today one source of warmth or energy you usually take for granted.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
