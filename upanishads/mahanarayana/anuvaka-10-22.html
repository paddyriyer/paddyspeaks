Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 22 of 80

Namo hiraṇyabāhave: homage to the golden one

A litany of homage to Śiva as golden, as the husband of Ambikā and Umā, and as lord of all creatures.

5 Faithful English translation

Homage to the golden-armed, to the golden-coloured, to the one of golden form, to the lord of gold, to the husband of Ambikā, to the husband of Umā, to the lord of creatures — homage, homage.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Homage to him whose arms are gold, whose colour is gold, whose form is gold, the lord of gold; to the husband of Ambikā, the husband of Umā, the lord of all creatures — homage, and again homage.

Key concepts मन्त्र mantra ईश्वर the Lord

7 Explain it to a beginner

This is a short chant of homage. Seven times it bows to the same god, each time with a new title. Three titles call him golden: golden-armed, golden-coloured, golden in form, and a fourth makes him lord of gold. Two name him as husband of the goddess, by two of her names, Ambikā and Umā. The last calls him Paśupati, lord of all creatures. The old commentators explain each title differently. One says the golden arm stands for a whole golden body; another hears in ‘golden-coloured’ the letters of the Veda, as precious and hard to obtain as gold. The chant ends ‘homage, homage’, the doubled bow that also closes many other Vedic chants to Rudra. It states its praise; it does not say what saying it brings.

An analogy Like a row of lamps lit one after another before the same image: each title is a new light on one figure.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

It offers homage to one figure under seven names: golden-armed, golden-coloured, of golden form, lord of gold, husband of Ambikā, husband of Umā, lord of creatures; and it ends with a doubled homage.

Traditional Interpretation

Who is honoured, and how is the mantra used?

The anonymous commentator introduces it as another mantra to the same deity as the preceding Īśāna mantra, पुनरपि तद्देवताकं मन्त्रान्तरमाह, and ends with homage to Śiva. Sāyaṇa, for whom this is his anuvāka 18 and the third of three on Rudra, says the mantras of the three are known in the manuals as the Tvaritarudra: अनेनानुवाकत्रयेणोक्तानां मन्त्राणां त्वरितरुद्राख्या मन्त्रकल्पेषु प्रसिद्धा. Their use, he adds, is as a supplement to the recitation of the Rudrādhyāya, to be learned from the ritual manuals: तद्विनियोगस्तु रुद्राध्यायजपशेषत्वेन कल्पेषु द्रष्टव्यः.

Traditional Interpretation

Why ‘lord of creatures’?

The anonymous commentator takes paśu, ‘creature’, in the widest sense, all beings from Brahmā down to a blade of grass, and cites a verse: ब्रह्माद्याः स्तम्बपर्यन्ताः पशवः परिकीर्तिताः. For Umā he cites a verse he attributes to the poets: उ मेति मात्रा तपसे निषिद्धा पश्चादुमाख्यां सुमुखी जगाम.

Traditional Interpretation

Ambikā and Umā: two names, one goddess?

Bhāskara offers two readings. The doubling may be for respect, the name Umā being dear to the seer Vāmadeva; or Ambikā is the supreme power as it brings forth the world, सर्वविकल्पोन्मेषवती परा शक्तिः, and Umā the same power at the world’s dissolution, called by a name equal to the praṇava: प्रणवपर्यायेण उमाशब्देनोच्यते.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • नमो हिरण्यबाहवे namo hiraṇyabāhave → नमः + हिरण्यबाहवे

    -aḥ before a voiced consonant

    -aḥ becomes -o before a voiced sound: namaḥ + hiraṇyabāhave → namo hiraṇyabāhave.

  • हिरण्यपतयेऽम्बिकापतय उमापतये hiraṇyapataye'mbikāpataya umāpataye → हिरण्यपतये + अम्बिकापतये + उमापतये

    -e before a; -e before another vowel

    A final e stays before a, which is elided and marked by the avagraha (P. 6.1.109 (एङः पदान्तादति)): hiraṇyapataye ’mbikāpataye. Before u the e becomes ay (P. 6.1.78 (एचोऽयवायावः)) and the y is dropped (P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य)), leaving the vowels apart: ambikāpataya umāpataye.

Morphology

नमः
The neuter noun namas, ‘a bow’, with the dative of the one honoured: namo hiraṇyabāhave, ‘homage to the golden-armed’. The verb ‘be’ is understood. Bhāskara notes that the closing namo namaḥ is not mere repetition, since each carries its own accent: नैतद्द्विर्वचनं द्वितीयस्याप्युदात्तत्वात्.
उमापतये
Ambikā and Umā are two names of the same goddess. Bhāskara explains the doubling either as respect, Umā being the name dear to the seer Vāmadeva, आदरार्थं पुनर्वचनम् उमाशब्दप्रियत्वाद् वामदेवस्य, or as two states of one power.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

One nominal sentence: namaḥ (‘homage [be]’) followed by seven datives, each a compound naming the one honoured, and closed by a doubled namo namaḥ.

Ambiguities

hiraṇyavarṇa
Literally ‘golden-coloured’. The anonymous commentator reads varṇa as the letters of the Veda, which come from him and are as rare as gold to those who have not studied under a teacher; Sāyaṇa and Bhāskara do not take it so.
hiraṇyapati
‘Lord of gold’: guardian of the worshipper’s wealth for Sāyaṇa, keeper of the nine treasures for the anonymous commentator (हिरण्योपलक्षितनवनिधिपालकत्वाद्धिरण्यपतिस्तस्मै), lord of what is good and delightful for Bhāskara.

Metre

prose · syllables counted as written: 45

A yajus litany of 45 syllables in one line, without metrical division.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Attention and praise

A litany says the same thing seven ways. Its point is not information but attention: each new name turns the mind to the same figure from another side. Naming something carefully, again and again, is how attention is held. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Four of the seven titles mention gold. What might gold stand for in a prayer?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The commentators give each title a different sense. Does a litany need one fixed meaning for each name, or does it work by allowing several?

A contemplation exercise Read the seven titles aloud slowly, pausing after each, and notice what image each one raises.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
