Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 6 of 80

Pāhi no agna enase: four offerings for protection

Four short offering verses asking Agni to protect the worshipper, the worshipper’s knowledge, the rite and everything.

5 Faithful English translation

Protect us, O Agni, from sin — svāhā. Protect us for [our] complete knowledge — svāhā. Protect the sacrifice, O rich in radiance — svāhā. Protect everything, O you of a hundred rites — svāhā.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Agni, guard us from wrongdoing; guard us so that we may know fully; radiant one, guard the rite; lord of a hundred rites, guard everything. Svāhā at each.

Key concepts अग्नि Agni, fire मन्त्र mantra ज्ञान knowledge

7 Explain it to a beginner

Four very short offerings, each ending with svāhā, the word spoken as something is placed in a sacred fire. Each asks Agni, the fire, to protect: first “us” from wrongdoing, then us for the sake of full knowledge, then the rite itself, and finally everything. The god is addressed by two old titles, “rich in radiance” and “lord of a hundred rites”. The commentators say these offerings belong to someone seeking liberation: they ask that whatever stands in the way of knowledge be removed, and that the teacher, the scriptures and the other means to knowledge be kept safe. Bhāskara says that from here on the anuvākas are for meditation. The record reports these readings and claims nothing about what the offering achieves.

An analogy Like asking a guard to keep the traveller, the road, the luggage and then simply everything safe.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

Four offerings with svāhā ask Agni to protect us from sin, to protect us for complete knowledge, to protect the sacrifice and to protect everything.

Traditional Interpretation

Who recites it, and why?

The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa: these are homa mantras for one seeking release, to obtain knowledge by removing its obstacles — अथ प्रतिबन्धनिवारणेन मुमुक्षोर्ज्ञानप्राप्त्यर्थं होममन्त्रा उच्यन्ते (anonymous). The sin to be guarded against is what obstructs knowledge (ज्ञानप्रतिबन्धकात्पापात्), and ‘everything’ is the means to knowledge: the teacher, scripture and the rest.

Traditional Interpretation

How does Bhāskara read it?

Bhāskara says the anuvākas from here are for meditation (इत उत्तरे अनुवाका उपासनार्थाः). He takes the sacrifice as one made of knowledge (नो ऽस्मदीयं यज्ञं ज्ञानात्मकं पाहि) and explains enase as a change of case, from sin.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What do the witnesses show?

No differences are listed for this anuvāka. The printed नो अग्न keeps the a unelided, a common Vedic spelling; see the sandhi exception.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • अग्न एनसे agna enase → अग्ने + एनसे

    e before a vowel other than a

    -e becomes -ay before a vowel and the y is dropped, leaving a gap: agna enase.

  • पाहि नो विश्ववेदसे pāhi no viśvavedase → पाहि + नः + विश्ववेदसे

    aḥ before a voiced consonant

    naḥ becomes no before v.

Junctions outside the general rules

  • नः · अग्ने — unjoined hiatus (as printed): no · agne: the a of agne is not elided after no, as often in Vedic recitation (classical no ’gne); every witness prints the two words apart.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • विश्ववेदसे viśvavedase

    karmadhāraya (or bahuvrīhi, of Agni) · विश्व + वेदस्

    Vigraha विश्वं वेदः

    all knowledge; one who knows all

  • विभावसो vibhāvaso

    bahuvrīhi · विभा + वसु

    Vigraha विभा वसु यस्य सः

    whose wealth is radiance

  • शतक्रतो śatakrato

    bahuvrīhi · शत + क्रतु

    Vigraha शतं क्रतवो यस्य सः

    of a hundred sacrifices

Morphology

नः
Vedic: before a, naḥ is written no and the a of agne is not elided (classical no ’gne); the record declares the gap as a sandhi exception.
विश्ववेदसे
Dative. The anonymous commentator reads it as the purpose, ‘for complete knowledge’; Bhāskara as an epithet of the god, ‘to you who know all’ (विश्ववेदसे विश्वस्य वेदित्रे).

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

Four parallel imperative clauses, each pāhi with an object (implied ‘us’, yajñam, sarvam) and a vocative or dative, each closed by svāhā.

Ambiguities

viśvavedase
A purpose (‘for complete knowledge’, the anonymous commentator, Sāyaṇa) or an epithet of the god (‘to you who know all’, Bhāskara).

Metre

anuṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 10 + 10 + 10 + 10

Bhāskara calls it an anuṣṭubh whose four pādas each end in svāhā (स्वाहाकारान्तपादचतुष्टया अनुष्टुबेषा). With svāhā each printed line counts 10 syllables (8 + 2).

Textual variations between witnesses

The witnesses differ here only in spelling (listed in section 14).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Protecting the conditions for learning

The prayer asks protection not only for the learner but for the rite, the teacher and the books — the conditions under which learning happens. Looking after those conditions is part of learning anything well. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin The prayer moves from “us” to “the rite” to “everything”. Why that order?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Is “knowledge” here the goal of the rite (the anonymous commentator) or the nature of the rite itself (Bhāskara’s “sacrifice made of knowledge”)?

A contemplation exercise Name one condition your own learning depends on, and do one thing to protect it.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
