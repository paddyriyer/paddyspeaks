Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 39 of 80

Brahma medhayā: the second trisuparṇa

The second trisuparṇa: brahman, the sweet, is reached through medhā; prayers to Savitṛ to send good and drive away evil; the ‘honey’ verses asking that the whole world be sweet; and the brāhmaṇa lines on its giving and recitation.

5 Faithful English translation

Brahman by medhā; sweetness by medhā; brahman itself, sweetness, by medhā. Today, god Savitṛ, impel for us good fortune with offspring; drive away what comes of bad dreams. All evils, god Savitṛ, drive away; what is good, that send to me. Sweetly [blow] the winds for one who seeks the right order; the rivers pour sweetness; sweet be the plants for us. Sweet [be] the night and the dawn; full of sweetness the earthly region; sweet be the sky, our father. Full of sweetness for us be the tree, full of sweetness be the sun; sweet be the cows for us. Whoever gives this trisuparṇa unasked to a brāhmaṇa — these truly destroy [the sin of] killing an embryo. The brāhmaṇas who recite the trisuparṇa attain soma. They purify the row up to a thousand. Om.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Brahman is reached through understanding; the sweet is reached through understanding; Brahman itself, the sweet, through understanding. Savitṛ, give us today good fortune and descendants, and send away whatever bad dreams bring. Send away every wrong, Savitṛ, and send me what is good. May the winds blow sweetly for one who seeks the true order; may the rivers flow sweet; may the plants be sweet for us; sweet the night and the dawn, sweet the dust of the earth, sweet the sky, our father; sweet the trees, sweet the sun, sweet the cows. The text adds that those who teach this unasked destroy the sin of killing the unborn, that its reciters attain soma, and that they purify a row of a thousand at the meal. Om.

7 Explain it to a beginner

The second trisuparṇa opens by saying that Brahman — which is also called ‘the sweet’ — is reached through medhā, the power to take in a teaching and hold on to it. Then it prays to Savitṛ, the god who sets things moving: give us good fortune today, drive away what bad dreams bring, drive away every wrong, send me what is good. The best-known lines follow, the ‘honey verses’: may the winds blow sweetly, the rivers flow sweet, the plants be sweet; sweet the night and the dawn, the earth, the sky our father, the trees, the sun, the cows. The old commentator reads all this as a student’s prayer that nothing in the world get in the way of learning. It ends, like the first, with the text’s own promises to those who teach and recite it.

An analogy Like a student who asks, before an exam, for a quiet room, a clear head, good food and no bad news.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

That brahman, the sweet, is reached by medhā; it asks Savitṛ to send good fortune and drive evils away; it wishes that winds, rivers, plants, night and dawn, earth, sky, trees, sun and cows be sweet; and it states the reward of giving and reciting the trisuparṇa.

Traditional Interpretation

What is medhā here?

The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa: the power to retain the great sayings taught by the teacher and their meaning, by which brahman is gained: तन्मेधया गुरूपदिष्टमहावाक्यतदर्थधारणशक्त्या लभ्यतामिति शेषः. Bhāskara: brahman is reached or known by a special understanding (मेधाविशेषेण प्राप्यते ज्ञायते वा), and the verses that follow ask for it.

Traditional Interpretation

Why pray for sweet winds and rivers?

The anonymous commentator: a strong wind brings illness and hinders knowledge, so its favour is asked, and the same holds for each wish that follows: प्रबले तु वायौ रोगोत्पत्त्या तत्त्वज्ञानविघ्नः संपद्यतेऽतः स मा भूदिति वायोरानुकूल्यं प्रार्थ्यते. Bhāskara: here too the honey is knowledge of the self: अत्राप्यात्मज्ञानं मधु.

Traditional Interpretation

Where are the Savitṛ verses explained?

Sāyaṇa and Bhāskara both say these verses were explained earlier in their texts: Sāyaṇa refers to the anuvāka aṇor aṇīyān (अद्या नो देवेत्या कमृक्पञ्चकमणोरणीयानित्यनुवाके व्याख्यातम्), Bhāskara, who calls them two gāyatrīs (अद्या न इति द्वे गायत्र्यौ), to the passage beginning ‘uta naḥ priyā’.

Traditional Interpretation

What sin does this trisuparṇa remove?

The killing of an unborn child: ब्राह्मणगर्भस्य राजगर्भस्य वा हननं भ्रूणहत्या (the anonymous commentator); Bhāskara: the killing of a brāhmaṇa learned in the Veda and its auxiliaries (वेदवेदाङ्गविदो ब्राह्मणस्य वधः). The anonymous commentator later ranks the three: brahmahatyā is grave, bhrūṇahatyā graver, vīrahatyā gravest (10.41).

Where they disagree

No Vedānta-school commentary on this text is in the edition’s sources. The anonymous commentator reads the bad dream as the appearance of duality (द्वैतप्रतिभासं), an Advaita reading he gives in passing; Bhāskara reads the honey as knowledge of the self. Neither argues against another school, and Raṅgarāmānuja’s commentary on the Mahānārāyaṇa is not among the sources.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • अद्या नो देव adyā no deva → अद्या + नः + देव

    aḥ before a voiced consonant

    naḥ + deva → no deva (P. 6.1.114 (हशि च)).

  • सवितर्दुरितानि savitarduritāni → सवितः + दुरितानि

    r-visarga before a voiced sound

    The visarga of savitaḥ stands for r, which shows before a voiced consonant.

  • तन्म आसुव tanma āsuva → तत् + मे + आसुव

    t before m; e before a vowel

    tat + me → tan me (P. 8.4.45 (यरोऽनुनासिकेऽनुनासिको वा)); me + āsuva → ma āsuva: e becomes ay and the y drops (P. 6.1.78 (एचोऽयवायावः), P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य)).

  • वाता ऋतायते vātā ṛtāyate → वाताः + ऋतायते

    āḥ before a vowel

    -āḥ loses its visarga before a vowel (P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि), P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य)).

  • सन्त्वोषधीः santvoṣadhīḥ → सन्तु + ओषधीः

    u before a dissimilar vowel

    santu + oṣadhīḥ → santv oṣadhīḥ (P. 6.1.77 (इको यणचि)).

  • नक्तमुतोषसि naktamutoṣasi → नक्तम् + उत + उषसि

    a + u

    uta + uṣasi → utoṣasi (P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः)).

  • मधुमां अस्तु madhumāṃ astu → मधुमान् + अस्तु

    final -ān before a vowel (Vedic)

    In the Veda a final -n after ā before a vowel becomes ṃ (P. 8.3.9 (दीर्घादटि समानपादे)); the Ṛgveda has madhumāṁ astu.

  • माध्वीर्गावो mādhvīrgāvo → माध्वीः + गावः

    īḥ before a voiced sound

    The visarga after ī becomes r before a voiced consonant.

  • य इमं ya imaṃ → यः + इमम्

    aḥ before a vowel other than a

    yaḥ + imam → ya imam (P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य)).

  • ब्राह्मणास्त्रिसुपर्णं brāhmaṇāstrisuparṇaṃ → ब्राह्मणाः + त्रिसुपर्णम्

    visarga before t

    brāhmaṇāḥ + trisuparṇam → brāhmaṇās trisuparṇam: the visarga becomes s before t (P. 8.3.34 (विसर्जनीयस्य सः)).

  • आसहस्रात्पङ्क्तिं āsahasrātpaṅktiṃ → आ + सहस्रात् + पङ्क्तिम्

    words written together

    ā and sahasrāt are written as one; final t stays before p.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • भ्रूणहत्याम् bhrūṇahatyām

    ṣaṣṭhī-tatpuruṣa · भ्रूण + हत्या

    Vigraha भ्रूणस्य हत्या

    the killing of an embryo

  • त्रिसुपर्णम् trisuparṇam

    dvigu (a name: ‘the three suparṇas’) · त्रि + सुपर्ण

    Vigraha त्रयः सुपर्णाः यस्मिन्

    the three-suparṇa [text]

Morphology

अद्या
adya ‘today’ with its final vowel lengthened, as often in the Ṛgveda; the anonymous commentator glosses it ‘on this day’.
सावीः
An s-aorist of √sū without augment, used as a request (‘send!’); the anonymous commentator: सावीः प्रेरय.
मधुमान्
The text prints madhu mān no, with a space, for madhumān no; the words are the same as in the Ṛgveda and the Bṛhadāraṇyaka (see parallels).

Anvaya

ब्रह्म मेधया [लभ्यताम्] । मधु मेधया । ब्रह्मम् एव मधु मेधया । देव सवितः, अद्या नः प्रजावत् सौभगम् सावीः । दुष्ष्वप्नियम् परा सुव । देव सवितः, विश्वानि दुरितानि परासुव । यत् भद्रम् तत् मे आसुव । ऋतायते वाताः मधु [वान्तु] । सिन्धवः मधु क्षरन्ति । ओषधीः नः माध्वीः सन्तु । नक्तम् उत उषसि मधु [अस्तु] । पार्थिवम् रजः मधुमत् [अस्तु] । नः पिता द्यौः मधु अस्तु । वनस्पतिः नः मधुमान् [अस्तु] । सूर्यः मधुमान् अस्तु । गावः नः माध्वीः भवन्तु । यः इमम् त्रिसुपर्णम् अयाचितम् ब्राह्मणाय दद्यात्, एते वै भ्रूणहत्याम् घ्नन्ति । ये ब्राह्मणाः त्रिसुपर्णम् पठन्ति ते सोमम् प्राप्नुवन्ति । [ते] आ सहस्रात् पङ्क्तिम् पुनन्ति । ओम् ।

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Three verbless statements with medhayā (the verb ‘is reached’ supplied); then imperatives to Savitṛ (sāvīḥ, parā … suva, parāsuva, āsuva) with the relative clause yad bhadram; then a string of wishes with madhu/mādhvīḥ/madhumat as predicates and imperatives of √as and √bhū, some verbs supplied. The brāhmaṇa part has a singular relative (yaḥ … dadyāt) taken up by a plural (ete … ghnanti).

Ambiguities

medhayā
The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa supply ‘is to be gained’ (brahman is gained by the power to retain the teaching); Bhāskara reads ‘is reached or known by a special understanding’, and the following verses as a prayer for that understanding (अथ तादृशीं मेधां प्रार्थयते).
the honey verses
The anonymous commentator reads each wish as a prayer that nothing — wind, water, food, time, place — hinder the student’s learning; Bhāskara says the honey here is knowledge of the self (अत्राप्यात्मज्ञानं मधु).
bhrūṇahatyā
The killing of an unborn child (of a brāhmaṇa or a king: the anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa) or of a brāhmaṇa learned in the Veda (Bhāskara).

Metre

mixed · syllables counted as written: 5 + 5 + 9 + 16 + 8 + 16 + 8 + 16 + 7 + 16 + 8 + 15 + 8 + 17 + 9 + 11 + 7 + 9 + 1

Prose formulas; then gāyatrī verses (Bhāskara: अद्या न इति द्वे गायत्र्यौ, मधु वाता ऋतायत इति तिस्त्रो गायत्र्यः), and the brāhmaṇa in prose.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Conditions for learning

The commentator reads a hymn about sweet winds and sweet water as a list of what a student needs: health, food, rest, a safe place, no disturbing news. Learning depends on conditions, not only on effort. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin If you could ask for one thing to be ‘sweet’ around you so that you could learn better, what would it be?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Bhāskara says the honey is knowledge of the self. What changes if everything in the world is asked to be sweet for the sake of knowing?

A contemplation exercise Before study, set out what you need — water, light, quiet — as the hymn does, one item at a time.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
