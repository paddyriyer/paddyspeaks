Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 42 of 80

Medhāṃ ma indraḥ: understanding asked of the gods

Medhā is asked of Indra, Sarasvatī and the Aśvins; then the medhā found among the Apsarases, the Gandharvas and the gods is asked to favour the speaker, with svāhā.

5 Faithful English translation

Medhā may Indra give me; medhā the goddess Sarasvatī; medhā may the two Aśvins, both, place in me, wearing lotus garlands. The medhā that is in the Apsarases, and the mind that is in the Gandharvas, divine medhā, Sarasvatī — may that medhā, the fragrant, favour me. Svāhā.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

May Indra give me understanding; may the goddess Sarasvatī give it; may both Aśvins, garlanded with lotuses, set it in me. The understanding of the heavenly nymphs, the mind of the heavenly singers, the understanding of the gods, Sarasvatī herself — may that understanding come to me, fragrant and generous. Svāhā.

Key concepts मेधा medhā, retentive intelligence देवता deity गन्धर्व gandharva, celestial musician मन्त्र mantra

7 Explain it to a beginner

This short prayer asks several gods for medhā — understanding, and the power to keep what one learns. First Indra, king of the gods; then Sarasvatī, goddess of speech and learning; then the two Aśvins, the twin gods, garlanded with lotuses. The second verse widens the request: the understanding found among the heavenly nymphs, the mind of the heavenly singers, the understanding of the gods, and Sarasvatī herself — may all of it come to me, sweet and generous. It ends with svāhā, the word said when an offering is made into the fire, so the prayer is meant to accompany an offering.

An analogy Like a student writing to several teachers at once, asking each for whatever they can share.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

It asks Indra, Sarasvatī and the two Aśvins to give the speaker medhā, and asks the medhā of the Apsarases, the Gandharvas and the gods, Sarasvatī, to favour him, with svāhā.

Traditional Interpretation

How do the commentators read it?

The anonymous commentator: a prayer to Indra and the others, who grant medhā (मेधाप्रदानिन्द्रादीन्प्रार्थयितुं मन्त्रान्तरमाह), and then a further mantra that grants medhā, asking the medhā found in the divine women, the mind of the gods’ singers, the medhā of Hiraṇyagarbha and the other gods, and Sarasvatī in the form of the Vedas, to come fragrant and wish-yielding to the speaker. Sāyaṇa reads the same; he adds the medhā of men learned in the Vedas and śāstras (मनुष्यजा मनुष्येषु वेदशास्त्रवित्सु या मेधाऽस्ति).

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • म इन्द्रो ma indro → मे + इन्द्रः

    e before a vowel; aḥ before a voiced sound

    me + indraḥ → ma indraḥ (P. 6.1.78 (एचोऽयवायावः), P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य)); indraḥ + dadātu → indro dadātu.

  • अश्विनावुभावाधत्तां aśvināvubhāvādhattāṃ → अश्विनौ + उभौ + आधत्ताम्

    au before a vowel

    au becomes āv before a vowel (P. 6.1.78 (एचोऽयवायावः)).

  • यन्मनः yanmanaḥ → यत् + मनः

    t before m

    yat + manaḥ → yan manaḥ (P. 8.4.45 (यरोऽनुनासिकेऽनुनासिको वा)).

  • जुषताग् स्वाहा juṣatāg svāhā → जुषताम् + स्वाहा

    final m before a sibilant

    The m becomes anusvāra before s (P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः)); the Taittirīya recitation writes it ग्.

Junctions outside the general rules

  • मे · अश्विनौ — unjoined hiatus (as printed): me · aśvinau: the e of me kept before a without elision (classical me ’śvinau); a Vedic hiatus (prakṛtibhāva, P. 6.1.115 (प्रकृत्यान्तःपादमव्यपरे)), printed so in every witness.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • पुष्करस्रजा puṣkarasrajā

    bahuvrīhi · पुष्कर + स्रज्

    Vigraha पुष्कराणां स्रजौ ययोः तौ

    wearing lotus garlands

Morphology

पुष्करस्रजा
A Vedic dual in -ā for -au, agreeing with aśvinau; the anonymous commentator restores पुष्करस्रजौ.
दैवीम्
Accusative in form where a nominative is meant; the commentator: दैवीं व्यत्ययेन दैवी.
अप्सरासु
For apsaraḥsu / apsarassu, from the stem apsaras, treated as an ā-stem.

Anvaya

इन्द्रः मे मेधाम् ददातु । देवी सरस्वती मेधाम् [ददातु] । पुष्करस्रजा उभौ अश्विनौ मे मेधाम् आधत्ताम् । अप्सरासु च या मेधा, गन्धर्वेषु च यत् मनः, दैवीम् मेधा, सरस्वती, सा मेधा सुरभिः माम् जुषताम् । स्वाहा ।

indraḥ me medhām dadātu | devī sarasvatī medhām dadātu | puṣkarasrajā ubhau aśvinau me medhām ādhattām | apsarāsu ca yā medhā, gandharveṣu ca yat manaḥ, daivīm medhā, sarasvatī, sā medhā surabhiḥ mām juṣatām | svāhā |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Two verses. The first: three imperatives (dadātu, said once and understood for Sarasvatī; ādhattām) with medhām as object. The second: relative clauses (yā medhā, yan manaḥ) and appositions resumed by sā … medhā, with the imperative juṣatām and the call svāhā.

Ambiguities

two verses or two mantras
The anonymous commentator treats the second verse as a further mantra granting medhā (मेधाप्रदं मन्त्रान्तरमाह); Sāyaṇa comments on the two verses as his anuvākas 40 and 41.
surabhiḥ
‘Fragrant’ or ‘the wish-yielding cow’; the anonymous commentator gives both.

Metre

anuṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 16 + 16 + 16 + 20

Two anuṣṭubh verses, with svāhā at the end outside the metre.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Learning from many

The prayer asks for understanding from many quarters at once — from the gods of power and of speech, and from the twin Aśvins, and from the singers and dancers of heaven. Understanding, it suggests, can be gathered from very different kinds of minds. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Who are the different people you have learned from, and what did each give you?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The verse places medhā among singers and dancers as well as among gods of speech. What kinds of understanding live in music and movement?

A contemplation exercise Ask one person today to explain something they know well, and listen.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
