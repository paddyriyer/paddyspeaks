Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 62 of 80

Manyur akārṣīt: “anger did it”

An offering formula in which the speaker attributes past wrongs to anger, not to himself, and offers ghee to anger as a deity; its pair, 10.61, does the same with desire.

5 Faithful English translation

Anger has done [it]; homage, homage. Anger has done [it]; anger does [it], I do not do [it]; anger is the doer, I am not the doer; anger is the one who causes [it] to be done, I am not the one who causes [it] to be done. This [offering] is for you, O Anger, for anger. Svāhā.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Anger did it — homage, homage. Anger did it: anger acts, not I; anger is the doer, not I; anger drives the deed, not I. This offering is yours, Anger: to Anger, svāhā.

Key concepts कर्मन् action, ritual work मन्त्र mantra त्याग giving up, relinquishment

7 Explain it to a beginner

This is a short offering formula. The speaker says that anger did the wrong — not he himself. Anger acted, anger was the doer, anger made him do it; then he offers a spoonful of ghee into the fire to Anger, honoured as a deity, with the word svāhā. The previous anuvāka has the same words for desire. The old commentator does not read this as an excuse. He says it is spoken by someone in remorse, who feels pushed by desire and anger into acts he did not want, and asks the deity to take the wrong away. The edition reports that reading and makes no claim about what the rite achieves.

An analogy Like someone who, looking back on an outburst, says “that was my temper talking”, and wants to be rid of the temper rather than to escape the blame.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the formula say?

It salutes, then three times sets anger against ‘I’: anger does, is the doer, has [it] done; I do not, am not, have not. It ends by offering ‘this’ to Anger with svāhā.

Traditional Interpretation

How does the commentator gloss anger?

He makes Manyu the deity who presides over anger, मन्युः कोपाभिमानी देव, and the dative ‘to Manyu’ as ‘whose form is anger’, मन्यवे क्रोधरूपाय; the rest is as in the formula on desire: अन्यत्पूर्ववत्.

Traditional Interpretation

Is the formula an excuse?

He raises the question as a point of mīmāṃsā: कारयितुरेव पापं न कर्तुरनिच्छतः — the sin belongs to the one who makes another act, not to the unwilling doer; but यदि स्वयमपीच्छत्यन्योऽपि कारयति तर्ह्युभयोरपि पापलेपः — if the doer also wants it, both are stained. To the objection that the dharmaśāstras prescribe expiation even for sins done under compulsion, ननु बलात्कारितपापस्यापि धर्मशास्त्रेषु प्रायश्चित्तं श्रूयत इति चेन्न, he answers that such sins are lighter. He pictures the speaker lifting his hands and weeping: काममन्यू बलात्पापं मां कारयतोऽनिच्छन्तमपि किं करोमि क्व गच्छामि — desire and anger force sin on me against my will; what can I do, where can I go? — and concludes तस्माद्रोदनपश्चातापादिवशादेवमवगम्यते कारयितुरेव पापं न कर्तुरिति.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • मन्युरकार्षीन्मन्युः manyurakārṣīnmanyuḥ → मन्युः + अकार्षीत् + मन्युः

    -uḥ before a vowel; t before a nasal

    manyuḥ + akārṣīt → manyur akārṣīt: after a vowel other than a/ā the visarga becomes r before a voiced sound; akārṣīt + manyuḥ → akārṣīn manyuḥ: t becomes n before m.

  • मन्युरकार्षीन्नमो manyurakārṣīnnamo → मन्युः + अकार्षीत् + नमः

    final t before a nasal

    akārṣīt + namaḥ → akārṣīn namaḥ: t becomes n before n.

  • नाहं nāhaṃ → न + अहम्

    a + a → ā

    na + aham → nāham.

  • कारयिता एष kārayitā eṣa → कारयिता + एषः

    unjoined vowels; eṣaḥ before a consonant

    kārayitā eṣaḥ is printed without joining the vowels (see sandhi_exceptions); eṣaḥ + te → eṣa te: eṣaḥ drops its visarga before a consonant.

Junctions outside the general rules

  • कारयिता · एषः — hiatus at a phrase boundary: kārayitā eṣa is printed with the vowels apart, at the break between the confession and the offering formula.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this anuvāka.

Morphology

अकार्षीत्
s-aorist 3rd singular of √kṛ: ‘has done’ — the deed is past and complete.
कर्ता
Agent noun in -tṛ: ‘the doer’, set against kārayitā, the causative agent noun ‘the one who has [it] done’.
कारयिता
Agent noun from the causative kāraya-: ‘the one who causes [another] to act’.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

A short salutation, then three parallel pairs of clauses, each setting anger against ‘I’ with the negation na: anger does / I do not; anger is the doer / I am not; anger has it done / I do not. The last sentence is an offering formula: eṣa te … svāhā, ‘this is for you’, with the vocative and the dative of the deity.

Ambiguities

whose deed
The text names no deed; as with 10.61, the anonymous commentator takes it as the sins already spoken of (अन्यत्पूर्ववत् — the rest as before).
does the formula deny responsibility?
Read literally, the speaker says he is not the doer. The commentator discusses this directly (see commentary): the guilt lies with the one who compels, not with an unwilling doer, and the formula is spoken in remorse.

Metre

prose · syllables counted as written: 9 + 45

A prose formula (yajus). The salutation counts 9 syllables, the rest 45.

Textual variations between witnesses

The witnesses differ here only in spelling (listed in section 14).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Self-mastery

The formula separates the self from an impulse it does not endorse: “anger did it, not I.” Read with the commentator, it is not an alibi but a way of naming the impulse so that it can be given up. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin When anger pushes you into something, do you experience it as “me” or as something acting on you?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The commentator says the guilt lies with the one who compels, not the unwilling doer, yet the speaker still makes an offering. What does that combination say about responsibility?

A contemplation exercise Next time anger rises, name it silently (“this is anger”) before you act.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
