Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 53 of 80

Mā nas toke: spare our children, our life, our cattle and our men

Rudra is asked not to harm the speakers in their children and descendants, their life, their cattle and horses, and not to strike their men in anger; they worship him with oblations and homage.

5 Faithful English translation

Do not harm us in [our] offspring and descendant, us in [our] life, us in [our] cattle, us in [our] horses. Do not, O Rudra, angered, strike our men. Bearing oblations, we would worship you with homage.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Rudra, do not harm us in our children and grandchildren, in our lives, our cattle or our horses; even if we have angered you, do not strike our men. We come with offerings and bow to you.

7 Explain it to a beginner

This is the companion of the previous prayer and, like it, is addressed to Rudra. It asks him not to harm the speakers in the things on which a household depends: children and grandchildren, life itself, cattle and horses. It adds: even if you are angry, do not strike our men. It closes with the worshippers coming with offerings and bowing. The old commentator explains that the anger meant is anger caused by our own faults, and that the worshippers hold the ladles with the offering ready. The verse is also in the Ṛgveda and in the Śrī Rudram, where its last words differ. In this Upaniṣad it stands among the short prayers for protection before the offering formulas. The edition reports this and makes no claim about what reciting it achieves.

An analogy Like a farmer’s prayer before the season: name the children, the life of the house, the herds, and ask that none be lost.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

It asks Rudra not to harm the speakers in their offspring, their life, their cattle and their horses, and, though angered, not to strike their men; it ends with the speakers worshipping him with oblations and homage.

Traditional Interpretation

How does the commentator explain it?

The anonymous commentator takes the list as everything the speakers have: एतदुपलक्षितमस्मदीयं सर्वस्व मा रीरिषः — do not harm all that is ours, of which these are examples. The anger is anger caused by our own offences: भामितोऽस्मदपराधैः कारितक्रोधः. The homage is a bow, नमसा प्रणामेन, and the worship a sacrifice: ते तवेज्यां विधेम कुर्याम.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

How does it differ from the Ṛgveda?

The Ṛgveda has the verse at 1.114.8. There the second word of life is āyau, मा न आयौ, where the Mahānārāyaṇa has āyuṣi, both locatives of ‘life’; and the last pāda reads हविष्मन्तःसदमित् त्वा हवामहे, ‘bearing oblations, we call on you always’, where this text has हविष्मन्तो नमसा विधेम ते, ‘we would worship you with homage’.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • नस्तोके nastoke → नः + तोके

    visarga before t

    naḥ before t becomes nas.

  • न आयुषि na āyuṣi → नः + आयुषि

    visarga after a before a vowel other than a

    naḥ before ā drops its visarga and stands in hiatus: na āyuṣi.

  • नो अश्वेषु no aśveṣu → नः + अश्वेषु

    visarga after a before a; the a kept

    naḥ before a becomes no, and the initial a of aśveṣu is kept, not elided: see sandhi exceptions.

  • वीरान्मा vīrānmā → वीरान् + मा

    final n before m

    vīrān keeps its n before m and is written joined.

  • भामितो वधीर्हविष्मन्तो bhāmito vadhīrhaviṣmanto → भामितः + वधीः + हविष्मन्तः

    visarga after a before a voiced consonant; after ī before a voiced consonant

    bhāmitaḥ before v becomes bhāmito; vadhīḥ before h becomes vadhīr; haviṣmantaḥ before n becomes haviṣmanto.

Junctions outside the general rules

  • नः · अश्वेषु — vedic-prakṛtibhāva: naḥ aśveṣu is written no aśveṣu: the visarga becomes o, but the initial a is kept, as in the Ṛgveda’s text of this verse, where classical sandhi would elide it.

Morphology

तोके
The locatives toke, tanaye, āyuṣi, goṣu, aśveṣu say in what respect the speakers are not to be harmed: ‘do not harm us in our children …’, a common Vedic construction with √riṣ.
हविष्मन्तः
Possessive adjective in -mat from haviṣ, ‘oblation’: ‘having oblations’, i.e. bringing them. The commentator: यथाविधिनिष्पादितहविर्युक्तजुहूहस्ता वयं — we, with ladles in hand holding the oblation prepared as prescribed.

Anvaya

हे रुद्र नः तोके तनये मा रीरिषः नः आयुषि मा [रीरिषः] नः गोषु मा [रीरिषः] नः अश्वेषु मा रीरिषः । भामितः नः वीरान् मा वधीः । हविष्मन्तः [वयम्] नमसा ते विधेम ।

he rudra naḥ toke tanaye mā rīriṣaḥ naḥ āyuṣi mā rīriṣaḥ naḥ goṣu mā rīriṣaḥ naḥ aśveṣu mā rīriṣaḥ | bhāmitaḥ naḥ vīrān mā vadhīḥ | haviṣmantaḥ vayam namasā te vidhema |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Four prohibitions in the first line, each mā naḥ with a locative of respect, the verb rīriṣaḥ coming once at the end. The second line has a further prohibition with its own verb (vadhīḥ) and object (vīrān), the vocative rudra and the participle bhāmitaḥ qualifying the god, then a closing optative (vidhema) with haviṣmantaḥ qualifying the speakers.

Ambiguities

bhāmitaḥ
‘Angered’ may describe Rudra as already angry or as one who might be angered; the commentator reads it as anger caused by our offences, भामितोऽस्मदपराधैः कारितक्रोधः.

Metre

jagatī · syllables counted as written: 24 + 22

As printed the first line has 24 syllables (12 + 12) and the second 22 (11 + 11): the last pāda, haviṣmanto namasā vidhema te, has eleven syllables where the jagatī norm is twelve. The Ṛgveda’s version of this pāda is different (see commentary).

Textual variations between witnesses

  • Witness error Ānandāśrama pariśiṣṭa text (1927): न आयुषि प्ना नो गोषु — The Ānandāśrama pariśiṣṭa text alone reads प्न for म; the base and the Tirupati e-text agree.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Responsibility

The prayer admits that the god may be angry and that the speakers may be the cause, and asks that the consequences not fall on the household. Owning a fault while asking that others not suffer for it is a demanding stance. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Which of the things named here — children, life, livelihood — feels most fragile to you?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The commentator says the god’s anger is caused by our offences. How does that change the request ‘do not strike us’?

A contemplation exercise When you are tempted to let someone else bear the cost of your mistake, name the mistake first.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
