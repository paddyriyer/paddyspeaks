Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 8 of 80

Yaś chandasām ṛṣabhaḥ: om, the bull of the metres

A japa for one who wishes to obtain the Veda: om, foremost of the Vedic words, and the lord who taught the upaniṣad to the seers.

5 Faithful English translation

Who, the bull of the metres, of every form, [arose] from the metres [and] entered the metres — reachable by the good — the eldest Indra declared the upaniṣad to the seers for power. Homage to the gods; svadhā to the fathers. Earth, mid-air, heaven: the Veda — om.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Om, the foremost of the Vedic words, which holds every form, came from the Vedas and entered every metre. Reachable by the good, the first lord taught the secret teaching to the seers, to give them the power to know. Homage to the gods, svadhā to the ancestors. Earth, mid-air, heaven: may the Veda be mine — om.

7 Explain it to a beginner

This anuvāka praises a “who” it never names. The commentators say it is om, the sacred syllable. It is called the bull, that is, the foremost, of the Vedic metres; it takes every form; it came out of the Vedas and then entered every metre, so that it is said at the start of every mantra. The second line says that the first lord, Indra (understood by the commentators as the supreme self), declared the secret teaching to the seers to give them the power to know, and that he can be reached by the good. Then come the familiar greetings to the gods and the ancestors, and the three vyāhṛtis with the word for the Veda. The anonymous commentator calls it a prayer to be recited by someone who wishes to receive the whole Veda; the record reports this and claims nothing about its effect.

An analogy Like the keynote a singer hums before every song: drawn from the songs, and then present at the start of each one.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

It describes ‘one who’ is foremost of the metres, of every form, who came from the metres and entered them; it says the eldest Indra, reachable by the good, declared the upaniṣad to the seers; and it closes with the salutations, the vyāhṛtis, the word chandaḥ and om.

Traditional Interpretation

What is the ‘one who’?

Sāyaṇa and the anonymous commentator: om, the praṇava — यः प्रणवश्छन्दसां वेदानां मध्य ऋषभः श्रेष्ठः (Sāyaṇa). It came from the Vedas as their essence and entered the metres because it is said at the beginning of every mantra; Sāyaṇa quotes the Prapañcasāra for this: अस्य तु वेदादित्वात्सर्वमनूनां प्रयुज्यते ह्यादौ.

Traditional Interpretation

Who is Indra here, and what did he teach?

Sāyaṇa: Indra is the supreme self, endowed with supreme lordship, the one om designates (तेन प्रणवेन प्रतिपाद्य इन्द्रः परमैश्वर्ययुक्तः); he taught the upaniṣad, the knowledge of Brahman established by all the Vedānta, to the seers — प्रोवाचोपनिषदं ब्रह्मविद्यां सर्ववेदान्तसिद्धामुक्तवान्. Bhāskara similarly takes Indra as the supreme self who is breath (इन्द्र ईश्वरः प्राणात्मा परमात्मोच्यते), and concludes that om, as the three vyāhṛtis, is to be meditated on.

Traditional Interpretation

What is the anuvāka recited for?

The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa: it is a japa for one who wishes to obtain the whole Vedānta that teaches knowledge — ज्ञानप्रतिपादककृत्स्नवेदान्तप्राप्तिकामेन जप्यं मन्त्रमाह (the anonymous commentator; Sāyaṇa has the same sentence); homage to the gods and fathers is made to remove obstacles, and the closing words mean ‘I obtain the Vedas present in the three worlds’. Sāyaṇa notes that some therefore read the close as भूर्भुवः सुवश्छन्द ओम् (एतदेवाभिप्रेत्य केचिद्भूर्भुवः सुवश्छन्द ओमिति पठन्ति), as this text does.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What do the witnesses show?

The base prints श्चन्दाग्स्य, with च after the visarga; the Ānandāśrama text reads श्छन्दाँस्य (listed for review). For पुरोवाच the Tirupati e-text reads प्रोवाच, the form both commentators gloss; the base and the Ānandāśrama text agree on पुरोवाच, so the sources page counts the Tirupati reading as a witness error. The Ānandāśrama text’s सचां for सतां is also listed.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • यश्छन्दसामृषभो yaśchandasāmṛṣabho → यः + छन्दसाम् + ऋषभः

    visarga before ch; final m before a vowel; aḥ before a voiced consonant

    yaḥ becomes yaś before ch; chandasām joins ṛṣabhaḥ; ṛṣabhaḥ becomes ṛṣabho before v.

  • छन्दोभ्यश्चन्दाग्स्याविवेश chandobhyaścandāgsyāviveśa → छन्दोभ्यः + चन्दाग्सि + आविवेश

    visarga before c; i before ā

    The visarga becomes ś before c; the final i becomes y before ā. The Taittirīya recitation writes the anusvāra of chandāṃsi as ग्स्.

  • पुरोवाचोपनिषदिन्द्रो purovācopaniṣadindro → पुरोवाच + उपनिषत् + इन्द्रः

    a + u; t before a vowel; aḥ before a voiced consonant

    a + u → o (uvācopaniṣad); t → d before a vowel (upaniṣad indro).

  • ज्येष्ठ इन्द्रियाय jyeṣṭha indriyāya → ज्येष्ठः + इन्द्रियाय

    aḥ before a vowel other than a

    -aḥ loses its visarga before a vowel other than a, leaving a gap: jyeṣṭha indriyāya.

  • सुवश्छन्द ओम् suvaśchanda om → सुवः + छन्दः + ओम्

    visarga before ch; aḥ before o

    suvaś chanda om.

Junctions outside the general rules

  • इन्द्रियाय · ऋषिभ्यः — unjoined hiatus (as printed): indriyāya · ṛṣibhyaḥ: the final ā is not joined to the following ṛ (classical indriyāyarṣibhyaḥ); every witness prints the words apart.

Morphology

चन्दाग्सि
The word is chandāṃsi, accusative plural of chandas. The base writes it with च (after the preceding visarga, श्च) and with the Taittirīya ग्स् for the anusvāra; the Ānandāśrama text has श्छन्दाँसि. It is listed as printed.
पुरोवाच
The base and the Ānandāśrama text print पुरोवाच; the Tirupati e-text and both commentators read प्रोवाच, ‘declared’. The word is listed as printed and translated as the commentators read it.
उपनिषत्
Accusative in sense, without the ending; Bhāskara notes the dropped case ending (द्वितीयाया लुक्).

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

The first line is a relative clause (yaḥ … āviveśa) whose correlative is left to be understood — the commentators supply ‘om’. The second line has its own subject (indraḥ jyeṣṭhaḥ) and verb (provāca), then the salutations of the earlier anuvākas, then the vyāhṛtis with chandaḥ and om.

Ambiguities

who ‘yaḥ’ is
The relative pronoun has no named antecedent. The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa take it as the syllable om (यः प्रणवः); Bhāskara as the supreme self who becomes the whole of speech, the Vedas, histories and Purāṇas (विश्वरूपः वेदेतिहासपुराणवाड्मयरूपश्).
indriyāya
For the seers’ power to know (Sāyaṇa: ज्ञानसामर्थ्यार्थम्) or for the rite seen by Indra (Bhāskara: इन्द्रेण दृष्टं कर्म इन्द्रियं).

Metre

mixed · syllables counted as written: 20 + 40

A triṣṭubh line (20 syllables as printed, two short of 22 because of the joined forms) followed by a prose line of 40 syllables with the salutations and vyāhṛtis.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme What comes before the words

The anuvāka praises the sound that comes before every other sound in the recitation. Many disciplines have something like it — a tuning note, a breath, a moment of quiet — that is part of no piece and yet begins them all. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.
