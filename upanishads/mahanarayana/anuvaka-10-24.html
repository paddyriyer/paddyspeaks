Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 24 of 80

Sarvo vai rudraḥ: all this is Rudra

Three statements that everything — the person, being and light, the whole manifold world past and present — is Rudra, each closed with homage.

5 Faithful English translation

All, truly, [is] Rudra; to him, to Rudra, let there be homage. The person, truly, [is] Rudra, being, light; homage, homage. The universe, what has come to be, the world, manifold, born in many ways and being born — whatever [is]: all this, indeed, [is] Rudra; to him, to Rudra, let there be homage.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Rudra is all that is: homage to Rudra. Rudra is the Person, being and light: homage, homage. Whatever there is — the world, all that has come to be, manifold, born in many forms and still being born — all of it is Rudra. Homage to Rudra.

Key concepts पुरुष person जगत् world, ‘the moving’ जीव individual self

7 Explain it to a beginner

This passage makes one large claim three times. First: Rudra is all. Then: Rudra is the Person, being itself, and light. Finally, after listing everything there is — the world, every living thing, all that has been born and all that is being born now — it says that all of this is Rudra. After each statement it bows: homage to Rudra. The old commentators explain the claim this way: Rudra has entered every living body as its inner life, and the conscious Person that appears as Rudra is the light of being itself. The passage praises; it does not argue, and it does not say what reciting it brings. Its repeated ending, ‘homage to Rudra’, is what makes the list of everything into an act of reverence.

An analogy Like a refrain in a song: each verse names something new, and the refrain brings every verse back to the same point.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

That all is Rudra; that the person is Rudra, being and light; and that whatever there is, the world born and being born, is Rudra. Each statement ends in homage to him.

Traditional Interpretation

How is Rudra ‘all’?

Sāyaṇa and the anonymous commentator explain that Rudra, the husband of Pārvatī known from the Purāṇas, has entered every body as its living self: यो रुद्रः पार्वतीपतिः पुराणेषु प्रसिद्धः स एव सर्वो जीवरूपेण सर्वशरीरेषु प्रविष्टत्वात्. The person of the second line is the conscious principle as distinct from inert nature, which takes Rudra’s form for the sake of devotees: स एव भक्तानुग्रहाय रुद्रमूर्तिरूपेणावभासते.

Traditional Interpretation

Why can the world be called Rudra?

Because, Sāyaṇa says, the world cannot be described as real apart from him: तव्द्यतिरेकेण वास्तवस्य जगतो निरूपयितुमशक्यत्वात्. Being and light he explains through the Chāndogya: सदेव सोम्येदमग्र आसीत्.

Traditional Interpretation

How is this mantra used?

Sāyaṇa treats this anuvāka (his 16), kad rudrāya (10.25, his 17) and namo hiraṇyabāhave (10.22, his 18) as a group, and says their mantras are known in the manuals as the Tvaritarudra: अनेनानुवाकत्रयेणोक्तानां मन्त्राणां त्वरितरुद्राख्या मन्त्रकल्पेषु प्रसिद्धा.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • सर्वो वै रुद्रस्तस्मै sarvo vai rudrastasmai → सर्वः + वै + रुद्रः + तस्मै

    -aḥ before v; visarga before t

    -aḥ becomes -o before a voiced sound: sarvo vai; the visarga becomes s before t (P. 8.3.34 (विसर्जनीयस्य सः)): rudras tasmai.

  • रुद्रस्सन्महो rudrassanmaho → रुद्रः + सत् + महः

    visarga before s; t before a nasal

    The recitation text writes the visarga before s as a doubled s (rudras san); the t of sat becomes n before the nasal m (P. 8.4.45 (यरोऽनुनासिकेऽनुनासिको वा)): san mahaḥ.

  • ह्येष रुद्रस् hyeṣa rudras → हि + एषः + रुद्रः

    i before a vowel; eṣaḥ before a consonant

    hi + eṣaḥ → hy eṣaḥ (P. 6.1.77 (इको यणचि)); the visarga of eṣaḥ is dropped before a consonant (P. 6.1.132 (एतत्तदोः सुलोपोऽकोरनञ्समासे हलि)): eṣa rudraḥ.

Junctions outside the general rules

  • नमः · अस्तु — vedic-prakṛtibhāva: namo · astu: the o of namo is kept before a and the a is not elided (classical namo ’stu); Vedic recitation keeps e and o before a inside a pāda (P. 6.1.115 (प्रकृत्याऽन्तःपादमव्यपरे)). All witnesses print namo astu.
  • नमः · अस्तु — vedic-prakṛtibhāva: namo · astu again, in the refrain that closes the anuvāka; the same Vedic keeping of o before a.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this anuvāka.

Morphology

सत्
Sāyaṇa reads sat and mahaḥ together as ‘light that is being’, and supports sat with the Chāndogya’s सदेव सोम्येदमग्र आसीत् (ChU 6.2.1). Sat could also be read as the participle ‘being [Rudra], [he is] light’; the commentators take it as a noun.
यत्
The relative clause viśvaṃ bhūtaṃ … jāyamānaṃ ca yat (‘whatever is …’) has no main clause of its own; the next line, sarvo hy eṣa rudraḥ, completes it: whatever is, all that is Rudra.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

Three short identifications (sarvo vai rudraḥ; puruṣo vai rudraḥ, san mahaḥ), each followed by homage in the dative (tasmai rudrāya namo astu; namo namaḥ). The third line is a long relative clause with no verb, gathering the world in all its tenses; the refrain sarvo hy eṣa rudraḥ, with hi giving the ground, completes it and repeats the homage.

Ambiguities

sarvaḥ
‘All’ as the whole of things, or Rudra present in every body as its living self — Sāyaṇa’s and the anonymous commentator’s reading. The text says only that all is Rudra.
san mahaḥ
Read by the commentators as ‘light that is being’ (sat and mahas as nouns). It could also be heard as ‘being [Rudra], he is light’. The commentators do not discuss the alternative.

Metre

mixed · syllables counted as written: 14 + 13 + 20 + 15

Not a regular metre: the checker counts 14, 13, 20 and 15 syllables in the four lines. The first, second and fourth lines are short formulas of homage; the third, the list of all that is born, runs to 20 syllables.

Textual variations between witnesses

The witnesses differ here only in spelling (listed in section 14).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Wholeness

The passage refuses to leave anything out: the inert and the living, what has already come to be and what is coming to be now. Whatever one makes of its theology, the habit it models — taking in the whole before bowing — is a discipline of attention that works against seeing only the part one prefers. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin The passage says ‘all is Rudra’ three times, in different words. What does the repetition add?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Sāyaṇa says the world cannot be described as real apart from Rudra. How is that claim different from saying the world was made by him?

A contemplation exercise Look at one ordinary object near you and think of everything it took for it to come into being.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
