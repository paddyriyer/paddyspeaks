Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 57 of 80

Ye te sahasram: the thousand nooses of Death

Death’s thousand, ten thousand nooses, set to kill the mortal, are all to be removed by the power of the sacrifice.

5 Faithful English translation

Which nooses of yours, O Death, a thousand, ten thousand, [are there] to kill the mortal — all those we remove by the power of the sacrifice.

Death, your nooses for killing mortals number a thousand, ten thousand: by the power of the sacrifice we take them all away.

7 Explain it to a beginner

This prayer is addressed to Death and pictures him as a hunter with snares: a thousand, even ten thousand nooses, all set to catch and kill mortal beings. The speakers say that by the power of the sacrifice they take all those nooses away. The verb used does not mean "worship" here; with its prefix it means "to remove by sacrifice", and the old commentator points this out. He gives two ways of hearing "by the power of the sacrifice": either by means of the sacrifice, or by its strength. In this Upaniṣad the verse comes just before the offerings made "to Death". The edition reports what the words and the commentator say; it makes no claim about what the rite achieves.

An analogy Like a trapper’s snares laid across a field, found one by one and lifted before anyone steps into them.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

It speaks to Death of his nooses, a thousand and ten thousand, set to kill the mortal, and says that by the power of the sacrifice the speakers remove them all.

Traditional Interpretation

How does the commentator read it?

The anonymous commentator: हे मृत्यो मर्त्याय व्यत्ययेन — the dative ‘for the mortal’ stands by interchange for the accusative, so the nooses are there to kill every living being. ‘We remove them’ means we ward them off, तान्सर्वान्पाशानवयजामहे निवारयामः, because joined to its prefix the verb yaj changes its sense, अत्रोपसर्गयोगेण यजतेर्भिन्नार्थतोच्यते. The ‘māyā of the sacrifice’ is either the device of good works or their power: मायाशब्दः शक्तिपर्यायः.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • सहस्रमयुतं पाशा मृत्यो sahasramayutaṃ pāśā mṛtyo → सहस्रम् + अयुतम् + पाशाः + मृत्यो

    final m before a vowel; final m before a consonant; visarga after ā before a voiced consonant

    sahasram is joined to ayutam; ayutam becomes ayutaṃ before p; pāśāḥ before m drops its visarga.

  • सर्वानवयजामहे sarvānavayajāmahe → सर्वान् + अवयजामहे

    final n before a vowel

    sarvān is joined to avayajāmahe.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this anuvāka.

Morphology

हन्तवे
A Vedic infinitive in -tave from √han: ‘to kill’, glossed by the commentator with the classical infinitive hantum.
अवयजामहे
√yaj with ava means not ‘worship’ but ‘remove by sacrifice’, ‘sacrifice away’; the commentator says so: अत्रोपसर्गयोगेण यजतेर्भिन्नार्थतोच्यते — joined to the prefix, yaj is said to have a different sense.
तान्
The base prints a comma after tān (तान्,), marking a pause in recitation; it does not change the words.

Anvaya

हे मृत्यो ते ये सहस्रम् अयुतम् पाशाः मर्त्याय हन्तवे [सन्ति] तान् सर्वान् यज्ञस्य मायया अवयजामहे ।

he mṛtyo te ye sahasram ayutam pāśāḥ martyāya hantave santi tān sarvān yajñasya māyayā avayajāmahe |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

A relative clause (ye … pāśāḥ) with numbers in apposition and a dative of purpose with an infinitive (martyāya hantave), resumed by tān … sarvān as object of the main verb avayajāmahe, with the instrumental yajñasya māyayā.

Ambiguities

māyā
The commentator gives both senses: the sacrifice as a device or pretext, मिषेण च्छद्मना, and the sacrifice’s power, मायाशब्दः शक्तिपर्यायः. The Vedic sense of māyā as wondrous power is the second; the first is his alternative.

Metre

anuṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 18 + 15

As printed 18 + 15 syllables: ye te sahasram ayutaṃ (8) pāśā mṛtyo martyāya hantave (10); tān yajñasya māyayā (7) sarvān avayajāmahe (8). The second pāda has ten and the third seven; the edition reports the counts.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Mortality

The image multiplies death’s snares beyond counting and answers them not with fear but with an act done deliberately and together. Facing an overwhelming threat by doing what can be done is an old human response. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.
