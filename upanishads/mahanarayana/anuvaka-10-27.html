Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 27 of 80

Kṛṇuṣva pājaḥ: five verses against obstructing forces

A reference, by their opening words, to five verses to Agni that the reciter supplies from the Saṃhitā; the commentators take them as prayers to remove what obstructs.

7 Explain it to a beginner

This anuvāka has only four words: ‘Spread out your strength … thus, five.’ It is a pointer, not a text. Reciters knew a large body of verses by heart, so a book could name a passage by its first words and the number of verses, and the reciter would supply the rest. The commentators say where the five verses are found: in the first book of the Taittirīya Saṃhitā, at the end of its second chapter. They are verses to Agni, the god of fire, asking him to drive away hostile forces with his flames. One commentator explains that in this part of the Upaniṣad, which concerns release, the hostile forces are also inner ones, such as desire and anger. The anuvāka points to the verses; it says nothing about what reciting them brings.

An analogy Like a hymn book that prints only ‘Hymn 27, verses 1–5’: the singers know the words; the page only says which.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

It names the five verses beginning kṛṇuṣva pājaḥ, to be recited in full.

Traditional Interpretation

Where are the five verses, and how are they used?

The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa place them in the first book of the Saṃhitā, at the close of its second chapter: संहितायाः प्रथमकाण्डे द्वितीयप्रपाठकस्यान्तिमानुवाके समाम्नाताः (Sāyaṇa), and say they are to be recited quietly, ऋचः पञ्च जपितव्या इति शेषः. The anonymous commentator gives all five with an explanation, and names the seer as Vāmadeva and the deity as Agni: वामदेवदृष्टा अग्निदेवताकाः.

Traditional Interpretation

Why are they placed here?

Having pointed to rites as aids to purity of mind in this section on release, the text now gives mantras that remove obstacles, says the anonymous commentator: चित्तशुद्धिहेतूनां कर्मणां संग्रहेणोपयोगमस्मिन्मोक्षप्रकरणे सूचयित्वा. The fruit of praying to Agni, he says, is that enemies outside and within cease: अस्मिन्मन्त्रे बाह्माभ्यन्तरशत्रुनिवृत्तिरग्निप्रार्थनायाः फलम्. Bhāskara: the five are demon-slaying verses used in the worship of the god in the form of fire, इत्यादयः पञ्च राक्षोघ्न्यो ऽग्नयात्मनो देवस्य उपासनादौ विनियोज्याः, and here they are to be explained as setting out Brahman, ताश्चात्र प्रकरणानुरोधाद् ब्रह्मप्रतिपादकतया व्याख्येयाः.

Where they disagree

No Vedānta-school commentary on this anuvāka is in the edition’s sources (Raṅgarāmānuja’s commentary on the Mahānārāyaṇa is not available here). The commentators agree on what the five verses are; they differ on how to read them here — against inner enemies (the anonymous commentator), or as teaching Brahman (Bhāskara).

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • पाज इति pāja iti → पाजः + इति

    -aḥ before a vowel other than a

    Before a vowel other than a, -aḥ loses its visarga (it becomes y, P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि), and the y is dropped, P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य)), leaving the vowels apart: pājaḥ + iti → pāja iti.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this anuvāka.

Morphology

इति
This anuvāka is a pratīka: it gives only the opening words of a passage, kṛṇuṣva pājaḥ, and the number of verses, pañca, and the reciter supplies the rest from the Saṃhitā. The commentators identify the five verses (see below).

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

Not a sentence but a citation: the opening words of a verse, the particle iti marking them as quoted, and the number of verses meant.

Ambiguities

What the five verses ask
Addressed to Agni to strike at demons with his flames. The anonymous commentator reads the enemies as also inner ones — desire, anger and the like (कामक्रोधादिशत्रुसंहारार्थं); Bhāskara says that here, by the context, they are to be explained as setting out Brahman.

Metre

prose · syllables counted as written: 9

The anuvāka itself is a citation of 9 syllables. The verses it cites are metrical: the anonymous commentator calls them anuṣṭubh (पञ्चानुष्टुप्छन्दस्का), though the first line he quotes, kṛṇuṣva pājaḥ prasitiṃ na pṛthvīṃ yāhi rājevāmavāṁ ibhena, has two eleven-syllable pādas.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Memory and text

A pratīka trusts the reader to carry the rest of the passage in memory. It is a reminder that many texts were made to be completed by people who already knew them, and that reading such a text well can mean knowing what it leaves out. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.
