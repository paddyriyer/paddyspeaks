Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 71 of 80

The person the size of a thumb

After the offerings to the breaths, the eater turns from the food to the one who abides within: the thumb-sized person, lord of the whole world, who is satisfied by the meal.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

The Person the size of a thumb, who rests in the thumb — lord of the whole world, its master, who partakes of all — is satisfied.

Key concepts अङ्गुष्ठमात्र aṅguṣṭhamātra, thumb-sized पुरुष person ईश्वर the Lord अन्न food

7 Explain it to a beginner

This short verse belongs to the prayers said at a meal. After the first mouthfuls have been offered to the breaths within the body, the eater turns to the one who is said to dwell within: a ‘person the size of a thumb’. The old commentators explain the size: the space in the heart is about as big as a thumb, so the one who lives there is described by that measure. The verse then says this same inner person is the lord and master of the whole world and the one who ‘eats’ everything, and that he is satisfied. The anonymous commentator reads it as a wish that the Lord be pleased by the meal. Bhāskara adds that at this point water is poured over the right thumb. The edition reports the rite as the commentators describe it and makes no claim about its effects.

An analogy Like a small window through which the whole sky is seen: the small measure belongs to the opening, not to what is seen through it.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse say?

A person of a thumb’s measure, resting on the thumb, is called the lord of the whole world, its master and the eater of all, and is said to be pleased.

Traditional Interpretation

When is it said?

The anonymous commentator places it after the meal’s first offerings, once the restlessness hunger causes has settled: क्षुदादिजनितचित्तविक्षेपशान्तेरूर्ध्वं भोक्तुर्जीवस्य परमेश्वरस्वरूपानुसंधानहेतुं मन्त्रं दर्शयति — a mantra by which the one who eats calls to mind the nature of the supreme Lord. Sāyaṇa says the same in nearly the same words (भोक्तुर्जीवस्य परमेश्वररूपत्वानुसंधाने हेतुभूतं मन्त्रं दर्शयति). Bhāskara gives the act that goes with it: दक्षिणाङ्गुष्ठे पाणिजलं स्रावपति — one lets water from the hand flow over the right thumb.

Comparative Vedānta

Who is the thumb-sized person?

The anonymous commentator measures it by the heart: हृदयमध्यगत आकाशः स्वाङ्गुष्ठपरिमितः — the space in the middle of the heart is the size of one’s thumb; the intellect there has that size, and the individual self limited by it is called thumb-sized (जीवरूपः पुरुषोऽप्यङ्गुष्ठमात्रः). Without that limiting adjunct, in its true form, it is the controller of the world (स चोपाधिसंबन्धमन्तरेण स्वकीयेन वास्तवरूपेण सर्वस्य जगत ईशो नियन्ता). Bhāskara starts from the Lord: unlimited, he is called thumb-sized because he is found in the heart, which has that measure (ईश्वरो ऽनवच्छिन्नोप्य् अङ्गुष्ठप्रमाणहृदयदेशोपलम्भात्).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Indicative or wish?

The base has the indicative prīṇāti, ‘is pleased’. The Tirupati e-text and the text Sāyaṇa comments on read the imperative prīṇātu, and Sāyaṇa glosses it प्रीणातु, अनेन भोजनेन प्रीतो भवतु. The anonymous commentator, commenting on prīṇāti, also gives the sense of a wish. Bhāskara explains prīṇāti first and then glosses प्रीणातु तृप्यतु, ‘may he be pleased, satisfied’.

Where they disagree

No Vedānta-school commentary on this text is in the edition’s sources: Raṅgarāmānuja’s is not available, and the commentaries present (the anonymous bhāṣya, Sāyaṇa, Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara) are ritual and Vedic explanations, not school readings. Between the commentators present, the anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa read the thumb-sized person as the individual self whose true nature is the Lord; Bhāskara reads it as the Lord found in the heart.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • पुरुषोऽङ्गुष्ठं puruṣo'ṅguṣṭhaṃ → पुरुषः + अङ्गुष्ठम्

    visarga after a before a

    puruṣaḥ + aṅguṣṭham → puruṣo ’ṅguṣṭham: -aḥ becomes -o and the following a is elided, marked by the avagraha.

  • ईशस्सर्वस्य īśassarvasya → ईशः + सर्वस्य

    visarga before s

    The Taittirīya recitation writes a visarga before a sibilant as the sibilant doubled: īśaḥ sarvasya → īśas sarvasya.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • विश्वभुक् viśvabhuk

    upapada-tatpuruṣa · विश्व + भुज्

    Vigraha विश्वं भुङ्क्ते इति

    the one who eats, enjoys, the whole

Morphology

अङ्गुष्ठमात्रः
aṅguṣṭha with -mātra, ‘having the measure of’: a word of size, not a compound of two independent words.
प्रीणाति
Present indicative of √prī (9th class): ‘is pleased’. The Tirupati e-text and the text printed with Sāyaṇa read the imperative प्रीणातु, ‘may he be pleased’ (see flags).
विश्वभुक्
Nominative of the root-noun -bhuj, ‘eating, enjoying’: the final j becomes k in the pausal form.

Anvaya

अङ्गुष्ठमात्रः अङ्गुष्ठम् च समाश्रितः पुरुषः सर्वस्य जगतः ईशः प्रभुः विश्वभुक् प्रीणाति

aṅguṣṭhamātraḥ aṅguṣṭham ca samāśritaḥ puruṣaḥ sarvasya jagataḥ īśaḥ prabhuḥ viśvabhuk prīṇāti

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

One sentence. The subject puruṣaḥ carries two qualifiers joined by ca (aṅguṣṭhamātraḥ, aṅguṣṭhaṃ samāśritaḥ); three nouns stand in apposition to it (īśaḥ … prabhuḥ … viśvabhuk), the first two governing the genitive sarvasya jagataḥ; the verb prīṇāti comes last but one.

Ambiguities

prīṇāti or prīṇātu
The base reads the indicative (‘is pleased’); the Tirupati e-text and the text printed with Sāyaṇa read the imperative (‘may he be pleased’). The anonymous commentator, on the base, still glosses a wish (प्रीतो भवतु), and Bhāskara glosses both forms (प्रीणाति विश्वभुक्, प्रीणातु तृप्यतु).
who is the thumb-sized person
The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa: the individual self, limited by the intellect in the thumb-sized space of the heart, which in its own nature is the lord of the world. Bhāskara: the Lord himself, called thumb-sized because he is perceived in the heart, which has that measure.
aṅguṣṭhaṃ ca samāśritaḥ
For the anonymous commentator the person rests on the thumb and, by ca, on the head as well: it pervades the body from foot to head. Bhāskara ties the words to the rite: with this verse water from the hand is let flow over the right thumb.

Metre

anuṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 15 + 16

Bhāskara names it an anuṣṭubh (अङ्गुष्ठेत्यनुष्टुभा). As printed the two half-verses count 15 and 16 syllables: puruṣo ’ṅguṣṭham elides one a; read with the a, the first half also has 16.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Gratitude

The verse comes at the point of a meal when hunger has eased, and turns attention from the food to the one who is fed. Pausing after the first relief of hunger to recognise what sustains one is a habit many traditions share. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Why might a prayer at a meal speak of the one within rather than of the food?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The anonymous commentator says the thumb-size belongs to the space of the heart and not to the person in it. What does that distinction let him say about the person?

A contemplation exercise At your next meal, pause once after the first few mouthfuls before going on.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
