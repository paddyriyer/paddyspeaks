Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 58 of 80

Mṛtyave svāhā: two oblations to Death

Two oblations of ghee are offered to Death, each with the words ‘to Death, svāhā’.

7 Explain it to a beginner

This is the shortest anuvāka in this part of the Upaniṣad: two offerings, each made with the words "to Death, svāhā". "Svāhā" is the call with which an offering is poured into the sacred fire. The old commentator explains that the formula is said twice because two offerings are made, and that both are offered to Death, that by which living beings die. He also explains where these formulas belong: from here on come offering formulas for removing wrongdoing, and the person who wishes to be rid of wrongdoing performs the offering according to the rules of his own household manual and offers the main oblations with these words. The edition reports this as the commentator’s account of the rite; it makes no claim about what the offering achieves.

An analogy Like two stones laid on a cairn by a traveller: the same gesture twice, each one counted.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

Two oblations, each with the words ‘to Death, svāhā’.

Traditional Interpretation

What is the rite, according to the commentator?

The anonymous commentator says that from here offering formulas for removing every sin are taught: अथेदानीं पापमात्रनिवर्हणार्था होममन्त्रा उच्यन्ते. One who wishes to be rid of sin performs the preliminary rites according to his own domestic manual, तेन स्वगृह्योक्तविधिना, and offers the main oblations with these formulas. The formula is repeated to mark two oblations, आहुतिद्वयद्योतनार्था मन्त्रावृत्तिः, and both go to Death: इदमाहुतिद्वयं मृत्युदेवत्यम्.

Where they disagree

No Vedānta-school commentary on this anuvāka is in the edition’s sources (Raṅgarāmānuja’s commentary on the Mahānārāyaṇa is not among them). The anonymous bhāṣya explains the words and the rite; it does not argue an Advaita, Viśiṣṭādvaita or Dvaita position here, so the edition gives none.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Mortality

An offering made to Death itself, plainly and twice, treats death neither as an enemy to be fought nor as something unmentionable, but as a power to be acknowledged by name. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.
