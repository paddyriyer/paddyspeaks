Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 56 of 80

Tryambakam yajāmahe: released from death like the ripe fruit from its stalk

The three-eyed one, fragrant, who increases prosperity, is worshipped; the speaker asks to be released from death as a ripe cucumber is from its stalk, and not from immortality.

5 Faithful English translation

We worship the three-eyed one, the fragrant, the increaser of prosperity. Like a cucumber from its stalk, may I be released from death, not from immortality.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

We worship the three-eyed lord, fragrant, who makes all things thrive. As a ripe cucumber comes away from its stem, may I be freed from death, but not from the deathless.

Key concepts मृत्यु death अमृत immortality, the deathless मोक्ष liberation मन्त्र mantra

7 Explain it to a beginner

This is one of the best known verses of the Veda. It worships the "three-eyed" god, the commentator says the lord who is Pārvatī’s husband, calling him fragrant and the one who makes things grow and thrive. Then it asks for one thing with a striking image: as a ripe cucumber comes away from the vine without effort, may I be freed from death — but not from the deathless. The commentator explains "the deathless" as release itself: the speaker asks to be loosed from death and never separated from immortality. In this Upaniṣad the verse stands among the short prayers and offering formulas near its end. The edition reports what the verse and the commentator say; it makes no claim about what reciting it does for health or life.

An analogy Like a ripe fruit that falls from the vine at a touch: no tearing, only a letting go that comes from ripeness.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

We worship the three-eyed one, fragrant, increaser of prosperity; like a cucumber from its stalk, may I be released from death, not from immortality.

Traditional Interpretation

How does the commentator read it?

The anonymous commentator addresses the god as the lord of Pārvatī, हे भगवन्पार्वतीपते त्वां यजामहे, and glosses tryambaka as three-eyed, त्र्यम्बकं त्रिनेत्रम्. ‘Fragrant’ is the fragrance of his divine body, smelt from afar like that of the ketakī flower: यथा केतक्यादिसौगन्ध्यं दूरादाघ्रायते तथा तदीयदिव्यदेहसौरभ्यं सोऽयं सुगन्धिः. The release from death is asked for after worshipping him through rites such as the sandhyā worship, and the immortality not to be lost is release itself, अमृतान्मोक्षान्मा मुक्षोमहि न वियुज्यामहे.

  • उर्वारुकमिव urvārukamiva → उर्वारुकम् + इव

  • बन्धनान्मृत्योर्मुक्षीय bandhanānmṛtyormukṣīya → बन्धनात् + मृत्योः + मुक्षीय

    bahuvrīhi · त्रि + अम्बक

    having three eyes

  • पुष्टिवर्धनम् puṣṭivardhanam

    upapada-tatpuruṣa · पुष्टि + वर्धन

    increaser of prosperity

Morphology

सुगन्धिम्
After su-, gandha becomes gandhi: P. 5.4.135 (गन्धस्येदुत्पूतिसुसुरभिभ्यः). Hence sugandhi, ‘well-scented’.
मुक्षीय
Aorist optative (precative) middle, first singular, of √muc: ‘may I be freed’. The commentator glosses it with a plural, मुक्षीमहि मुक्ता भवेम, and explains the singular as an interchange of number.
मा
Here mā negates the second ablative: ‘from death, not from immortality’. The commentator: अमृतान्मोक्षान्मा मुक्षोमहि न वियुज्यामहे — let us not be parted from immortality, that is, release.

Anvaya

सुगन्धिम् पुष्टिवर्धनम् त्र्यम्बकम् यजामहे । उर्वारुकम् बन्धनात् इव मृत्योः मुक्षीय अमृतात् मा [मुक्षीय] ।

sugandhim puṣṭivardhanam tryambakam yajāmahe | urvārukam bandhanāt iva mṛtyoḥ mukṣīya amṛtāt mā mukṣīya |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

A first person plural statement of worship (yajāmahe) with an object and two epithets; then a first person singular wish (mukṣīya) with two ablatives, the first affirmed (mṛtyoḥ), the second negated (mā amṛtāt), and a simile (urvārukam iva bandhanāt) placed before them.

Ambiguities

mā amṛtāt
‘Not from immortality’: the release asked for is from death only. The commentator glosses amṛta as release, अमृतान्मोक्षान्मा मुक्षोमहि न वियुज्यामहे.
the simile
The commentator takes the fruit as ripe and falling easily, यथोर्वरुकं कर्कट्यादेः फलं पक्कं सद्बन्धनाट्वृन्तादनायासेन मुच्यते (his text writes urvaruka): release without struggle, as of ripeness. The verse itself does not say ‘ripe’.

Metre

anuṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 15 + 17

As printed 15 + 17 syllables: tryambakaṃ yajāmahe (7) sugandhiṃ puṣṭivardhanam (8); urvārukam iva bandhanān (9) mṛtyor mukṣīya māmṛtāt (8). The first pāda has seven (tryambakam read with tri- as two syllables would give eight) and the third nine; the edition reports the counts and does not emend.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Mortality

The verse does not ask never to die; it asks that the parting from death be like ripe fruit leaving the vine — natural, unforced — and that nothing deathless be lost in it. It holds acceptance and hope in one image. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin What in your own life would you like to let go of as easily as ripe fruit leaves the vine?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The verse asks for release from death but ‘not from immortality’. What does that second half add to the first?

A contemplation exercise Notice one attachment that has become ‘ripe’ — no longer needed — and let it go without forcing.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
