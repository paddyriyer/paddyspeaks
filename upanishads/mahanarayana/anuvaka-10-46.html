Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 46 of 80

Paraṃ mṛtyo: go by your own road

Death is asked to take its own road, not the path of the gods, and is told plainly not to harm the speakers’ children and people.

5 Faithful English translation

O Death, go away along the further path, which is your own, other than the path of the gods. To you who have eyes, who hear, I speak: do not harm our offspring, nor our men.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Death, go away by your own road, the far road that is not the path of the gods. You see and you hear, so I say this to you: do not harm our children, and do not harm our people.

Key concepts मृत्यु death देवता deity लोक world, realm

7 Explain it to a beginner

Here the speaker talks to Death directly. Go away, it says, by your own road, the far one that is not the path of the gods. The old commentator explains that in Vedic thought the dead may travel by two paths, the path of the gods and the path of the fathers; Death is asked to keep off both and go its own way. Then the speaker adds: you have eyes and you hear, so listen to me. The commentator imagines Death objecting that it cannot see or hear a prayer, and the verse answering that it can. The request that follows is simple and human: do not harm our children, and do not harm our people. The same verse opens a funeral hymn of the Ṛgveda.

An analogy Like asking a stranger walking towards your house to keep to the road and pass by.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse ask?

That Death go by its own road, other than the path of the gods, and harm neither the speakers’ offspring nor their people.

Traditional Interpretation

Why say that Death has eyes and ears?

The anonymous commentator imagines Death saying it is blind and deaf, and so cannot see or hear the one who prays; the verse answers that Death does see and hear: चक्षुष्मते शृण्वते श्रोत्रवते ते तुभ्यं.

Traditional Interpretation

Which roads are meant?

The anonymous commentator: the path of the gods is the path of light (arcirādi-mārga), and with it the path of the fathers; Death’s own road is other than both, and the speaker says, in effect, I shall go by one of these two, I do not look to you (अहं त्वेताभ्यामन्यतरेण वा गमिष्यामि न त्वामीक्षे).

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • परेहि parehi → परा + इहि

    ā + i → e

    parā + ihi → parehi (P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः)).

  • यस्ते yaste → यः + ते

    visarga before t

    yaḥ + te → yas te (P. 8.3.34 (विसर्जनीयस्य सः)).

  • स्व इतरो देवयानात् sva itaro devayānāt → स्वः + इतरः + देवयानात्

    -aḥ before a vowel other than a; -aḥ before a voiced consonant

    svaḥ + itaraḥ → sva itaraḥ, the visarga dropped (P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य)); itaraḥ + devayānāt → itaro devayānāt (P. 6.1.114 (हशि च)).

  • रीरिषो मोत rīriṣo mota → रीरिषः + मा + उत

    -aḥ before m; ā + u → o

    rīriṣaḥ + mā → rīriṣo mā (P. 6.1.114 (हशि च)); mā + uta → mota (P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः)).

Junctions outside the general rules

  • मृत्यो · अनु — unjoined hiatus (as printed): mṛtyo · anu: the o of the vocative kept before a without elision (classical mṛtyo ’nu); a Vedic hiatus (prakṛtibhāva, P. 6.1.115 (प्रकृत्यान्तःपादमव्यपरे)), printed so in every witness.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • देवयानात् devayānāt

    tatpuruṣa · देव + यान

    Vigraha देवानां यानम्

    the path by which the gods go

Morphology

स्वः
The a-stem sva ‘own’, not svar ‘heaven’; so the visarga drops before itaraḥ (sva itaraḥ) instead of becoming r. The Ānandāśrama pariśiṣṭa text omits the word (corrections.json, a witness error); the commentator reads it (स्वः स्वभूतः).
रीरिषः
A reduplicated aorist of √riṣ, used with mā as a prohibition (injunctive).

Anvaya

मृत्यो यः ते स्वः देवयानात् इतरः [पन्थाः] [तम्] परम् पन्थाम् अनु परेहि । चक्षुष्मते शृण्वते ते ब्रवीमि । नः प्रजाम् मा रीरिषः उत वीरान् मा [रीरिषः] ।

mṛtyo yaḥ te svaḥ devayānāt itaraḥ panthāḥ tam param panthām anu parehi | cakṣuṣmate śṛṇvate te bravīmi | naḥ prajām mā rīriṣaḥ uta vīrān mā rīriṣaḥ |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

A command (anu parehi) with its object panthām, defined by a relative clause (yas te sva itaro devayānāt); then a statement (bravīmi) addressed to Death, described by two datives; then two prohibitions with mā and the aorist rīriṣaḥ, the second sharing the verb.

Ambiguities

which path
The verse contrasts Death’s road only with the path of the gods. The commentator adds the path of the fathers: Death is to keep away from both, the two roads the dead may take.
vīrān
Literally ‘heroes, men’; the commentator explains them as dependants (bhṛtyān).

Metre

triṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 21 + 22

One triṣṭubh verse printed in two lines; the checker counts 21 and 22 syllables (the first line is short by one, mṛtyo anu being kept in hiatus but counted as written).

Textual variations between witnesses

  • Witness error Ānandāśrama pariśiṣṭa text (1927): पन्थां यस्ते इतरो देवयानात्। — The Ānandāśrama pariśiṣṭa text alone reads — for स्व; the base and the Tirupati e-text agree.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Speaking plainly

The verse does not plead or bargain; it tells a feared power what not to do, in a few words. Speaking plainly to what we fear, rather than avoiding it, is a kind of courage. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.
