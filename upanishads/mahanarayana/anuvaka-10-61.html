Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 61 of 80

Kāmo ’kārṣīt: “desire did it”

An offering formula in which the speaker attributes past wrongs to desire, not to himself, and offers ghee to desire as a deity; its pair, 10.62, does the same with anger.

5 Faithful English translation

Desire has done [it]; homage, homage. Desire has done [it]; desire does [it], I do not do [it]; desire is the doer, I am not the doer; desire is the one who causes [it] to be done, I am not the one who causes [it] to be done. This [offering] is for you, O Desire, for desire. Svāhā.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Desire did it — homage, homage. Desire did it: desire acts, not I; desire is the doer, not I; desire drives the deed, not I. This offering is yours, Desire: to Desire, svāhā.

Key concepts कर्मन् action, ritual work मन्त्र mantra त्याग giving up, relinquishment

7 Explain it to a beginner

This is a short offering formula. The speaker says that desire did the wrong — not he himself. Desire acted, desire was the doer, desire made him do it; then he offers a spoonful of ghee into the fire to Desire, honoured as a deity, with the word svāhā. The next anuvāka repeats the same words for anger. The old commentator does not read this as an excuse. He says it is spoken by someone in remorse, who feels pushed by desire and anger into acts he did not want, and asks the deity to take the wrong away. The edition reports that reading and makes no claim about what the rite achieves.

An analogy Like someone who, looking back on an outburst, says “that was my temper talking”, and wants to be rid of the temper rather than to escape the blame.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the formula say?

It salutes, then three times sets desire against ‘I’: desire does, is the doer, has [it] done; I do not, am not, have not. It ends by offering ‘this’ to Desire with svāhā.

Traditional Interpretation

Whom does the speaker salute, and what is offered?

The anonymous commentator takes the salutation as addressed to all the gods, हे त्रयस्त्रिंशत्कोटिदेवता युष्मभ्यं नमो नमः, and the deed as the sins already named: पूर्वोक्तानि पापानि कामो मकरध्वजोऽकार्षीच्चकार. The repetition removes the speaker’s own agency, स्वकर्तृ[त्व]निवृत्तिः in his words as printed, and the reason given is that desire masters the world: काम एव कर्ता नाहं कर्ता. The offering is ghee to remove that sin: तत्पापनिवृत्त्यर्थं ते तुभ्यमेष आज्यभागः.

Traditional Interpretation

Is this an excuse?

The commentator answers this under the next formula (10.62): guilt belongs to the one who compels, not to an unwilling doer, and the formulas are spoken by someone weeping in remorse. See 10.62.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • कामोऽकार्षीत् kāmo'kārṣīt → कामः + अकार्षीत्

    -aḥ before a

    kāmaḥ + akārṣīt → kāmo ’kārṣīt: the visarga becomes o and the initial a is elided (avagraha).

  • कामोऽकार्षीन्नमो kāmo'kārṣīnnamo → कामः + अकार्षीत् + नमः

    final t before a nasal

    akārṣīt + namaḥ → akārṣīn namaḥ: t becomes n before n.

  • नाहं nāhaṃ → न + अहम्

    a + a → ā

    na + aham → nāham.

  • कारयिता एष kārayitā eṣa → कारयिता + एषः

    unjoined vowels; eṣaḥ before a consonant

    kārayitā eṣaḥ is printed without joining the vowels (see sandhi_exceptions); eṣaḥ + te → eṣa te: eṣaḥ drops its visarga before a consonant.

Junctions outside the general rules

  • कारयिता · एषः — hiatus at a phrase boundary: kārayitā eṣa is printed with the vowels apart, at the break between the confession and the offering formula.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this anuvāka.

Morphology

अकार्षीत्
s-aorist 3rd singular of √kṛ: ‘has done’ — the deed is past and complete.
कर्ता
Agent noun in -tṛ: ‘the doer’, set against kārayitā, the causative agent noun ‘the one who has [it] done’.
कारयिता
Agent noun from the causative kāraya-: ‘the one who causes [another] to act’.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

A short salutation, then three parallel pairs of clauses, each setting desire against ‘I’ with the negation na: desire does / I do not; desire is the doer / I am not; desire has it done / I do not. The last sentence is an offering formula: eṣa te … svāhā, ‘this is for you’, with the vocative and the dative of the deity.

Ambiguities

whose deed
The text names no deed; the anonymous commentator supplies ‘the sins mentioned before’: पूर्वोक्तानि पापानि.
does the formula deny responsibility?
Read literally, the speaker says he is not the doer. The commentator discusses this directly (see commentary): the guilt lies with the one who compels, not with an unwilling doer, and the formula is spoken in remorse.

Metre

prose · syllables counted as written: 8 + 44

A prose formula (yajus). The salutation counts 8 syllables, the rest 44.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Self-mastery

The formula separates the self from an impulse it does not endorse: “desire did it, not I.” Read with the commentator, it is not an alibi but a way of naming the impulse so that it can be given up. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin When desire pushes you into something, do you experience it as “me” or as something acting on you?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The commentator says the guilt lies with the one who compels, not the unwilling doer, yet the speaker still makes an offering. What does that combination say about responsibility?

A contemplation exercise Next time desire rises, name it silently (“this is desire”) before you act.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
