Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 80 of 80

The sacrifice of the one who knows: his life is the rite, his death its closing bath

For the one who knows thus, every part of his person and every act of his life is a part of the sacrifice — his self the sacrificer, faith the wife, his body the fuel; his eating the oblation, his death the closing bath; dying in the northern or the southern course he reaches the sun or the moon, and through them the greatness of brahman.

5 Faithful English translation

Of him who knows thus, of the sacrifice, the self [is] the sacrificer, faith the wife, the body the firewood, the chest the altar, the hairs the sacrificial grass, the tuft of hair the grass-broom, the heart the sacrificial post, desire the clarified butter, anger the victim, austerity the fire, restraint the quieter, the fee [is …], speech the Hotṛ, breath the Udgātṛ, the eye the Adhvaryu, the mind the Brahman priest, the ear the Agnīdh. As long as he lives, that [is] the consecration. What he eats, that [is] the oblation; what he drinks, that [is] his soma drinking; what he delights in, that [is] the upasads; that he moves about, sits down and stands up, that [is] the pravargya; what [is] the mouth, that [is] the offering fire; what [is] the utterance, the offering; what [is] his discerning knowledge, that he offers. What he eats evening and morning, that [is] the fuel-stick; morning, midday and evening — those [are] the soma pressings; the two that are day and night, those [are] the new- and full-moon rites; the half-months and months, those [are] the four-monthly rites; the seasons, those [are] the animal sacrifices; the saṃvatsara and parivatsara years, those [are] the rites of many days. This indeed [is] the sattra with all one’s goods as fee; what [is] death, that [is] the closing bath. This indeed [is] the agnihotra and sattra that ends with old age and death. Who, knowing thus, dies in the northern course, having gone to the greatness of the gods, goes to union with the sun; but who dies in the southern [course], having gone to the greatness of the ancestors, attains union with the moon, sharing its world. These two greatnesses of the sun and the moon a brāhmaṇa who knows wins; from that he attains the greatness of brahman; from that, the greatness of brahman — thus the secret teaching.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

For the one who knows this, his whole life is a sacrifice. His self is the sacrificer and his faith the sacrificer’s wife; his body is the firewood, his chest the altar, his hair the sacred grass; his heart is the post, his desire the butter, his anger the victim, his austerity the fire. His speech, breath, eye, mind and ear are the four priests and their helper. As long as he lives, he is under consecration. His eating is the oblation and his drinking the soma; his resting, walking, sitting and rising are the rites of the sacrifice; his mouth is the offering fire, his words are the offering, and his knowing is what he pours into the fire. His meals are the fuel; morning, noon and evening are the three pressings of soma; days and nights, fortnights, months, seasons and years are the great rites of the calendar. His life is a long sacrificial session in which he gives everything he has, and his death is the closing bath. If such a knower dies while the sun goes north, he reaches the greatness of the gods and union with the sun; if while it goes south, the greatness of the ancestors and the world of the moon. A knower who wins these two goes on to the greatness of brahman. This is the secret teaching.

7 Explain it to a beginner

The last section of the Upaniṣad turns a whole human life into a Vedic sacrifice. In the great soma rite there is a sacrificer, his wife, firewood, an altar, sacred grass, a post, butter, an animal, fire and four kinds of priest. Here each is matched to something in the person who knows: the self is the sacrificer, faith is the wife, the body is the firewood, anger is the animal, the senses are the priests. Ordinary acts become the rite too — eating is the offering, drinking is the soma, walking and sitting are ceremonies, and the times of day and the seasons of the year are the calendar of rites. Death, in this picture, is the bath that closes the sacrifice. The commentators explain why this is said: the one who knows has no need to keep performing rites, because his whole life already is one. The passage ends with where such a person goes at death, and with the greatness of brahman.

An analogy Like an actor who no longer needs the stage, because every gesture of his daily life has become the play.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

For the one who knows, it maps his person onto the persons and materials of the sacrifice, his acts onto its rites, and the divisions of time onto the great seasonal rites; it calls his life a sattra in which all is given, and his death its closing bath. Dying in the northern course of the sun he reaches the greatness of the gods and union with the sun; in the southern, the greatness of the ancestors and the moon. The one who knows wins both and attains the greatness of brahman.

Traditional Interpretation

Why is the knower’s life called a sacrifice?

Sāyaṇa and the anonymous commentator: renunciation has been taught as the means to knowledge; to answer the doubt whether one who has seen the truth should go on performing rites, all his worldly doings are declared to be sacrifice — तत्त्वज्ञानिव्यवहाराणां लौकिकानां सर्वेषां यागरूपत्वमुच्यते — and no question of a qualification for sacrifice arises for what already is a sacrifice: न हि यागस्य यागाधिकारशङ्काऽस्ति. Bhāskara gives the opposite purpose: knowledge itself is described as sacrifice to show that the obligatory rites are not given up — ज्ञानमेव यागात्मना वर्ण्यते, नित्यकर्मापरित्यागसूचनार्थं.

Traditional Interpretation

How are the acts of life matched to the rite?

Sāyaṇa: the time the knower holds out without food is his consecration — यावन्तं कालं भोजनमकृत्वा ध्रियते विदुषा धार्यते सा धृतिर्दीक्षाख्यसंस्काररूपा. Bhāskara reads यावद्ध्रियते as ‘as long as he lives’: यावद् ध्रियते जीवति मा दीक्षा देहशोधनत्वात्. Sāyaṇa takes the whole span of years as the knower’s life: यदायुस्तत्सर्वस्वदक्षिणोपेतं सत्रमित्यर्थः. Bhāskara adds that the image shows life is not to be given up at will (ज्ञापकं चैतन् न स्वेच्छया त्यक्तव्याः प्राणा इति), since the agnihotra lasts until old age and death (जरया वयोहान्या म्रियते सन्तिष्ठते इति जरामर्यं).

Traditional Interpretation

Is this the same as the Chāndogya’s teaching that a person is a sacrifice?

The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa take up the Brahmasūtra’s discussion (third adhyāya, third pāda) of whether this ‘person-knowledge’ is one with the Tāṇḍins’ पुरुषो वाव यज्ञः: पुंविद्यैका विभिन्ना वा तैत्तिरीयकताण्डिनोः. They conclude it is not, because the forms differ and because the Taittirīya passage is not a meditation but a praise of the knowledge of brahman: न तैत्तिरीयाणामुपासनमिदं किं तर्हि ब्रह्मविद्याप्रशंसा.

Traditional Interpretation

What does the end mean?

The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa: the last part gives the step-by-step release of one who meditates on the sacrifice of the knower — क्रममुक्तिलक्षणं फलमाह — he who dies in the southern course reaches the moon’s greatness as described (य उपासको दक्षिणायने म्रियते). Bhāskara: the repetition of the last clause marks the end of the Upaniṣad — एवं समाप्तिसूचनार्था वाक्यावृत्तिः.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What does the text read at two places?

The base and the Tirupati e-text read व्याहृतिरहुतिः, the Ānandāśrama text व्याहृतिराहुतिः ‘the utterance is the offering’, which the translation follows. The base ends with (इत्युपनिषत्) in brackets, as printed; the Ānandāśrama text has महिमानम् alone, and the Tirupati e-text a shorter close. The edition keeps the base.

The base and the Tirupati e-text print अहुतिः with a short a; the Ānandāśrama text reads आहुतिः ‘the offering’, which the sense needs (‘the utterance is the offering’). The edition keeps the base and translates the Ānandāśrama reading.
समिधम्
In form an accusative of samidh (or a neuter a-stem); it stands where a nominative is expected (‘what he eats evening and morning, that is the fuel’).
ये
With the dual अहोरात्रे the relative and correlative (ये … ते) are taken as duals; elsewhere in the list they are plurals.
प्रमीयते
pra-√mī ‘to die’, middle; the subject is the one who knows, who dies in the northern or the southern course of the sun.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

Three parts. (1) A long series of nominal identifications of the knower’s parts with parts of the soma sacrifice: subject (part of the person) and predicate (part of the rite) in the nominative, without a verb. (2) Relative–correlative pairs (यत् … तत्, यावत् … सा, ये … ते) that map his acts and his times onto rites. (3) Two conditional relative sentences (य एवं विद्वान् … प्रमीयते …; अथ यो दक्षिणे प्रमीयते …) with absolutives (गत्वा), and the closing statement, its last clause repeated, ended by इत्युपनिषत्.

Ambiguities

the two genitives विदुषो यज्ञस्य
Sāyaṇa and the anonymous commentator, following the Brahmasūtra discussion they cite, take them apart: the sacrifice belonging to the knower, whose self is the sacrificer — not the knower as the sacrifice (विदुषो यो यज्ञस्तस्य यज्ञस्याऽऽत्मेति तैत्तिरीयके व्यधिकरणे षष्ठ्यौ). Bhāskara: the sacrifice of the renunciant who knows, whose self is the sacrificer by its own nature.
दमः शमयिता दक्षिणा
The pairing is uncertain: either ‘restraint is the quieter, [and X] the fee’, or ‘restraint, the quieter, is the fee’. Sāyaṇa: restraint, which quiets the senses, is to be understood as the fee (यस्तु दमाख्यः शमयिता सर्वेन्द्रियोपशमकारी चित्तवृत्तिविशेषः, तस्य दक्षिणारूपत्वमुन्नेयम्); Bhāskara pairs शमयिता with दक्षिणा.
the time of dying
The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa read the last part as the reward of one who meditates, reached step by step (क्रममुक्तिलक्षणं फलमाह), and say the brāhmaṇa who wins the two greatnesses goes on to Hiraṇyagarbha’s world and then to brahman; Bhāskara takes ‘brāhmaṇa’ as an example of anyone who knows (ब्राह्मणग्रहणं प्रदर्शनार्थं - सर्वोपि तद्विद्वानभिजयति).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Meaning in ordinary life

The passage takes the most ordinary acts — eating, drinking, resting, walking, sitting, standing — and names each as part of a sacred rite. One can read it today as saying that a life lived with full attention needs no separate ceremony to be meaningful. This is a modern reflection, not part of the traditional reading.
