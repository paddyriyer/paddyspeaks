Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 30 of 80

Āpaḥ punantu: the water sipped at the midday worship

A prayer that the waters, which purify the earth and the teacher, purify the one who sips them of what was wrongly eaten, done or accepted.

5 Faithful English translation

May the waters purify the earth; may the earth, purified, purify me. May they purify the lord of brahman; may brahman, purified, purify me. Whatever is leftover, unfit to eat, or whatever is my misconduct — may the waters purify me of all [that], and of the accepting [of gifts] from the bad. Svāhā.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

May the waters purify the earth, and the purified earth purify me. May they purify the teacher, the guardian of the Veda, and the purified Veda purify me. Whatever I have eaten that was left over or unfit, whatever I have done amiss, whatever I have taken from the unworthy — of all of it may the waters purify me. Svāhā.

Key concepts सन्ध्या sandhyā, the junction of day and night; the worship performed at it मन्त्र mantra ब्रह्मन् Brahman वेद Veda आचार्य ācārya, teacher

7 Explain it to a beginner

In the daily worship at sunrise, noon and sunset, the worshipper sips a little water while reciting a short prayer. This is the prayer for noon. It imagines purification passing along a chain: water cleans the earth, and the cleaned earth cleans me; water cleans the teacher who guards the sacred texts, and the texts, made clean, clean me. Then it names what needs cleaning: food that was left over or should not have been eaten, things done wrongly, and gifts taken from people who should not have been accepted. The prayer ends with svāhā, the word spoken when something is offered into a sacred fire. The old commentators explain that the water sipped is itself treated as an offering, placed into the fire of one’s own mouth. What the prayer asks for is stated; nothing here claims what reciting it does.

An analogy Like rinsing a cloth in a stream: the water that cleaned the riverbed upstream reaches the cloth too.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

It asks the waters to purify the earth and, through the purified earth, the speaker; to purify ‘the lord of brahman’ and, through purified brahman, the speaker; and to purify the speaker of leftover or forbidden food, misconduct and acceptance from the bad. It ends with svāhā.

Traditional Interpretation

When is it recited?

Both commentaries say it is the mantra for drinking consecrated water in the midday worship. The anonymous commentator: माध्याह्निकसंध्यानुष्ठानेऽभिमन्त्रितजलपानार्थं मन्त्रमाह. Sāyaṇa, with almost the same words: माध्याह्निकसंध्यानुष्ठानेऽभिमन्त्रजलपानार्थं मन्त्रमाह. The anuvākas that follow give the evening (10.31) and morning (10.32) mantras for the same act.

Traditional Interpretation

Who is the ‘lord of brahman’?

Sāyaṇa takes brahma as the Veda and its lord as the teacher who guards it: पतिं प्रतिपालकमाचार्यमेता आपः पुनन्तु; the brahma that purifies is the Veda that teacher taught: तेनाऽऽचार्येणोपदिष्टं ब्रह्म. The anonymous commentator repeats the reading. Neither takes Brahmaṇaspati here as the god of that name.

Traditional Interpretation

What is to be purified?

Sāyaṇa explains each item: leftover food is what remains after another has eaten (अन्यभुक्तावशिष्टरूपमुच्छिष्टं), misconduct is doing what is forbidden (प्रतिषिद्धाचरणरूपं). He glosses ‘the bad’ in asatām pratigraham by a list of social groups of his own time (शूद्रादीनां प्रतिग्रहं); the edition reports that gloss, it does not adopt it. The text itself says only ‘of the bad’.

Traditional Interpretation

Why does a water-sipping end with svāhā?

Svāhā is said when an offering is put into fire. Both commentators make the sip an offering into the fire of the speaker’s mouth: स्वाहा मदीयवक्त्राग्नौ स्वाहुतमस्तु (Sāyaṇa; the anonymous commentator has सुहुतमस्तु). The same idea, eating and drinking as an offering within, is worked out in the Chāndogya’s account of the first morsel offered to breath.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What do the witnesses show?

The three witnesses agree on the words. The Ānandāśrama pariśiṣṭa text writes an extra nasal sign in प्रतिग्रहं स्वाहा, and the Tirupati e-text keys छ as च्ह; both are classified as spelling or witness errors on the sources page. Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara’s comment on this mantra is not in the edition’s copy of his work.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • आपः पुनन्तु āpaḥ punantu → आपः + पुनन्तु

    visarga before a voiceless labial

    The visarga stays before p: āpaḥ punantu.

  • ब्रह्मणस्पतिर्ब्रह्म brahmaṇaspatirbrahma → ब्रह्मणस्पतिः + ब्रह्म

    visarga sandhi (after i)

    A visarga after a vowel other than a or ā becomes r before a voiced sound: brahmaṇaspatiḥ + brahma → brahmaṇaspatir brahma.

  • यदुच्छिष्टमभोज्यं yaducchiṣṭamabhojyaṃ → यत् + उच्छिष्टम् + अभोज्यम्

    final t voiced; final m before a vowel

    A final t becomes d before a vowel (yad ucchiṣṭam); a final m is simply joined to a following vowel (ucchiṣṭam abhojyam).

  • मामापोऽसतां māmāpo'satāṃ → माम् + आपः + असताम्

    final m before a vowel; aḥ before a

    m joins the vowel (mām āpaḥ); -aḥ before a becomes -o and the a is elided, marked by avagraha: āpaḥ + asatām → āpo ’satām (P. 6.1.109 (एङः पदान्तादति)).

Morphology

ब्रह्मणस्पतिः
The sense requires an object of punantu (‘may [the waters] purify the lord of brahman’), and both commentators read it so (Sāyaṇa: पतिं प्रतिपालकमाचार्यमेता आपः पुनन्तु); the text prints the nominative form. Brahmaṇaspati is also the name of a Vedic deity; the commentators here take the phrase as the teacher who guards the Veda.
पूता
Feminine, agreeing with pṛthivī in the first line; in the second line it stands with the neuter brahma, where the commentators read it as pūtam (Sāyaṇa: पूता स्वयं पूतं सन्). The repetition of the first line’s wording explains the form.
स्वाहा
The exclamation used when an offering is made into fire. Here the water sipped is treated as an offering; both commentators say into the fire of one’s own mouth.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

Three wishes in the imperative, each built the same way: the waters purify X, and X, purified, purifies me (earth; the lord of brahman and brahman). The third gathers what needs purifying into two relative clauses (yad … yad vā) summed up by sarvam, adds a further object joined by ca (asatāṃ ca pratigraham), and closes with svāhā.

Ambiguities

brahmaṇaspati and brahma
Brahmaṇaspati is a Vedic god; both commentators instead take the phrase as ‘the lord of the Veda’, the teacher, and brahma as the Veda he taught. The text itself does not say which is meant.
pratigraham
The object of ‘purify’ is grammatically loose: ‘[purify me of] the accepting from the bad’. Both commentators supply that the act has been done by the speaker.

Metre

anuṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 17 + 31 + 17

Two anuṣṭubh stanzas printed in three lines, counted 17, 31 and 17 syllables. The pādas run 8 + 9 (āpaḥ punantu pṛthivīm / pṛthivī pūtā punātu mām), 8 + 8, then, from yad ucchiṣṭam in the middle line, 7 + 8 and 8 + 7, with the closing svāhā (2) outside the metre.

Textual variations between witnesses

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Wealth and consumption

The prayer lists, without drama, the ordinary ways a day goes wrong: eating what one should not, acting badly, taking what one should not have taken. It asks to be cleaned of them in the middle of the day, not only at its end. A periodic, honest look at what one has consumed and accepted is a discipline that does not depend on any rite. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin The prayer names three kinds of thing to be cleaned of. Which would you add or remove for your own day?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Why might a text describe purification as a chain — water, earth, me; water, teacher, Veda, me — rather than as water acting on the speaker directly?

A contemplation exercise At midday, pause for a minute and recall what you have taken in and given out since morning, without judging it.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
