Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 74 of 80

Prāṇānāṃ granthir asi: touching the heart after the meal

A short formula said while touching the heart after eating: the one addressed is the knot that holds the breaths together, asked to enter as Rudra and to grow strong by the food.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

You are the knot that holds the breaths together. Enter me as Rudra, the one who brings to an end; grow strong by that food.

Key concepts प्राण breath, life-force ग्रन्थि knot (of the heart) अन्न food

7 Explain it to a beginner

This is one of the short formulas said after a meal. According to the commentators, the speaker touches the region of the heart and addresses what is there. The first words say: you are the knot of the breaths, the thing that ties the body’s vital breaths together so that they do not come apart. Then: enter me as Rudra, the one who brings things to an end; and grow strong by that food. The commentators disagree about who is being spoken to. Two of them say it is the sense of ‘I’ that sits in the heart and holds the faculties together. Bhāskara says it is the fire of digestion, which keeps the breaths in place by digesting what was eaten. The edition reports their readings and makes no claim about effects.

An analogy Like the knot at the centre of a bundle of cords: it is not one of the cords, but without it they fall apart.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the formula say?

It tells someone ‘you are the knot of the breaths’, asks that one to enter the speaker as Rudra, the ender, and to swell, or make the speaker swell, by that food.

Traditional Interpretation

When is it said?

Sāyaṇa places it after the formula that addresses the food just eaten (10.70): अनुमन्त्रणादूर्ध्वं हृदयाभिमर्शने मन्त्रमाह — the mantra for touching the heart. Bhāskara names the same act: प्राणानामिति हृदयाभिमर्शनम्. The anonymous commentator also has it said with a touch, after the japa that precedes it.

Comparative Vedānta

Who is the knot of the breaths?

Sāyaṇa addresses the I-sense: त्वं वायुरूपाणामिन्द्रियरूपाणां च प्राणानां ग्रन्थिरसि परस्परमविश्लेषाय ग्रथनहेतुरसि — you are the knot of the breaths, both the airs and the senses, the cause of their being tied so that they do not separate; the anonymous commentator says the same (प्राणानां ग्रन्थिरसि। परस्परमविश्लेषाय ग्रन्थनहेतुरसि). Bhāskara addresses the digestive fire: हे जाठराग्ने … त्वया हि भुक्ते जीर्णे प्राणास् तिष्ठन्ति — when what is eaten has been digested by you, the breaths stay.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Grow, or make grow?

āpyāyasva is the middle imperative of √pyāy, ‘swell’. The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa gloss it causatively, ‘make me grow’. Bhāskara, explaining the same verb in the formula of 10.70, says that pyāyati is to be understood with the causative sense contained in it: अन्तर्भावितण्यर्थः प्यायतिर्द्रष्टव्यः.

Traditional Interpretation

Why Rudra, the ender?

The anonymous commentator takes Rudra as the deity of what is addressed and ‘ender’ as the one who ends sorrow (दुःखस्य विनाशको भूत्वा), and the last words as ‘make me grow by the food I have eaten’ (मामभिवर्धय). Bhāskara reads Rudra as the great fire that consumes: रोदयिता अन्तकः महार्थसंहारी.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • ग्रन्थिरसि granthirasi → ग्रन्थिः + असि

    visarga after i before a vowel

    granthiḥ + asi → granthir asi: after a vowel other than a/ā the visarga becomes r before a vowel or voiced sound.

  • रुद्रो मा rudro mā → रुद्रः + मा

    visarga after a before a voiced consonant

    rudraḥ + mā → rudro mā: -aḥ becomes -o before a voiced consonant.

  • विशान्तकः viśāntakaḥ → विश + अन्तकः

    a + a

    viśa + antakaḥ → viśāntakaḥ: two short a fuse into ā.

  • तेनान्नेनाप्यायस्व tenānnenāpyāyasva → तेन + अन्नेन + आप्यायस्व

    a + a; a + ā

    tena + annena → tenānnena; annena + āpyāyasva → annenāpyāyasva: a fuses with a or ā into ā.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this anuvāka.

Morphology

आप्यायस्व
Imperative middle of √pyāy with ā-, ‘swell, increase’. Read intransitively it says ‘grow strong’; the anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa gloss it with a causative, ‘make me grow’. Bhāskara, on the same verb at 10.70, notes that pyāyati can carry the causative sense within it (अन्तर्भावितण्यर्थः प्यायतिर्द्रष्टव्यः).
मा
The enclitic accusative of the first person (= mām), not the prohibitive particle mā: the verb viśa is a positive imperative.

Anvaya

प्राणानाम् ग्रन्थिः असि । रुद्रः अन्तकः मा विश । तेन अन्नेन आप्यायस्व

prāṇānām granthiḥ asi | rudraḥ antakaḥ mā viśa | tena annena āpyāyasva

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Three short clauses addressed to one ‘you’: a statement with asi, a command with viśa whose subject is described as rudraḥ … antakaḥ, and a command with āpyāyasva governing the instrumental tena annena.

Ambiguities

who is addressed
The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa address the I-sense in the heart (ahaṅkāra), which ties the breaths and the senses together. Bhāskara addresses the digestive fire: when what is eaten is digested, the breaths remain.
antakaḥ
For the anonymous commentator the ‘ender’ is the one who ends sorrow (दुःखस्य विनाशको); for Bhāskara Rudra as consumer, the great fire that burns up what is eaten.
āpyāyasva
Intransitive (‘grow strong’) or causative (‘make me grow’); see morphology.

Metre

prose · syllables counted as written: 14 + 7

A yajus formula, not a metrical verse; the two lines count 14 and 7 syllables.

Textual variations between witnesses

The witnesses differ here only in spelling (listed in section 14).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Embodiment

The formula treats eating as something that happens to the whole person: the faculties are thought of as held together, and the meal as what lets them stay together. A moment of noticing the body after eating is a simple form of attention. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin What could it mean to call something ‘the knot’ of the breaths?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Two commentators address the I-sense, one the digestive fire. What does each choice say about what keeps a living body together?

A contemplation exercise After a meal, sit still for a minute and notice how the body feels before getting up.
