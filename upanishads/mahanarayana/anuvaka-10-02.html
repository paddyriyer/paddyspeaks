Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 2 of 80

Jātavedase sunavāma: the hymn to Durgā

Seven verses to Agni Jātavedas and the goddess Durgā asking to be carried across every difficulty, as a boat carries across the sea.

1 Original Sanskrit

जातवेदसे सुनवाम सोममरातीयतो निदहाति वेदः ।स नः पर्षदति दुर्गाणि विश्वा नावेव सिन्धुं दुरिताऽत्यग्निः ।तामग्निवर्णां तपसा ज्वलन्तीं वैरोचनीं कर्मफलेषु जुष्टाम् ।दुर्गां देवीं शरणमहं प्रपद्ये सुतरसि तरसे नमः ।अग्ने त्वं पारया नव्यो अस्मान्त्स्वस्तिभिरति दुर्गाणि विश्वा ।पूश्च पृथ्वी बहुला न उर्वी भवा तोकाय तनयाय शंयोः ।विश्वानि नो दुर्गहा जातवेदस्सिन्धुं न नावा दुरिताऽतिपर्षि ।अग्ने अत्रिवन्मनसा गृणानोऽस्माकं बोध्यविता तनूनाम् ।पृतनाजितं सहमानमुग्रमग्निं हुवेम परमात्सधस्थात् ।स नः पर्षदति दुर्गाणि विश्वा क्षामद्देवो अतिदुरिताऽत्यग्निः ।प्रत्नोषि कमीड्यो अध्वरेषु सनाच्च होता नव्यश्च सत्सि ।स्वां चाग्ने तनुवं पिप्रयस्वास्मभ्यं च सौभगमायजस्व ।गोभिर्जुष्टमयुजो निषिक्तं तवेन्द्र विष्णोरनुसञ्चरेम ।नाकस्य पृष्ठमभि संवसानो वैष्णवीं लोक इह मादयन्ताम् ॥ १६ ॥

5 Faithful English translation

To Jātavedas let us press soma; may the knower burn down those who are hostile. May he carry us across all difficulties; as with a boat across the sea, Agni [carries us] across troubles. To her, fire-coloured, blazing with heat, belonging to the radiant one, favoured in the fruits of action — to Durgā the goddess I go for refuge; you who carry across well, to the one who carries across, homage. O Agni, to be praised, carry us safely across all difficulties; let our fort be broad and abundant, our land wide; be welfare and health for our children and descendants. Destroyer of difficulties, Jātavedas, you carry us across all troubles as across the sea by a boat. O Agni, praised in the mind like Atri, be the protector of our bodies. Let us call Agni, conqueror of armies, overpowering, fierce, from his highest seat; may he carry us across all difficulties, the forgiving god, Agni [carrying us] across troubles. You spread happiness, praised at the sacrifices; of old and new you sit as hotṛ. O Agni, gladden your own body, and bestow good fortune on us. What is enjoyed by cows, poured down, of you the unjoined one, O Indra, of Viṣṇu — may we follow it. Dwelling upon the vault of heaven, may they gladden [me, devoted] to Viṣṇu, here in this world.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

For the fire that knows all, let us press the soma; may he burn away all that is hostile, and carry us across every danger, as a boat carries across the sea. In the fire-bright goddess Durgā, blazing with her own heat, I take refuge; homage to her who carries us across. Agni, carry us safely over every hardship; make our home broad and our land wide; be well-being for our children. Watch over us as the sage Atri does; we call you from your highest place, conqueror and protector; forgive our faults and carry us across. Praised at every rite, gladden yourself and give us good fortune. May we follow Indra and Viṣṇu, and may the gods who dwell above give joy here to the one devoted to Viṣṇu.

Key concepts अग्नि Agni, fire मन्त्र mantra तपस् austerity; disciplined effort

7 Explain it to a beginner

This is the hymn many people know as the Durgā Sūkta. Its picture is a crossing: as a boat carries people over the sea, so may the fire-god, Agni, “the one who knows all that is born”, carry us over every danger and every wrong. In the second verse the speaker takes refuge in the goddess Durgā, bright as fire and blazing with her own heat, and greets her as the one who carries across. The other verses ask Agni to burn away what is hostile, to make the home secure and the land wide, to watch over the family, to forgive faults, and to bring good fortune; the last calls on Indra and Viṣṇu. Bhāskara says these verses are used in recitation and offerings when fear or misfortune comes; the anonymous commentator, for warding off harm. The record reports this and claims nothing about what reciting it achieves.

An analogy Like a ferryman asked, in seven different ways, to take passengers safely over a wide and dangerous river.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

In seven verses it presses soma for Jātavedas and asks him to burn the hostile and carry us across all difficulties as a boat crosses the sea; takes refuge in the fire-coloured goddess Durgā; asks Agni to carry us across, make our dwelling broad and be welfare for our children; to protect our bodies; it calls Agni from his highest seat; praises him as the priest of old and new; and asks to follow Indra and Viṣṇu.

Traditional Interpretation

When is it recited?

Bhāskara: these Durgā mantras are for japa and homa when fear of thieves, tigers or spirits arises, or in misfortune — चोरव्याघ्रपिशाचादिभये समुपस्थिते दौर्गत्ये च जपहोमादिकर्म एभिः कुर्यात्. Sāyaṇa and the anonymous commentator: mantras to be recited for warding off harm (अथानिष्टपरिहारार्थत्वेन जप्या मन्त्रा उच्यन्ते, Sāyaṇa), the first verse explained as the ṛk to Agni who burns enemies and carries us across sins as a boatman carries across the sea.

Traditional Interpretation

Who is Durgā here?

The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa: the Durgā known from the Durgā rituals, called vairocanī because she is seen by Virocana, the supreme self — विशेषेण रोचते स्वयमेव प्रकाशत इति विरोचनः परमात्मा, तेन दृष्टत्वाद्वैरोचनीम् (Sāyaṇa). Bhāskara: the supreme nature belonging to the god who shines in many forms (विविधमाविर्भवतः देवस्य स्वभूतां परप्रकृतिं), and Durgā is a name of fire (दुर्ग इत्यग्नेर्नाम तदात्मिका देवी दुर्गा, in his comment on the Durgā gāyatrī of 10.1).

Traditional Interpretation

What does ‘like Atri’ mean?

Sāyaṇa and the anonymous commentator take Atri as a liberated sage, free of the three afflictions, who wishes all beings well in his mind; Agni is asked to do the same and so protect our bodies. Sāyaṇa quotes two verses for the attitude: आत्मौपम्येन भूतानां दयां कुर्वीत मानवः and सर्वे च सुखिनः सन्तु सर्वे सन्तु निरामयाः.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Where do the verses come from?

The first verse is the Ṛgveda’s 1.99.1 and the third its 1.189.2, with small differences of spelling; the others are found elsewhere in the Vedic hymns (see references). The commentators of this text explain all seven; Sāyaṇa and Bhāskara stop before the seventh (gobhir juṣṭam), which only the anonymous commentator explains. The witnesses differ in a few slips and in the reading नावा / वावा in the seventh half-verse, which the sources page lists for review.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • सोममरातीयतो somamarātīyato → सोमम् + अरातीयतः

    m before a vowel; aḥ before a voiced consonant

    somam arātīyato (before n).

  • स नः पर्षदति sa naḥ parṣadati → सः + नः + पर्षत् + अति

    saḥ before a consonant; t before a vowel

    saḥ drops its visarga before a consonant (sa naḥ); parṣat + ati → parṣad ati.

  • नावेव nāveva → नावा + इव

    ā + i

    nāvā + iva → nāveva (P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः)).

  • दुरिताऽत्यग्निः duritā'tyagniḥ → दुरिता + अति + अग्निः

    ā + a; i before a dissimilar vowel

    duritā + ati → duritāti (the print marks the merged a with avagraha); ati + agniḥ → aty agniḥ.

  • अस्मान्त्स्वस्तिभिरति asmāntsvastibhirati → अस्मान् + स्वस्तिभिः + अति

    n before s; visarga after i before a vowel

    A t is inserted between final n and s (asmānt svastibhiḥ); svastibhiḥ becomes svastibhir before a vowel.

  • गृणानोऽस्माकं gṛṇāno'smākaṃ → गृणानः + अस्माकम्

    aḥ before a

    gṛṇāno ’smākam, with the a elided (P. 6.1.109 (एङः पदान्तादति)).

  • तवेन्द्र tavendra → तव + इन्द्र

    a + i

    tava + indra → tavendra (guṇa).

  • लोक इह loka iha → लोके + इह

    e before a vowel other than a

    loke + iha → loka iha, the y dropped and a gap left.

Junctions outside the general rules

  • अस्मान् · स्वस्तिभिः — unjoined hiatus (as printed): navyo · asmān: Vedic, the a is not elided after -o (classical navyo ’smān); the Ṛgveda text (RV 1.189.2) has the same.
  • अत्रिवत् · मनसा — unjoined hiatus (as printed): agne · atrivat: the a of atrivat is not elided after agne (classical agne ’trivat).
  • अति · दुरिता — unjoined hiatus (as printed): devo · ati: the a of ati is not elided after -o.
  • अध्वरेषु · सनात् — unjoined hiatus (as printed): īḍyo · adhvareṣu: the a is not elided after -o.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

Seven triṣṭubh verses, each in two printed lines. The first, second and fifth turn on the verb √pṛ ‘carry across’ (parṣat, pāraya, atiparṣi), with the image of the boat (nāvā iva/na sindhum). The second verse is the act of refuge (śaraṇam ahaṃ prapadye) to Durgā; the others are prayers to Agni, ending with Indra and Viṣṇu.

Ambiguities

Durgā and Agni
The hymn names the goddess Durgā only in the second verse; the rest address Agni. Bhāskara: Agni is presented as the special fire that is Durgā (अग्निर् अग्रणीर् दुर्गारूपोऽग्निविशेषः प्रतिपाद्यते); Sāyaṇa: Durgā as known from the Durgā rituals and the mantra-śāstras (येयं नवदुर्गाकल्पादिषु मन्त्रशास्त्रेषु प्रसिद्धा), which the anonymous commentator repeats.
durgāṇi
‘Hard places, dangers’: misfortunes for the anonymous commentator (विश्वा दुर्गाणि सर्वा आपदोऽतिपर्षत्); places such as saṃsāra for Bhāskara (स्थानानि संसारादीन्य्).

Metre

triṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 22 + 21 + 22 + 21 + 20 + 21 + 22 + 20 + 22 + 22 + 20 + 20 + 21 + 22

Seven triṣṭubh verses printed as fourteen half-verses, counted 22, 21, 22, 21, 20, 21, 22, 20, 22, 22, 20, 20, 21 and 22 syllables. The shortfalls come from vowels joined in the print (duritāty agniḥ, nāveva) and from the fourth half-verse (sutarasi tarase namaḥ), whose second pāda has 9 or 10 syllables; the Ṛgveda verses 1.99.1 and 1.189.2 that open verses one and three have the full count.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Crossing hard places

The hymn returns again and again to one picture: a crossing, made safely with help. It does not promise that the water will be calm, only that one need not cross alone. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Why might a prayer for safety use the image of a boat rather than, say, a wall?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The hymn names Durgā once and Agni throughout. What does it mean to take refuge in a goddess within a hymn to fire, and how do the commentators handle it?

A contemplation exercise Think of one “crossing” in your life now, and name who or what helps you across it.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.

Further notes

  • Other verses of the hymn also occur in the Ṛgveda outside its first book (for example the fourth verse, viśvāni no durgahā, at RV 5.4.9); the edition’s Ṛgveda source covers only the first book, so these are noted here and not quoted.
  • Sāyaṇa and Bhāskara comment on this hymn within their first anuvāka; Bhāskara refers verses three and four to his comments elsewhere in the Āraṇyaka (अग्ने त्वमिति व्याख्याता ‘उभा वां’ इत्यत्र).

Uncertainties recorded

  • review In सिन्धुं न नावा the Tirupati e-text reads वावा; the base and the Ānandāśrama text read नावा. Listed for checking.
  • interpretation Whether the hymn is addressed chiefly to Agni with Durgā as his form, or to Durgā, is a matter of commentary.
