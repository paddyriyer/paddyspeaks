Namo brahmaṇe: a prayer not to forget

A prayer to retain what has been heard: may it be held and not slip away.

5 Faithful English translation

Homage to Brahman. Let there be retention in me; may I be a holder without forgetting. What is heard in the ears — do not let it slip — of me, of that one. Om.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Homage to Brahman. May I hold what I have learned, and keep it without forgetting; let what my ears have heard not slip away from me. Om.

Key concepts ब्रह्मन् Brahman वेद Veda श्रुति śruti, ‘what is heard’

7 Explain it to a beginner

A short prayer that what one has learned should stay. It begins with homage to Brahman, then asks: may I hold what I have learned, may I keep it without forgetting, and may what my ears have heard not slip away. In ancient India knowledge was carried by memory, heard from a teacher and repeated until it was fixed, so forgetting meant losing it. The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa say this is a prayer, recited quietly, for keeping the Vedas one has received. Bhāskara reads it more inwardly: the holding is a steady focus on Brahman, and it is the senses that are told not to wander. The record reports these readings and claims nothing about what reciting it does.

An analogy Like asking a cupped hand not to open before the water reaches the mouth.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

It pays homage to Brahman, asks that there be retention in the speaker and that the speaker hold without forgetting, and asks that what the ears have heard should not slip away; it closes with om.

Traditional Interpretation

What is it for?

The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa: a japa for not forgetting the Vedas one has received — अथ लब्धानां वेदानामविस्मरणाय जप्यं मन्त्रमाह. Brahman is greeted as the cause of the world, and by its grace there should be retention of both the text and its meaning: तत्प्रसादान्मदीये चित्ते ग्रन्थतदर्थयोर्धारणमस्तु.

Traditional Interpretation

How does Bhāskara read the ‘holding’?

For Bhāskara the holding is one-pointedness, becoming one with Brahman: तद्भावापत्तिस् तदर्थं धारणम् ऐकाग्र्यं मे अस्तु. The plural ‘do not slip’ is addressed to the senses, which have enjoyed their objects until now and must not now fall away from Brahman: यूयं मा च्योढ्वं मा ब्रह्मणश्च्युतानि भूत.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What do the witnesses show?

Only one difference is listed for this anuvāka, a slip in one witness. The hiatus in मे अस्त्व् and अमुष्य ओम् is in every witness.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • नमो ब्रह्मणे namo brahmaṇe → नमः + ब्रह्मणे

    aḥ before a voiced consonant

    namaḥ becomes namo before b.

  • धारणं मे dhāraṇaṃ me → धारणम् + मे

    m before a consonant

    A final m becomes anusvāra before a consonant.

  • अस्त्वनिराकरणं astvanirākaraṇaṃ → अस्तु + अनिराकरणम्

    u before a dissimilar vowel

    astu + anirākaraṇam → astv anirākaraṇam.

  • ममामुष्य mamāmuṣya → मम + अमुष्य

    a + a

    mama + amuṣya → mamāmuṣya.

Junctions outside the general rules

  • मे · अस्तु — unjoined hiatus (as printed): me · astu: the e of me is not joined to the following a (classical me ’stu); every witness prints them apart, as Vedic texts often do.
  • अमुष्य · ओम् — unjoined hiatus (as printed): amuṣya · om: the a is not joined to om (classical amuṣyom); Bhāskara notes the hiatus as natural here (ईषाअक्षादित्वात् प्रकृतिभावः).

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • अनिराकरणम् anirākaraṇam

    nañ-tatpuruṣa · अ + निराकरण

    Vigraha न निराकरणम्

    non-rejection, not forgetting

Morphology

च्योढ्वम्
Second person plural middle of √cyu without augment, used with mā as a prohibition. Sāyaṇa supplies the gods as the persons addressed; Bhāskara the senses (हे तत्परिपन्थीनि! इन्द्रियाणि!).
धारयिता
An agent noun in -tṛ used with bhūyāsam: ‘may I be a holder’, i.e. may I retain.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

A salutation (namo brahmaṇe), a wish with astu, a wish with the precative bhūyāsam, and a prohibition with mā and the injunctive cyoḍhvam, whose subject or addressee is left to be supplied; mama amuṣya names the speaker.

Ambiguities

who is told ‘do not slip’
The plural cyoḍhvam has no stated subject. Sāyaṇa addresses the gods (हे देवा), the anonymous commentator a god, Bhāskara the senses that draw the mind away.
amuṣya
‘Of that one’: the anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa read it as the speaker (‘of me, the one praying thus’, a place for one’s own name); Bhāskara reads it as the supreme Brahman, with whom the speaker is one (तदात्मनो ब्रह्मीभूतस्य मम).

Metre

prose · syllables counted as written: 36

A prose prayer, one printed line of 36 syllables; no metre is claimed.

Textual variations between witnesses

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Memory and learning

The prayer treats forgetting as the real danger to learning, and asks not for more knowledge but for keeping what has been received. Revisiting what one has already learned before seeking more is still sound advice. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin What is one thing you learned long ago that you are glad you never forgot?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Is the “holding” here memory (the anonymous commentator, Sāyaṇa) or concentration (Bhāskara)? Can the two be separated?

A contemplation exercise Recall something you learned last week, without looking it up, and write it down.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
