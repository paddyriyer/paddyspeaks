Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 32 of 80

Sūryaś ca mā: the water sipped at the morning worship

The morning counterpart of 10.31: Sūrya and the powers over anger are asked for protection, the night’s wrongs are left to the night, and what remains is offered into the sun, the light.

5 Faithful English translation

May Sūrya and manyu and the lords of manyu protect me from sins done through manyu. Whatever sin I have done by night — by mind, by speech, by the hands, by the feet, by the belly, by the sexual organ — may the night wipe that out. Whatever wrong [is] in me, this [and] myself I offer into the source of immortality, into the sun, into the light. Svāhā.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

May the Sun, and anger, and the powers that rule anger guard me from the wrongs that anger brings. Whatever wrong I did in the night — in thought, in word, with hands or feet, by appetite or desire — may the night that has passed wipe it away. Whatever wrong remains in me, I offer it, and myself with it, into the source of deathlessness, the sun, the light. Svāhā.

Key concepts सन्ध्या sandhyā, the junction of day and night; the worship performed at it सूर्य the Sun मनस् mind अमृत immortality, the deathless मन्त्र mantra

7 Explain it to a beginner

This is the prayer for sipping water at the morning worship. It repeats the evening prayer of the previous section with three changes. The Sun takes the place of Fire as the first protector; the wrongs to be wiped away are those done during the night, and the night that has just ended is asked to take them; and the speaker offers what remains, and himself, ‘into the sun, the light’. The old commentators say little more than that: one of them says the whole is explained as before, and another explains that the sun is named because it rules the day that is beginning. Together with the noon and evening prayers, it frames the day in three short reviews.

An analogy Like opening a shop in the morning: the night’s mess is swept out first, so the day starts clean.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

The words of 10.31 with three changes: Sūrya for Agni, the sin done ‘by night’ wiped out by the night, and the offering made ‘into the sun, into the light’.

Traditional Interpretation

When is it recited?

The anonymous commentator: प्रातःसंध्याकाले जलपानार्थं मन्त्रमाह — the mantra for drinking water at the morning worship; Sāyaṇa has the same sentence. Bhāskara: सूर्यश्चेति प्रातराचमनीयम्.

Comparative Vedānta

How do the commentators explain the changes?

The anonymous commentator explains only ‘the sun’, as Brahman with the sun as its adjunct, and refers the rest to the evening mantra: सूर्येऽहर्निष्पादके सूर्योपाधिके। अन्यत्सर्वं पूर्ववद्ध्याख्येयम्. Bhāskara makes the sun the lord of the day that is beginning (सूर्य उपक्रान्तस्य अह्नोऽधिपतिः), notes that the night is to wipe out the night’s sin, and that the offering is made into the lord of the day (उपक्रान्तस्यान्ह्नोऽधिपतौ होमः) — as in the evening the offering was made into Agni, lord of the night.

Traditional Interpretation

Where do the word glosses come from?

Both commentators send the reader back to the evening mantra (अन्यत्सर्वं पूर्ववद्ध्याख्येयम्), so the glosses in the word table are taken from Sāyaṇa’s comment on 10.31 (his anuvāka 24): manyu as the deity of anger, the source of immortality as the deathless cause of the world (मरणरहिते जगत्कारणे). The Vedic instrumental rātriyā is of the kind the anonymous commentator calls Vedic usages when it recurs in 10.34 (एते छान्दसाः प्रयोगाः).

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • पापमकार्षम् pāpamakārṣam → पापम् + अकार्षम्

    final m before a vowel

    pāpam akārṣam: the m is joined to the vowel.

  • यत्किञ्च yatkiñca → यत् + किम् + च

    final m before c

    kim + ca → kiñ ca: m becomes the nasal of the following stop (P. 8.4.59 (वा पदान्तस्य)).

  • इदमहं माममृतयोनौ idamahaṃ māmamṛtayonau → इदम् + अहम् + माम् + अमृतयोनौ

    final m before a vowel

    idam aham, mām amṛtayonau: m joined to the following vowel; aham before m is written with anusvāra.

  • सूर्यश्च sūryaśca → सूर्यः + च

    visarga before c

    A visarga becomes ś before c: sūryaḥ + ca → sūryaś ca.

  • यद्रात्रिया yadrātriyā → यत् + रात्रिया

    final t voiced before a voiced consonant

    yat + rātriyā → yad rātriyā (P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते)).

  • रात्रिस्तदवलुम्पतु rātristadavalumpatu → रात्रिः + तत् + अवलुम्पतु

    visarga before t; final t voiced

    rātriḥ + tat → rātris tat; tat + avalumpatu → tad avalumpatu.

Morphology

शिश्ना
Instrumental of śiśna with the Vedic ending -ā for -ena; Bhāskara names the rule where the same words first occur (10.31, his 24): सुपां सुलुक् इत्याकारः.
अकार्षम्
First person singular of the s-aorist of √kṛ: ‘I have done’.
रात्रिया
A Vedic instrumental of rātri, ‘by night’, with -iyā for classical rātryā; the same form recurs in 10.34 (rātriyāt). The anonymous commentator calls such forms Vedic usages (एते छान्दसाः प्रयोगाः, on 10.34).

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

A wish in the third person imperative (rakṣantām), with three subjects joined by ca and an ablative of what is guarded against. Then a relative clause (yad … akārṣam) with six instrumentals of the means, resumed by tad and a second imperative (avalumpatu). The last sentence turns to the first person: a relative clause (yat kiṃ ca … mayi) and a main clause idam ahaṃ māṃ … juhomi with three locatives of where the offering is made.

Ambiguities

manyu
Anger (Sāyaṇa, the anonymous commentator, and Bhāskara’s alternative) or the thinking inner faculty (Bhāskara’s first reading).
what rakṣantām protects from
Sāyaṇa reads it as protection by removing sins already done (पापिनं मां तत्पापविनाशनेन पालयन्तु); Bhāskara as protection from sins not yet done, ‘as one guards a house against thieves’ (see commentary).
sūrye jyotiṣi
The evening mantra offers ‘into the true’ (satye); this one ‘into the sun’. The anonymous commentator reads the sun as Brahman known through the sun (सूर्योपाधिके); Bhāskara as the lord of the day now beginning.

Metre

mixed · syllables counted as written: 18 + 6 + 9 + 8 + 8 + 8 + 8 + 10 + 10

The pattern of 10.31 (which Bhāskara analyses), with rātriyā for ahnā: as printed the nine lines count 18, 6, 9, 8, 8, 8, 8, 10 and 10 syllables; the third line has one more than in the evening mantra because rātriyā has three syllables.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Psychology and inner life

The morning prayer does not carry the night forward: what was done is named and left with the night. Starting a day by deliberately setting down the previous one is a habit many traditions and many people find useful. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin What would you want to leave behind with the night before starting a day?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Why might the morning mantra make the sun, and the evening one ‘the true’, the place of offering?

A contemplation exercise On waking, take one breath to name anything from yesterday you want to set down before the day begins.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
