Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 34 of 80

Āyātu varadā devī: the invitation of the gāyatrī

After the sipping and sprinkling, the gāyatrī is invited as a boon-giving goddess and mother of the metres, the knowledge of the twilight junctions.

5 Faithful English translation

May the boon-giving goddess come, [to] the imperishable brahman, the measured. [O] gāyatrī, mother of the metres, accept this brahman of mine. Whatever sin [one] does in the day, from that [one] is released in the day; whatever sin [one] does in the night, from that [one] is released in the night. O [goddess] of every colour, great goddess, knowledge of the junctions, Sarasvatī!

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Come, boon-giving goddess, imperishable, the measure of Brahman. Gāyatrī, mother of the metres, accept this sacred word of mine. Whatever wrong is done by day is undone that day; whatever is done by night is undone that night. Goddess of every colour, great goddess, wisdom of the twilights, Sarasvatī!

Key concepts गायत्री gāyatrī सन्ध्या sandhyā, the junction of day and night; the worship performed at it मन्त्र mantra अक्षर akṣara, the imperishable; syllable ब्रह्मन् Brahman देवता deity

7 Explain it to a beginner

After the water has been sipped and sprinkled, the worshipper invites the gāyatrī, the verse at the heart of the daily worship, as if inviting a guest. Here she is a goddess: the giver of boons, the mother of all the Vedic metres, imperishable. She is asked to accept ‘this sacred word of mine’. Two lines then state that a wrong done by day is released within that day, and one done by night within that night. The passage ends by calling her by four names: goddess of every colour, great goddess, the knowledge of the twilights, and Sarasvatī. The old commentator explains ‘the knowledge of the twilights’ as the practice performed at the junctions of night and day, morning and evening. The text describes an invitation; the edition makes no claim about its effects.

An analogy Like greeting a respected teacher at the door before a lesson: the welcome comes first, and the lesson after.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

It asks the boon-giving goddess to come, calls her mother of the metres and asks her to accept ‘this brahman of mine’; it states that a sin done by day is released that day, and one done by night that night; and it addresses her as the goddess of every colour, great goddess, knowledge of the junctions, Sarasvatī.

Traditional Interpretation

Where does it stand in the rite?

The anonymous commentator: after the sprinkling, at all three junctions of the day, this is the mantra that invites the gāyatrī — मार्जनादूर्ध्वं गायत्र्या आवाहनमन्त्रमाह. Bhāskara: आयात्विति गायत्र्यावाहने अनुष्टुप् यजुरन्ता, the anuṣṭubh used in inviting the gāyatrī. The next anuvāka continues the invitation and gives the mantra of breath control; 10.36 gives the farewell.

Traditional Interpretation

Who is the goddess?

For the anonymous commentator she is the deity presiding over the gāyatrī metre (गायत्रीछन्दोभिमानिनी देवता), and she comes ‘to teach us the truth of Brahman’: अस्माकं ब्रह्मतत्त्वं बोधयितुमागच्छत्वित्यर्थः. Bhāskara calls her the shining deity who presides over the metres and the mother, the source, of the uṣṇih and the other metres (छन्दसामुष्णिगादीनां माता योनिभूता).

Traditional Interpretation

What are the lines on sin and release?

The anonymous commentator addresses them to the goddess: ‘O knowledge of the junctions, Sarasvatī, your devotee is released from the sin he does in a day, that same day’ — तदह्नात्तस्मिन्नहनि तेन पापेन प्रतिमुच्यते विमुच्यतां शुद्धो भूयादिति भावः — and calls the ablatives Vedic usages (एते छान्दसाः प्रयोगाः). Bhāskara’s comment passes straight from the invitation to ‘ojo ’si’ and does not gloss these lines, which his recension may not contain.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Witnesses

Two readings are left open on the sources page: the Tirupati e-text has naḥ for me in juṣasva me (Bhāskara’s gloss also reads इदं नो ऽस्मदीयं ब्रह्म), and the Ānandāśrama text has a doubled syllable in yad ahnāt. The Ānandāśrama text’s संमितम् is a spelling difference.

Traditional Interpretation

How does Sāyaṇa read the invitation?

Sāyaṇa places the mantra in all three sandhyās, after the sprinkling, as the invitation of the gāyatrī. He glosses the first words वरदाऽस्मदभीष्टवरप्रदा देवी गायत्री छन्दोभिमानिनी देवता — the boon-giver is the goddess Gāyatrī, presiding deity of the metre, who grants what we desire — and the second half as a request that she teach ‘us’ the Brahman the Vedānta sets out: नोऽस्मानिदं ब्रह्म वेदान्तप्रतिपाद्यं तत्त्वं जुषस्व जोषयतु. His gloss reads ‘us’ (नः), as the Tirupati witness does, where the base reads ‘me’; the edition keeps the base and lists the difference for review.

Where they disagree

No Viśiṣṭādvaita or Dvaita commentary on this text is in the edition’s sources. Bhāskara reads the first lines more concretely, as the gāyatrī verse that is ‘like brahman’ and is to be enjoyed by the goddess; the anonymous commentator, as the goddess coming to teach Brahman.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • मातेदं mātedaṃ → माता + इदम्

    vowel sandhi (guṇa)

    ā + i → e: mātā + idam → māteda(m) (P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः)).

  • यदह्नात्कुरुते yadahnātkurute → यत् + अह्नात् + कुरुते

    final t voiced before a vowel

    yat + ahnāt → yad ahnāt; ahnāt before k keeps its t.

  • यद्रात्रियात्कुरुते yadrātriyātkurute → यत् + रात्रियात् + कुरुते

    final t voiced before a voiced consonant

    yat + rātriyāt → yad rātriyāt (P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते)).

Junctions outside the general rules

  • देवी · अक्षरम् — unjoined hiatus (as printed): devī · akṣaram: the ī of devī is not joined to the following a (classical devy akṣaram); every witness prints the words apart, and the pāda keeps eight syllables.

Morphology

अह्नात्
An ablative where a locative (‘on the day’) is meant; with rātriyāt the anonymous commentator calls these Vedic usages: एते छान्दसाः प्रयोगाः.
प्रतिमुच्यते
A present passive, ‘is released’; the anonymous commentator turns it into a wish, ‘may he be released’ (विमुच्यतां).
गायत्रीम्
An accusative in a passage of nominatives and vocatives. Bhāskara makes it the object of juṣasva (‘enjoy the gāyatrī’, गायत्रीं गायत्रीछन्दस्कं जुषस्व); the anonymous commentator restores a nominative (gāyatrī) in apposition to the goddess.

Anvaya

वरदा देवी अक्षरम् सम्मितम् ब्रह्म [उद्दिश्य] आयातु । [हे] छन्दसाम् माता, मे इदम् ब्रह्म जुषस्व । यत् अह्नात् पापम् कुरुते, तत् अह्नात् प्रतिमुच्यते । यत् रात्रियात् पापम् कुरुते, तत् रात्रियात् प्रतिमुच्यते । [हे] सर्ववर्णे महादेवि सन्ध्याविद्ये सरस्वति ।

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

A third-person wish (āyātu) turns into a second-person request (juṣasva) addressed to the mother of the metres; two parallel relative-correlative sentences (yad … tad) state a release from the day’s and the night’s sins; four vocatives close the address. The cases are loose: gāyatrīm stands in the accusative and akṣaraṃ brahma sammitam has no governing word.

Ambiguities

akṣaraṃ brahma sammitam
The anonymous commentator takes it as the goal of her coming — the imperishable Brahman established by the Upaniṣads (सम्यग्वेदान्तप्रमाणेन निश्चितं ब्रह्म जगत्कारणं परं तत्त्वम्); Bhāskara reads akṣaram with ‘this brahman of ours’ and sammitam as ‘like brahman’ (ब्रह्मसम्मितं ब्रह्मसङ्काशं).
idaṃ brahma
The ‘brahman’ the goddess is asked to accept: for the anonymous commentator the truth taught by the Upaniṣads, which she is to teach; for Bhāskara ‘this brahman of ours’, the recitation, and gāyatrīm its object (see morphology).
juṣasva me / naḥ
The base reads me (‘of mine’); the Tirupati e-text reads naḥ (‘of ours’), and Bhāskara’s gloss also has इदं नो ऽस्मदीयं ब्रह्म. The other witness does not settle it (sources page: review).

Metre

anuṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 16 + 15 + 16 + 18 + 16

Bhāskara calls the invitation an anuṣṭubh ending in a prose formula (आयात्विति गायत्र्यावाहने अनुष्टुप् यजुरन्ता). The five lines count 16, 15, 16, 18 and 16 syllables: the second has 15 (gāyatrīṃ chandasāṃ mātedaṃ / brahma juṣasva me), the fourth 18 because of the trisyllabic rātriyāt (9 + 9).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Learning and apprenticeship

Before the central recitation of the day, the text has the reciter welcome what is about to be recited as a guest and a teacher. Treating a text one has recited for years as something to be received afresh each time is one way of keeping study from becoming routine. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

Further notes

  • Sāyaṇa’s comment on this mantra is his anuvāka 26 in his own numbering (quoted above). An earlier import had filed his breath-control comment under that number, because the source’s next file misprints its colophon; the edition now keys that file by its heading, as 27.

Uncertainties recorded

  • review juṣasva me (base) against naḥ (Tirupati e-text, and Bhāskara’s gloss इदं नो ऽस्मदीयं ब्रह्म); left open on the sources page.
  • uncertain Bhāskara does not gloss the lines on release from the day’s and the night’s sin; his recension may lack them.
  • interpretation Whether akṣaraṃ brahma sammitam names the goal of the goddess’s coming (the anonymous commentator) or qualifies the recited brahman (Bhāskara) is not settled by the text.
