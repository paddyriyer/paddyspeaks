Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 37 of 80

Ghṛṇiḥ sūrya ādityaḥ: the Sun as all things

A prayer to be repeated by one who cannot perform the meditations taught before it: the Sun moves untiring, the rivers flow with what he gives, and all that is — the waters, light, the deathless, the Veda, the worlds and Om — is he.

5 Faithful English translation

The blazing one, Sūrya, Āditya, like light moves, imperishable. They [the rivers] pour honey, that essence. That truly [is] the real: the essence, the waters, light, sap, the deathless, brahman, bhūḥ, bhuvaḥ, suvaḥ, Om.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

The blazing Sun, son of Aditi, travels like light itself, never failing. The rivers pour out sweetness, the water that comes from him. And that is the real: the essence of things, the waters, light, the sap of life, the deathless, the Veda, the three worlds and Om — all of it is the Sun.

Key concepts सूर्य the Sun ओम् Om व्याहृति the sacred calls ब्रह्मन् Brahman अमृत immortality, the deathless रस essence, savour सत्य truth, the real अक्षर akṣara, the imperishable; syllable

7 Explain it to a beginner

This short prayer is for someone who cannot manage the longer meditations the text has just taught. They are told simply to repeat it. It names the Sun three times — the blazing one, the one who brings the world forth, the son of Aditi — and says he travels through the sky like light itself and never fails. The rivers, it says, flow with sweet water, because their water comes from the rain the Sun brings. The last line is a list: the true, the essence of things, the waters, light, the sap of life, the deathless, the Veda, earth, mid-air, heaven, and the sound Om. The old commentator explains the point: the one who repeats it should hold in mind that all of these are the Sun. Two other commentators knew the prayer in a different wording.

An analogy Like a child told that bread comes from the farmer, the rain, the field and the sun at once, and learning to see the sun in every slice.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

It names the sun three times and says he moves like light, imperishable; that sweetness flows, ‘that essence’; and that ‘that’ is the real — the essence, the waters, light, sap, the deathless, brahman, the three worlds and Om.

Traditional Interpretation

For whom is it said?

The anonymous commentator introduces it as a prayer to be repeated, addressed to the Sun, for one unable to perform the meditations taught before: पूर्वोक्तोपासनास्वसमर्थस्याऽऽदित्यदेवताविषयं जप्यं मन्त्रमाह. Sāyaṇa, on the same mantra in his own recension, says the same: उपासनायामसमर्थस्याऽऽदित्यदेवताविषयं जप्यं मन्त्रमाह.

Traditional Interpretation

How does the commentator read the second half?

As a statement that the Sun, as brahman, is everything: उत्तरार्धेनाऽऽदित्यब्रह्मणः सर्वात्मकत्वमुच्यते. Each item of the list is glossed — brahman as the threefold Veda (ब्रह्म त्रयोविद्या), bhūr bhuvaḥ suvaḥ as the three worlds — and the one who repeats the mantra is to hold in mind that all of it is the Sun: एतत्सर्वमादित्य एवेति मनसा विभावयेदिति भावः.

Comparative Vedānta

How do Sāyaṇa and Bhāskara read it?

Both comment on a different wording of the mantra. Their text continues after ghṛṇiḥ sūrya ādityaḥ with ‘they worship’, ‘austerity, truth’, and ‘that is brahman, that is the waters’, where this text has ‘like light moves, imperishable’ and ‘that essence’. Sāyaṇa glosses Āditya as the son of the goddess Aditi (आदित्योऽदितिदेवतायाः पुत्रः). Bhāskara calls the first part an eight-syllable mantra of the Sun ending in Om, famous in the Kāṭhaka (सावित्रोऽष्टाक्षरः प्रणवान्तो मन्त्रः काठके विख्यातवैभवः), whose subject is the supreme brahman favouring the world in the form of the Sun (सवितृरूपेण जगदनुगृह्णानं परं ब्रह्मास्य प्रतिपाद्यम्).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What is the comparison in ‘na prabhā’?

The commentator takes na as a particle of comparison (नकार उपमार्थः): the sun moves like light. He adds that the comparison is in fact impossible, since the sun has no likeness but his own light.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • घृणिस्सूर्य ghṛṇissūrya → घृणिः + सूर्यः

    visarga before a sibilant

    ghṛṇiḥ + sūryaḥ: the visarga becomes s before s (P. 8.3.36 (वा शरि)); the Taittirīya recitation writes the doubled sibilant स्स.

  • सूर्य आदित्यो sūrya ādityo → सूर्यः + आदित्यः

    aḥ before a vowel other than a

    -aḥ loses its visarga before a vowel other than a, and the vowels stay apart (P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य)).

  • आदित्यो न ādityo na → आदित्यः + न

    aḥ before a voiced consonant

    -aḥ becomes -o before a voiced sound (P. 6.1.114 (हशि च)).

  • वात्यक्षरम् vātyakṣaram → वाति + अक्षरम्

    i before a dissimilar vowel

    vāti + akṣaram → vāty akṣaram (P. 6.1.77 (इको यणचि)).

  • तद्रसम् tadrasam → तत् + रसम्

    final t before a voiced sound

    t becomes d before a voiced consonant (P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते)).

  • ज्योती रसो jyotī raso → ज्योतिः + रसः

    r before r

    jyotiḥ (= jyotir) + rasaḥ: the first r drops and the vowel before it is lengthened (P. 8.3.14 (रो रि), P. 6.3.111 (ढ्रलोपे पूर्वस्य दीर्घोऽणः)).

  • रसोऽमृतं raso'mṛtaṃ → रसः + अमृतम्

    aḥ before a

    rasaḥ + amṛtam → raso ’mṛtam (P. 6.1.109 (एङः पदान्तादति)).

  • भुवस्सुवरोम् bhuvassuvarom → भुवः + सुवः + ओम्

    visarga before s; r before a vowel

    bhuvaḥ + suvaḥ → bhuvas suvaḥ (spelled स्स); suvar + om → suvar om, the r of suvar showing before the vowel.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this anuvāka.

Morphology

अक्षरम्
Neuter in form, describing the sun; the anonymous commentator restores the masculine (अक्षरमक्षरः).
न
In the Veda na after a noun can mean ‘like’; the commentator takes it so here (नकार उपमार्थः), so the line compares the sun’s course to the course of light.
रसम्
In the third line it stands in a list of nominatives; it is read as a nominative (‘that essence’), as the commentator’s gloss रसं मधुरादिरसज्ञातम् treats it.

Anvaya

घृणिः सूर्यः आदित्यः प्रभा न अक्षरम् वाति । [सिन्धवः] तत् रसम् मधु क्षरन्ति । तत् वै सत्यम् — रसम् आपः ज्योतिः रसः अमृतम् ब्रह्म भूः भुवः सुवः ओम् ।

ghṛṇiḥ sūryaḥ ādityaḥ prabhā na akṣaram vāti | sindhavaḥ tat rasam madhu kṣaranti | tat vai satyam — rasam āpaḥ jyotiḥ rasaḥ amṛtam brahma bhūḥ bhuvaḥ suvaḥ om |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Three statements. The first: three names of the sun as subject, a comparison (prabhā na) and a verb of motion (vāti) with the predicate akṣaram. The second: a verb in the plural whose subject, the rivers, is supplied by the commentator, with madhu and tad rasam as objects. The third: a nominal sentence identifying ‘that’ with the true, followed by a list of nominatives without a verb, ending in the three vyāhṛtis and Om.

Ambiguities

na
Negation (‘light does not move’) or comparison (‘like light’). The anonymous commentator takes the comparison and says it is an impossible simile: the sun has nothing to be compared with but his own light.
who pours the honey
The verb is plural with no subject. The anonymous commentator supplies the rivers, which carry the water the sun’s rain gives (आदित्यलब्धवृष्ट्युदकमेव नद्यो वहन्ति); Sāyaṇa, on his own text of the mantra, supplies the worshippers, who offer sweet things to the sun.

Metre

prose · syllables counted as written: 14 + 8 + 22

A japa formula in prose (yajus). Bhāskara calls its first part an eight-syllable mantra of the sun ending in Om (सावित्रोऽष्टाक्षरः प्रणवान्तो मन्त्रः): ghṛṇiḥ sūrya ādityaḥ om.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Interdependence

The prayer asks the one who repeats it to see one source behind water, light, food and the words of the Veda. Tracing the things one uses every day back to what makes them possible is a way of seeing how much depends on what is rarely noticed. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Pick one thing you used today. How many steps back does it take to reach the sun?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The commentator calls the simile ‘like light’ impossible, because the sun is compared only with itself. What does it mean to describe something that has no likeness?

A contemplation exercise When you next drink a glass of water, recall the rain and the sun that it came from.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.

Further notes

  • Sāyaṇa (his anuvāka 15) and Bhāskara (his anuvāka 15) comment on this mantra in a different wording; their comments are cited for what they say of the shared opening and of the mantra’s use. The edition’s computed concordance maps this anuvāka to Sāyaṇa 15 and 28 and to no Bhāskara anuvāka: Sāyaṇa 28 is the alternative breath-control mantra, and Bhāskara 15 is the comment that belongs here.
  • The last words, āpo jyotī raso ’mṛtaṃ brahma bhūr bhuvaḥ suvar om, are the ‘head’ (śiras) recited with the gāyatrī in breath control (10.35).

  • variant Sāyaṇa’s and Bhāskara’s recensions read this mantra differently after ghṛṇiḥ sūrya ādityaḥ (‘they worship; austerity, truth … that is brahman, that is the waters’); the edition prints the 80-anuvāka text, whose three witnesses agree in substance.
  • interpretation na prabhā is read as a simile with the anonymous commentator; it could be read as a negation.
