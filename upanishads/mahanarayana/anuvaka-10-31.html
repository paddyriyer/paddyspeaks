Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 31 of 80

Agniś ca mā: the water sipped at the evening worship

At sunset the worshipper asks protection from the wrongs anger brings, names the day’s wrongs of mind, speech and body, and offers them and himself into the light that is the source of immortality.

5 Faithful English translation

May Agni and manyu and the lords of manyu protect me from sins done through manyu. Whatever sin I have done by day — by mind, by speech, by the hands, by the feet, by the belly, by the sexual organ — may the day wipe that out. Whatever wrong [is] in me, this [and] myself I offer into the source of immortality, into the true, into the light. Svāhā.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

May Fire, and anger, and the powers that rule anger guard me from the wrongs that anger brings. Whatever wrong I did today — in thought, in word, with hands or feet, by appetite or desire — may the passing day wipe it away. Whatever wrong remains in me, I offer it, and myself with it, into the source of deathlessness, the true, the light. Svāhā.

Key concepts सन्ध्या sandhyā, the junction of day and night; the worship performed at it अग्नि Agni, fire मनस् mind अमृत immortality, the deathless सत्य truth, the real मन्त्र mantra

7 Explain it to a beginner

This is the prayer for sipping water at the evening worship. It first asks Fire, and anger, and the powers that govern anger, to protect the speaker from the wrongs anger leads to. Then it lists the ways a day goes wrong: in thought, in speech, with the hands, with the feet, through appetite and through desire, and asks that the day which is now ending take those wrongs away with it. Finally the speaker offers whatever wrong remains, and himself along with it, into ‘the source of deathlessness, the true, the light’. The old commentators read that last phrase as the ultimate reality, and say the water sipped is the offering. The prayer describes a review of the day; the edition makes no claim about what reciting it achieves.

An analogy Like closing the day’s accounts at sunset: each kind of entry is named, and the balance is handed over rather than carried into the night.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

Agni, manyu and the lords of manyu are asked to protect the speaker from sins done through manyu. The speaker names the sin done ‘by day’ through mind, speech, hands, feet, belly and sexual organ and asks the day to wipe it out; then offers ‘whatever wrong is in me’, and himself, into the source of immortality, the true, the light, with svāhā.

Traditional Interpretation

When is it recited?

Sāyaṇa: सायंसंध्याकाले जलपानार्थं मन्त्रमाह — the mantra for drinking water at the evening worship; the anonymous commentator says the same. Bhāskara: पापशोधनार्थं सायम् अप आचामति — one sips water in the evening to cleanse sin. The sins named are those ‘of the day’ because the day is ending; the morning mantra (10.32) names those of the night.

Comparative Vedānta

What is manyu, and why Agni?

Sāyaṇa reads manyu as the deity presiding over anger and the ‘lords of manyu’ as the deities that govern it (क्रोधाभिमानी देवः, क्रोधस्वामिनस्तन्नियामका देवाः). Bhāskara first takes manyu as the thinking inner faculty, the cause of all action and restraint (मननशीलमन्तःकरणं सर्वप्रवृत्तिनिवृत्तिहेतुः), and only then reports anger as another view (क्रोध इत्येके); its lords are the breaths and the senses. Agni is named, for Bhāskara, as lord of the night that is beginning (उपक्रान्ताया रात्रेरधिपतिर् अग्निः).

Comparative Vedānta

Protection from which sins?

Sāyaṇa reads the protection as removal of sins already committed (पापिनं मां तत्पापविनाशनेन पालयन्तु). Bhāskara reads it as guarding against sins still to come, as a house is guarded so that thieves do not enter, and paraphrases: यथा अहं पापं न करिष्यामि तथा कुर्वन्तु. On his reading the mantra looks forward to the night as well as back over the day.

Traditional Interpretation

The six instruments of wrong

Sāyaṇa gives an example for each: harm thought of others is the sin of the mind (परहिंसादिचिन्तनं मानसं पापम्), unkind and untrue speech that of speech, eating what is forbidden that of the belly (अभोज्यभोजनमुदरकृतम्), and so on. Bhāskara gives parallel examples and adds that ‘whatever wrong is in me’ covers what was done in earlier births or by the other senses (प्राचीनजन्मसञ्चितं).

Traditional Interpretation

Into what is the offering made?

Sāyaṇa: the speaker offers the collected sin, and himself as the subtle body, into the deathless cause of the world, the real, the self-luminous: मरणरहिते जगत्कारणे सत्ये बाधरहिते ज्योतिषि स्वयंप्रकाशे वस्तुनि जुहोमि, ‘by this offering I burn it all’ (होमेन तत्सर्वं भस्मी). Bhāskara reads ‘the source of immortality’ as Brahman (अमृतयोनौ अमृतत्वकारणे ब्रह्मणि) and the light as the self that is fire, so that the sin, losing its nature as sin, becomes a cause of immortality (पापतां हित्वा अमृतत्वस्यैव कारणं स्यादिति भावः).

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • अग्निश्च agniśca → अग्निः + च

    visarga before c

    A visarga becomes ś before c: agniḥ + ca → agniś ca (P. 8.3.34 (विसर्जनीयस्य सः), with ś by assimilation).

  • यदह्ना yadahnā → यत् + अह्ना

    final t voiced before a vowel

    yat + ahnā → yad ahnā.

  • पापमकार्षम् pāpamakārṣam → पापम् + अकार्षम्

    final m before a vowel

    pāpam akārṣam: the m is joined to the vowel.

  • अहस्तदवलुम्पतु ahastadavalumpatu → अहः + तत् + अवलुम्पतु

    visarga before t; final t voiced

    The visarga of ahaḥ becomes s before t (ahas tad); tat before a vowel becomes tad (tad avalumpatu).

  • यत्किञ्च yatkiñca → यत् + किम् + च

    final m before c

    kim + ca → kiñ ca: m becomes the nasal of the following stop (P. 8.4.59 (वा पदान्तस्य)).

  • इदमहं माममृतयोनौ idamahaṃ māmamṛtayonau → इदम् + अहम् + माम् + अमृतयोनौ

    final m before a vowel

    idam aham, mām amṛtayonau: m joined to the following vowel; aham before m is written with anusvāra.

Morphology

शिश्ना
Instrumental of śiśna with the Vedic ending -ā for -ena; Bhāskara names the rule: सुपां सुलुक् इत्याकारः.
अकार्षम्
First person singular of the s-aorist of √kṛ: ‘I have done’.
मन्युः
manyu means anger in classical usage but has a wider older sense of spirited mind; the commentators divide on which is meant here (see commentary).

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

A wish in the third person imperative (rakṣantām), with three subjects joined by ca and an ablative of what is guarded against. Then a relative clause (yad … akārṣam) with six instrumentals of the means, resumed by tad and a second imperative (avalumpatu). The last sentence turns to the first person: a relative clause (yat kiṃ ca … mayi) and a main clause idam ahaṃ māṃ … juhomi with three locatives of where the offering is made.

Ambiguities

manyu
Anger (Sāyaṇa, the anonymous commentator, and Bhāskara’s alternative) or the thinking inner faculty (Bhāskara’s first reading).
what rakṣantām protects from
Sāyaṇa reads it as protection by removing sins already done (पापिनं मां तत्पापविनाशनेन पालयन्तु); Bhāskara as protection from sins not yet done, ‘as one guards a house against thieves’ (see commentary).
idam … mām
Sāyaṇa takes idam as the collected sin and mām as the speaker in his subtle body (लिङ्गशरीररूपम्); Bhāskara allows that mām may stand for ‘my sin’, the two being treated as one.

Metre

mixed · syllables counted as written: 18 + 6 + 8 + 8 + 8 + 8 + 8 + 10 + 10

Bhāskara names the metres: the first two lines a three-pāda gāyatrī; the six-word list a five-pāda paṅkti of eight-syllable pādas; idam aham … juhomi a virāj or two-pāda gāyatrī. He notes seven pauses inside the mantra and that ‘akārṣam manasā’ is to be recited together. As printed, the nine lines count 18, 6, 8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 10 and 10 syllables (svāhā included in the last).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Self-mastery

The prayer singles out anger first, then reviews the day by the channels through which harm is done — thought, speech, hands, feet, appetite, desire. It is a structured evening review: name what happened, by what means, and let the day close on it. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Of the six channels the prayer lists, which one most often carries your mistakes?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Bhāskara reads ‘protect me from sins done through manyu’ as looking forward, Sāyaṇa as looking back. Which reading makes the evening mantra more useful, and why?

A contemplation exercise Tonight, recall the day once through each channel — thought, speech, action — and then deliberately set it down.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.

Further notes

  • Bhāskara’s reading of the instrumental śiśnā by the rule supāṃ suluk and his analysis of the metres come from his comment on this anuvāka (his anuvāka 24).

Uncertainties recorded

  • interpretation manyu as anger or as the thinking mind, and protection from past or from future sins, are read differently by Sāyaṇa and Bhāskara; the text does not decide.
  • review The edition’s computed map sends Bhāskara’s anuvāka 24 to 10.32; by its content (agniś ca, sāyam) it comments on this anuvāka, and is cited here explicitly.
