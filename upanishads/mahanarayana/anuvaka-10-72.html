Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 72 of 80

Vāṅ ma āsan: the faculties in their places

After the meal the speaker names each faculty in its seat — speech, breath, sight, hearing, strength, vigour — declares the body unharmed, and offers homage asking not to be harmed.

5 Faithful English translation

Speech [is] in my mouth; breath in the nostrils; sight in the eyes; hearing in the ears; strength in the arms; vigour in the thighs; all the limbs [are] unharmed, the body. With my body, homage be to you; do not harm me.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Speech is in my mouth, breath in my nostrils, sight in my eyes, hearing in my ears, strength in my arms, vigour in my thighs; all my limbs and my body are unharmed. With my whole body, homage to you: do not harm me.

Key concepts इन्द्रिय indriya, a sense प्राण breath, life-force अन्न food

7 Explain it to a beginner

After the meal, the speaker goes through the body and names each faculty in its place: speech in the mouth, breath in the nose, sight in the eyes, hearing in the ears, strength in the arms, vigour in the thighs. Then: all my limbs and my body are unharmed. The last line offers homage to the one addressed and asks not to be harmed. The anonymous commentator reads it as said after eating one’s fill: before the meal hunger had weakened the faculties; now each is back in its place, and the speaker thanks the Lord and asks for protection for himself and his family. He adds that it should be said daily with attention to its meaning. The edition reports this as his reading.

An analogy Like a pilot running down a checklist before take-off: each instrument named in its place, and then the whole craft declared sound.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

It places each faculty in its seat — speech, breath, sight, hearing, strength, vigour — says that all the limbs and the body are unharmed, offers homage ‘with my body’, and asks not to be harmed.

Traditional Interpretation

When is it said, according to the commentator?

The anonymous commentator: after the formula that calls the Lord to mind at the end of the meal, the eater recites, from experience, a mantra declaring all his limbs well (भोक्तुः सर्वेषामङ्गानां स्वस्थताप्रतिपादकं मन्त्रमनुभवपूर्वकं पठति); before the meal they were weak from hunger (प्राग्भोजनात्क्षुदादिवशेन विकलान्यभवन्नित्यर्थः). He closes: मन्त्रोऽयं भोजनोत्तरं प्रत्यहं परमेश्वरकृतोपकृतिस्मृतिसिद्धये ब्राह्मणैरर्थानुसंधानपूर्वकं पठनीय इति रहस्यम् — to be recited daily after the meal, with attention to its meaning, to remember the Lord’s kindness. The restriction to brāhmaṇas is his.

Traditional Interpretation

What is asked in the last line?

For the anonymous commentator the speaker asks protection for himself and his household for as long as he lives: यावन्मरणं सहकुटुम्बं मां मा हि ँसीर्मा पीडय. His text writes हिँसीः with the candrabindu sign; the base writes हिंसीः.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What forms are these?

Several are Vedic: āsan, a locative without ending; bāhuvoḥ, ūruvoḥ and tanuvā with -uv- for -v-; ariṣṭā with -ā for the neuter plural -āni; hiṃsīḥ, an aorist injunctive used with the prohibitive mā. The commentator glosses bāhuvoḥ and tanuvā with their classical forms (बाहुवोर्बाह्वोः, तनुवा तन्वा).

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • वाङ्म vāṅma → वाक् + मे

    final k before a nasal

    vāk + me → vāṅ me: a final stop becomes the nasal of its class before a nasal.

  • म आसन् ma āsan → मे + आसन्

    e before a vowel

    me + āsan → ma āsan: e becomes ay before a vowel and the y is dropped, leaving the vowels apart.

  • अक्ष्योश्चक्षुः akṣyoścakṣuḥ → अक्ष्योः + चक्षुः

    visarga before c

    akṣyoḥ + cakṣuḥ → akṣyoś cakṣuḥ: the visarga becomes ś before c.

  • बाहुवोर्बलम् bāhuvorbalam → बाहुवोः + बलम्

    visarga after o before a voiced consonant

    bāhuvoḥ + balam → bāhuvor balam: after o the visarga becomes r.

  • विश्वान्यङ्गानि viśvānyaṅgāni → विश्वानि + अङ्गानि

    i before a vowel

    viśvāni + aṅgāni → viśvāny aṅgāni: i becomes y before a dissimilar vowel.

  • नमस्ते namaste → नमः + ते

    visarga before t

    namaḥ + te → namas te: the visarga becomes s before t.

Junctions outside the general rules

  • ते · अस्तु — vedic-prakṛtibhāva: te astu: the e of te is not joined to the a of astu (classically te ’stu). The base, the Ānandāśrama pariśiṣṭa text and the Tirupati e-text all keep the vowels apart.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

Morphology

आसन्
āsan- ‘mouth’ in the Vedic locative without an ending, parallel to the locative duals that follow. The anonymous commentator supplies आस्ये, ‘in the mouth’, and then uses āsan as the verb ‘were’ (imperfect 3rd plural of √as): the faculties were restored after the meal.
बाहुवोः
Vedic locative dual of bāhu with -uvoḥ for -voḥ; ūruvoḥ is formed the same way.
अरिष्टा
Neuter plural nominative with the Vedic ending -ā for -āni, agreeing with aṅgāni; a-riṣṭa, ‘not harmed’, from √riṣ.
तनुवा
Vedic instrumental singular of tanū with -uvā for -vā.
हिंसीः
Aorist injunctive (s-aorist without the augment) of √hiṃs, used with the prohibitive mā: ‘do not harm’.

Syntax

Six nominal sentences of one pattern, a faculty in the nominative and its seat in the locative, with ‘is’ understood; a seventh with the predicate ariṣṭā; then a wish with astu and a prohibition with mā … hiṃsīḥ.

Ambiguities

āsan
A locative, ‘in the mouth’, matching the seats that follow; or, as the anonymous commentator also uses it, the verb ‘were’ (लब्धस्वास्थ्यान्यासन्), the faculties having been restored by the meal.
tanūḥ and tanuvā
The anonymous commentator takes tanūḥ as the gross body and tanuvā (‘with the body’) as the subtle body (लिङ्गशरीरेण).
to whom
The text does not name the ‘you’ addressed; the anonymous commentator addresses the Lord (हे भगवन्).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Embodiment

The formula is a body scan in words: each faculty named in its place, the whole declared sound, before the day goes on. Naming what is working, rather than only what is not, is a simple practice of attention. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Which of the faculties named here do you notice only when it is not working?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The commentator reads the formula as said from experience, after the meal. How does saying ‘speech is in my mouth’ differ from merely having speech?

A contemplation exercise Once today, name to yourself, one by one, five things your body is doing well.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
