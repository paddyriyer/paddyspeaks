Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 51 of 80

Mā chido mṛtyo: a prayer to Death to spare strength, children and life

Death, addressed as the fierce one who watches all people, is asked not to cut the worshipper off, not to strike, not to take away his strength, his children or his life; the worshippers offer him oblations.

5 Faithful English translation

Do not cut [me] off, O Death; do not strike; do not tear away my strength; do not carry [it] off. Do not harm my offspring, [my] life, O fierce one. We would worship you, the watcher of people, with oblation.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Death, do not cut me off, do not strike me down, do not tear away my strength or carry it off. Fierce one, do not harm my children or my life. With offerings we would serve you, who watch over all people.

7 Explain it to a beginner

This short prayer speaks directly to Death. It asks five things in a row, each with the word for "do not": do not cut me off, do not strike, do not tear away my strength, do not carry it off, do not harm my children or my life. Death is called "fierce" and also the one who "watches over all people". The old commentator explains that this watching is a looking at what each person has done, good and bad. The prayer ends by saying that the worshippers serve Death with offerings. It belongs to a group of short prayers and offering formulas near the end of the Upaniṣad that ask for protection and for release from wrongdoing. The edition reports what the words say and what the commentator says they are for; it makes no claim about what reciting them does.

An analogy Like a petition placed before a stern magistrate: it names, one by one, each thing it asks him not to take.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

It addresses Death twice, as mṛtyu and as ugra, ‘fierce’, and forbids five actions: cutting off, striking, tearing away strength, carrying it off, and harming offspring and life. It closes by declaring that the speakers serve him with oblation, calling him nṛcakṣas, ‘the watcher of people’.

Traditional Interpretation

How does the commentator read it?

The anonymous commentator calls Death हे मृत्यो हे उग्र क्रूर — O Death, O fierce, cruel one. He supplies what is to be spared: our performance of good works (अस्मत्सत्कर्मानुष्ठानं मा वधीर्मा हिंसय), bodily strength (मे मम शारीरं बलं मा विवृहो मा पीडय) and our means to the next world (अस्मदीयपरलोकसाधनं मा प्रमोषीर्मा चोरव). He explains nṛcakṣas as one who looks at living beings to examine their merit and sin: नृन्प्राणिनश्चष्टे पुण्यपापरीक्षायै पश्यतीति नृचक्षास्तं नृचक्षसम्. The oblation is the reason Death is asked to spare them: तदर्थ त्वा त्वां हविषा, ‘for that, [we serve] you with oblation’.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Why are all the verbs aorists without the augment?

Sanskrit forbids with mā and an aorist, P. 3.3.175 (माङि लुङ्), and after mā the aorist takes no augment, P. 6.4.74 (न माङ्योगे): so chidaḥ, not acchidaḥ; vadhīḥ, not avadhīḥ. Five such forms in two lines give the prayer its insistent rhythm.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • छिदो मृत्यो chido mṛtyo → छिदः + मृत्यो

    visarga after a before a voiced consonant

    aḥ before a voiced consonant becomes o: chidaḥ mṛtyo → chido mṛtyo.

  • वधीर्मा vadhīrmā → वधीः + मा

    visarga after ī before a voiced consonant

    A visarga after a vowel other than a or ā becomes r before a voiced sound: vadhīḥ mā → vadhīr mā.

  • बलं विवृहो balaṃ vivṛho → बलम् + विवृहः

    final m before a consonant; visarga after a

    balam becomes balaṃ before v; vivṛhaḥ becomes vivṛho before m.

  • रीरिष आयुरुग्र rīriṣa āyurugra → रीरिषः + आयुः + उग्र

    visarga after a before a vowel; visarga after u before a vowel

    rīriṣaḥ before ā loses its visarga and stands in hiatus (rīriṣa āyuḥ); āyuḥ before u becomes āyur (āyur ugra).

  • नृचक्षसं त्वा nṛcakṣasaṃ tvā → नृचक्षसम् + त्वा

    final m before a consonant

    nṛcakṣasam becomes nṛcakṣasaṃ before t.

    upapada-tatpuruṣa · नृ + चक्षस्

    Vigraha नॄन् चष्टे इति

    one who watches people

Morphology

छिदः
An aorist without the augment (an injunctive), as always after the prohibitive mā: P. 6.4.74 (न माङ्योगे). The same holds for vadhīḥ, vivṛhaḥ, pramoṣīḥ and rīriṣaḥ: five prohibitions, all aorist injunctives.
रीरिषः
Reduplicated aorist of √riṣ, ‘harm’: the reduplication rī- with long vowel is the Vedic causative-type aorist.
त्वा
The enclitic accusative of the second person, as in the Veda generally.

Anvaya

हे मृत्यो मा छिदः मा वधीः मे बलम् मा विवृहः मा प्रमोषीः । हे उग्र मे प्रजाम् आयुः [च] मा रीरिषः । नृचक्षसम् त्वा हविषा विधेम ।

he mṛtyo mā chidaḥ mā vadhīḥ me balam mā vivṛhaḥ mā pramoṣīḥ | he ugra me prajām āyuḥ ca mā rīriṣaḥ | nṛcakṣasam tvā haviṣā vidhema |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Five prohibitions, each mā with an aorist injunctive (chidaḥ, vadhīḥ, vivṛhaḥ, pramoṣīḥ, rīriṣaḥ), addressed to Death in the vocative (mṛtyo, ugra). The objects are given only for the third and fifth (balam; prajām and āyuḥ). The verse closes with a first person plural optative (vidhema) whose object is tvā, qualified by nṛcakṣasam, with the instrumental haviṣā.

Ambiguities

what is not to be cut off or struck
The text gives no object for chidaḥ and vadhīḥ. The anonymous commentator supplies them: for vadhīḥ अस्मत्सत्कर्मानुष्ठानं (our performance of good works), and for pramoṣīḥ अस्मदीयपरलोकसाधनं (our means to the next world). Read without him, the objects may simply be the speaker himself.

Metre

triṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 19 + 22

As printed the first half has 19 syllables (mā chido mṛtyo mā vadhīr mā me balaṃ vivṛho mā pramoṣīḥ) and the second 22 (11 + 11). The first half falls three syllables short of the triṣṭubh norm of 22; the edition records the count and does not emend.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Mortality

The prayer does not argue with death or deny it; it names what a person most fears to lose — strength, children, life — and asks that these be spared for now. Naming what is at stake is itself a way of facing it. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Of the five things the prayer asks Death not to take, which would you have put first?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The commentator reads ‘do not strike’ as ‘do not destroy our good works’ and ‘do not carry off’ as ‘do not take our means to the next world’. What does that reading add to the plain words?

A contemplation exercise Write down three things you would not want to lose, and one thing you do each day that protects them.
