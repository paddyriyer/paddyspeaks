Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 64 of 80

Tilāḥ kṛṣṇāḥ: sesame for cleansing

Sesame, black and white, is offered to cleanse the speaker of wrong, to quiet a list of grave sins named one by one, and to ask Agni for the goods of a good life.

5 Faithful English translation

Sesame seeds [are] black, sesame seeds [are] white, sesame seeds [are] gentle, obedient. May the sesame seeds purify me — the sin, whatever wrong [is] in me. Svāhā. A thief’s food, the first śrāddha, the slayer of a brāhmaṇa, the violator of the teacher’s bed, theft of cattle, drinking liquor, killing an embryo — may the sesame seeds bring [these] to rest. Svāhā. Prosperity and fortune and nourishments and good name and freedom from debt, piety, many children, faith and intelligence, wisdom — O Jātavedas, may [you] grant [these]. Svāhā.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Black sesame, white sesame, gentle and rightly ours: may this sesame cleanse me of whatever wrong is in me. Svāhā. Food taken from a thief, food eaten at a first death-rite, the killing of a brāhmaṇa, the violation of the teacher’s bed, cattle-theft, drinking liquor, the killing of an embryo — may the sesame bring all this to rest. Svāhā. Prosperity and fortune, nourishment and a good name, freedom from debt, piety, many children, faith, understanding and wisdom — Agni, knower of all, grant them. Svāhā.

7 Explain it to a beginner

This formula goes with an offering of sesame seeds, black and white, into the fire. The speaker asks the sesame to cleanse whatever wrong is in him. Then come seven grave wrongs named one after another — eating food from a thief, eating at a first death-rite, killing a brāhmaṇa, violating the teacher’s bed, stealing cattle, drinking liquor, killing an unborn child — and a request that the sesame bring them to rest. Last, Agni the fire god is asked for prosperity, a good name, freedom from debt, children, faith and wisdom. The old commentator explains that seeds by themselves have no power over sin; they act by the command of the Supreme. The edition reports these readings and makes no claim about what the rite achieves.

An analogy Like handing over a written list of debts to be settled, item by item, before asking for anything new.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

Sesame, black and white, gentle and ‘obedient’, is asked to purify the speaker of whatever wrong is in him; seven grave sins are named and the sesame is asked to bring them to rest; and Agni Jātavedas is asked to grant prosperity, fortune, nourishment, good name, freedom from debt, piety, many children, faith, intelligence and wisdom. Each formula ends with svāhā.

Traditional Interpretation

How does the commentator read the sesame?

The seeds are of both colours and harmless, केचित्कृष्णाः केचिच्छ्वेता उभयविधाः सर्वेऽपि सौम्या रोगाद्युपद्रवाप्रदाः; ‘obedient’ means rightly acquired, not stolen: स्वधर्मोपार्जितं द्रव्यं दत्त्वा विक्रीयाऽऽनीता. They purify only by the Supreme’s command: अचेतनानां तिलानां पापनाशनसामर्थ्यं त्वदाज्ञयेति भावः.

Traditional Interpretation

How does he gloss the sins?

He explains only what needs it: the first śrāddha is eating at the ekoddiṣṭa and similar rites, नवश्राद्धमेकोद्दिष्टाद्यन्नभोजनम्, and bhrūṇa has three senses, भ्रूणो गर्भः शिशुर्वीरो वा; the rest he calls clear. The request is that the sesame bring about the end of these sins: एतन्मन्त्रोक्तानां पापानां शान्तिं विनाशं शमयन्तु कुर्वन्तु.

Comparative Vedānta

Is this list known elsewhere?

The Chāndogya names four men who fall — the thief of gold, the drinker of liquor, the violator of the teacher’s bed and the slayer of a brāhmaṇa — and a fifth who keeps company with them. Three of those four stand in this list; the Chāndogya’s thief of gold corresponds here to cattle-theft and a thief’s food.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • कृष्णास्तिलाः kṛṣṇāstilāḥ → कृष्णाः + तिलाः

    visarga before t

    kṛṣṇāḥ + tilāḥ → kṛṣṇās tilāḥ: the visarga becomes s before t.

  • सौम्या वशानुगाः saumyā vaśānugāḥ → सौम्याः + वशानुगाः

    -āḥ before a voiced consonant

    saumyāḥ + vaśānugāḥ → saumyā vaśānugāḥ: after ā the visarga drops before a voiced sound.

  • यत्किञ्चिद्दुरितं yatkiñcidduritaṃ → यत् + किम् + चित् + दुरितम्

    m before c; t before d

    kim + cit → kiñcit (m as the palatal nasal); cit + duritam → cid duritam.

  • चोरस्यान्नं corasyānnaṃ → चोरस्य + अन्नम्

    a + a → ā

    corasya + annam → corasyānnam.

  • तिला शान्तिं tilā śāntiṃ → तिलाः + शान्तिम्

    visarga lost before ś (as printed)

    tilāḥ + śāntim is printed tilā śāntiṃ, without the visarga; classical sandhi keeps it (tilāḥ śāntim or tilāś śāntim). See sandhi_exceptions.

  • श्रीश्च śrīśca → श्रीः + च

    visarga before c

    śrīḥ + ca → śrīś ca.

Morphology

मे
The enclitic me can be genitive or accusative; the commentator takes it as the object, ‘me’: मे मां तिलाः पुनन्तु.
ब्रह्महा
Nominative of brahmahan, ‘slayer of a brāhmaṇa’; the list mixes the sins (theft, drinking) with the sinners named for them.
श्रीः
In this list śrīḥ, lakṣmīḥ and prajñā have the form of the nominative where the parallel list in 10.63 has accusatives (śriyam, lakṣmim); with sandadātu they are the things to be granted.
पुष्टीः
Accusative plural of puṣṭi, ‘kinds of nourishment’; 10.63 has the singular puṣṭim.
जातवेदः
Vocative of jātavedas, Agni ‘who knows all born things’, with a 3rd-person imperative (sandadātu): the commentator renders it with the polite bhavān, ‘may you grant’.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

Three offering formulas closed by svāhā. The first is a series of nominal sentences (tilāḥ kṛṣṇāḥ …) followed by an imperative (punantu) with the enclitic me and a relative clause (yat kiṃcid duritaṃ mayi). The second is a list of seven sins in the nominative followed by tilāḥ śāntiṃ śamayantu, ‘may the sesame bring [them] to rest’. The third repeats the list of goods from 10.63, now addressed to Agni with the vocative jātavedaḥ and the imperative sandadātu.

Ambiguities

the list of sins
The text lists the sins and the sinners together (corasyānnam, navaśrāddham, brahmahā, gurutalpagaḥ, gosteyam, surāpānam, bhrūṇahatyā). The commentator reads them as the sins whose ‘end’ the sesame should bring about.
bhrūṇa
An embryo, a child, or (in older usage) a learned brāhmaṇa; the commentator gives three senses: भ्रूणो गर्भः शिशुर्वीरो वा.
how sesame purifies
The commentator answers that inert seeds can remove sin only by the Supreme’s command: अचेतनानां तिलानां पापनाशनसामर्थ्यं त्वदाज्ञयेति भावः. The text does not say.

Metre

mixed · syllables counted as written: 16 + 34 + 35 + 8 + 17

Anuṣṭubh-like half-verses with svāhā and prose: the five printed lines count 16, 34, 35, 8 and 17 syllables (the second and third each contain two half-verses and a svāhā).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Accountability

The anuvāka does not leave wrongdoing vague: it names seven specific acts before asking for anything. Naming wrongs one by one, rather than in general, is a recognisable step in taking responsibility. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

A question to begin Why might a rite name each wrong separately instead of saying ‘all my wrongs’?
