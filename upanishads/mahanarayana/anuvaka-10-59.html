Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 59 of 80

Devakṛtasyainasaḥ: you are the removal of every wrong

Eleven oblations, each declaring that the offering is the removal of a kind of wrong: wrongs towards the gods, men and ancestors, done by oneself, by another, by us; by day and by night; asleep and awake; knowingly and unknowingly; and the gravest wrong.

5 Faithful English translation

You are the removal of sin done towards the gods, svāhā. You are the removal of sin done towards men, svāhā. You are the removal of sin done towards the ancestors, svāhā. You are the removal of sin done by oneself, svāhā. You are the removal of sin done by another, svāhā. You are the removal of sin done by us, svāhā. Whatever sin we have done by day and by night, you are the removal of that, svāhā. Whatever sin we have done sleeping and waking, you are the removal of that, svāhā. Whatever sin we have done in deep sleep and waking, you are the removal of that, svāhā. Whatever sin we have done knowing and not knowing, you are the removal of that, svāhā. You are the removal of the sin [beyond] sin, svāhā.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

You take away the wrongs done towards the gods, towards people, towards the ancestors; the wrongs done by me, by others, by us. Whatever we did wrong by day or by night, asleep or awake, in deep sleep or awake, knowing or not knowing — you take it away. You take away even the gravest wrong. Svāhā.

7 Explain it to a beginner

This anuvāka is a list of eleven short offering formulas, each ending with "svāhā", the call with which an offering is poured into the fire. Each says to the offering, or to the fire god Agni: "you are the removal of wrongdoing". Then it names the wrongdoing, as completely as language allows: wrongs towards the gods, towards people, towards one’s ancestors; wrongs done by oneself, by others, by all of us; by day and by night; asleep and awake; in deep sleep and awake; knowingly and unknowingly; and finally "the wrong beyond wrong", which the old commentator reads as the gravest wrongs. The list works like a net, leaving no kind of fault out. The edition reports what the formulas say and how the commentator reads them; it makes no claim about what the offering achieves.

An analogy Like a checklist read aloud at the end of a long journey: every compartment named, so that nothing is left behind unexamined.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

Eleven times it says ‘you are the removal of sin … svāhā’, naming sins done towards gods, men and ancestors, by oneself, by another and by us, by day and by night, sleeping and waking, in deep sleep and waking, knowingly and unknowingly, and the sin beyond sin.

Traditional Interpretation

How does the commentator explain the rite?

The anonymous commentator: since these eleven formulas name no deity to receive the oblation, Agni is to be taken as the deity, as the chief of all the gods: अग्निर्देवतात्वेनाङ्गीकर्तव्यः, सर्वदेवताप्रधानत्वादग्नेः. ‘You are the removal’ is said to the ghee, हे आज्य त्वमेनसः पापस्यावयजनं निवारकमसि, or to Agni. Of the pairs, he says waking is named twice because wrongdoing is likeliest when awake, जाग्रदवस्थायां पापबाहुल्यस्य संभावितत्वान्मन्त्रद्वये जाग्रच्छब्दप्रयोगः, while some subtler wrong is to be understood even in deep sleep, सुषुप्त्यवस्थायामपि किंचित्सूक्ष्मतरं पापं द्रष्टव्यम्. Knowing and not knowing means wrongs done with and without knowledge, ज्ञानाज्ञानपूर्वकृतस्येत्यर्थः.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Why ‘done towards the gods’?

The compounds in -kṛta can be read with the first member as the one done to or for (‘done towards the gods’) or as the doer (‘done by the gods’). The commentator takes the first sense — a defect in the rites of the gods, दैवेषु कर्मस्वङ्गवैकल्यादिरूपस्येत्यर्थः — and for ātmakṛta, anyakṛta and asmatkṛta the second, the doer: oneself, another, us.

  • एनस एनसोऽवयजनमसि enasa enaso'vayajanamasi → एनसः + एनसः + अवयजनम् + असि

    visarga after a before e; visarga after a before a

    The first enasaḥ before e drops its visarga and stands in hiatus (enasa enasaḥ); the second becomes enaso before a, which is elided.

Morphology

अवयजनम्
An action noun from √yaj with ava, ‘removal by sacrifice’; with asi, ‘you are the removal’. The commentator takes ‘you’ as the ghee or as Agni (see commentary).
चकृम
Perfect, first person plural, of √kṛ: ‘we have done’.
सुषुप्तः
A singular among plurals; the commentator reads it as plural by interchange of number, सुषुप्तो व्यत्ययेन सुषुप्ताः.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

Eleven parallel formulas, each with the copula asi, ‘you are’, and the predicate avayajanam governing a genitive. In six the genitive is enasaḥ qualified by a compound in -kṛta; in four a relative clause (yat … enaś cakṛma) is resumed by tasya; the last doubles the genitive (enasa enasaḥ). Each closes with svāhā.

Ambiguities

who ‘you’ are
The formulas address an unnamed ‘you’. The commentator gives two readings: the ghee, हे आज्य त्वमेनसः पापस्यावयजनं निवारकमसि, or Agni, the presiding deity of these formulas, अग्निर्देवतात्वेनाङ्गीकर्तव्यः.
enasa enasaḥ
The commentator gives two readings: ‘the sin greater than sin’, the gravest sins, यदेनो महापातकादि तस्य; or, taking enasaḥ as enasvinaḥ with the suffix dropped, एनसो विनो लोपश्छान्दसः, the sin that comes from contact with a sinful person.
devakṛta and the others
devakṛta may be ‘done towards the gods’ (a fault in the rites for them) or ‘done by the gods’; the commentator gives the first, दैवेषु कर्मस्वङ्गवैकल्यादिरूपस्येत्यर्थः, and as an alternative ‘done by the senses’. The same ambiguity runs through manuṣyakṛta and pitṛkṛta.

Yajus formulas, not verse; eleven formulas each closed by svāhā.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Responsibility

The list names wrongs done knowingly and unknowingly, by oneself and by those one is bound to, awake and even asleep. It treats responsibility as wider than intention. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Which pair in the list — day and night, asleep and awake, knowing and not knowing — makes you think hardest?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Why might a list of wrongs include those done ‘not knowing’ and ‘by another’?

A contemplation exercise At the end of today, review it once by these pairs and set down what you find.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
