Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 36 of 80

Uttame śikhare: the farewell of the gāyatrī

After the recitation the goddess invited in 10.34 is given leave to return to her highest peak, and asked, as she goes, for life, wealth and the lustre of learning.

5 Faithful English translation

On the highest peak, risen on the earth, on the mountain’s summit — given leave by the brāhmaṇas, go, goddess, as you please. Praised by me, the boon-giver, the mother of the Vedas, impelling as the wind, the twice-born — having given me, [who am] on earth, life, wealth, the lustre of sacred learning, [go to] the world of Brahmā that has arisen.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

To the highest peak, on the summit of the mountain that rises from the earth — go, goddess, with the leave of those who worship you, wherever you are at ease. Praised by me, boon-giver, mother of the Vedas, who moves all beings as the wind does — give me life, wealth and the lustre of learning, and go to the world of Brahmā.

Key concepts गायत्री gāyatrī देवता deity वेद Veda लोक world, realm मन्त्र mantra

7 Explain it to a beginner

After the gāyatrī has been recited, the worshipper bids her farewell, as one sees off a guest who was invited in. The first verse tells the goddess she may go, with the leave of those who worship her, to the highest peak on the summit of the mountain. The second praises her again — giver of boons, mother of the Vedas, the one who moves all beings as the wind moves — and asks that she give the speaker life, wealth and the lustre of learning before she goes to the world of Brahmā. One old commentator says the mountain is Meru and records that some place her home in the sun, some in Brahmā’s world. The passage closes the rite that began with her invitation two sections earlier.

An analogy Like seeing a teacher to the gate after a lesson, thanking them and asking for their good wishes as they go.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

It sends the goddess, with the leave of the brāhmaṇas, to the highest peak on the mountain’s summit, as she pleases; and, praising her as boon-giver, mother of the Vedas, impelling as the wind, twice-born, asks that she give the speaker life, wealth and the lustre of learning and go to the world of Brahmā.

Traditional Interpretation

Where does it stand in the rite?

After the japa of the gāyatrī: जपादूर्ध्वं गायत्रीविसर्जनमन्त्रमाह (the anonymous commentator); Sāyaṇa: जपादूर्ध्वं गायत्रीदेवताया विसर्जनमन्त्रमाह. Bhāskara: the anuṣṭubh is applied to the sending away of the sāvitrī (सावित्र्या उत्सर्जने विनियुक्ता). It answers the invitation of 10.34–10.35.

Comparative Vedānta

Where does the goddess go?

The anonymous commentator: to the highest peak of the mountain called Meru (पर्वतो मेरुनामकस्तस्य मूर्धन्युपरिभागे यदुत्तमं शिखरमस्ति); in the second verse, to the world of Brahmā, Sarasvatī’s lord; he reports that some take the sun’s disk or Brahmā’s world as her dwelling. Bhāskara reads the place loosely — ‘on the earth or on a mountain top’, any high and holy place (उत्तमे उद्गततमे पुण्यतमे शिखरे) — and says she goes for the brāhmaṇas’ sake and waits there to come back to them (पुनर् अपि ब्राह्मण-समीपम् आगन्तुं प्रतीक्षमाणैव उत्तमे स्थाने तिष्ठेति).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

How are the hard forms explained?

The anonymous commentator explains three Vedic irregularities: prajātum for prajātam (प्रयोगश्छान्दसः), stutaḥ for stutā (व्यत्ययेन स्तुता), and pavane as ‘the wind’, in an implied comparison: as breath moves every creature, so she impels them (यथा प्राणवायुश्चतुर्विधं प्राणिज्ञातं प्रेरयतीतस्ततो गमनागमनादिकं कारयति तथेति).

Traditional Interpretation

Who are the ‘twice-born’ here?

The anonymous commentator gives two readings of dvijātā: worshipped by the three twice-born classes, or born in two places, the sun’s disk and Brahmā’s world (द्वयोः सूर्यमण्डलब्रह्मलोकयोर्जाता प्रादुर्भूता). The first reflects the social order the commentary assumes; the second keeps to the passage’s own images.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Witnesses

The Tirupati e-text reads शिखरे देवि जाते (classed as a witness error) and दत्वा; the Ānandāśrama text reads मह्मं and ब्रहलोकम्. Bhāskara’s gloss has devī and no jāte, so the Tirupati reading may reflect a different recitation rather than a slip.

Where they disagree

No Viśiṣṭādvaita or Dvaita commentary on this text is in the edition’s sources. Bhāskara and the anonymous commentator differ on where the goddess goes — any high, holy place (Bhāskara) or Meru and Brahmā’s world (the anonymous commentator) — and on why: to wait for the brāhmaṇas’ next call, or to return to her own dwelling.

Morphology

स्तुतः
Masculine in form where the goddess is meant; the anonymous commentator reads it as stutā by the licence of exchange (व्यत्ययेन स्तुता).
प्रजातुम्
The commentator explains it as a Vedic form for prajātam, qualifying brahmalokam (‘the world of Brahmā, arisen [from the supreme self]’); the verb of going is supplied.
पवने
A locative in form; the commentator reads a nominative of comparison, ‘[like] the wind’ (पवने व्यत्ययेन पवनः).

Anvaya

देवि, ब्राह्मणेभ्यः अभ्यनुज्ञाता [त्वम्] भूम्याम् पर्वतमूर्धनि जाते उत्तमे शिखरे यथासुखम् गच्छ । मया स्तुतः वरदा वेदमाता पवने [इव] प्रचोदयन्ती द्विजाता [देवी] पृथिव्याम् मह्यम् आयुः द्रविणम् ब्रह्मवर्चसम् दत्त्वा प्रजातुम् ब्रह्मलोकम् [गच्छतु] ।

devi, brāhmaṇebhyaḥ abhyanujñātā tvam bhūmyām parvatamūrdhani jāte uttame śikhare yathāsukham gaccha | mayā stutaḥ varadā vedamātā pavane iva pracodayantī dvijātā devī pṛthivyām mahyam āyuḥ draviṇam brahmavarcasam dattvā prajātum brahmalokam gacchatu |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Two sentences. The first: an imperative (gaccha) with a vocative, a participle of leave (abhyanujñātā), an adverb (yathāsukham) and locatives of destination. The second, in the third person: a subject with appositions (varadā, vedamātā, pracodayantī, dvijātā), an absolutive clause (āyuḥ … dattvā) and an accusative of destination (prajātuṃ brahmalokam) whose verb of going is supplied.

Ambiguities

where she goes
The first verse names a mountain peak (Meru, for the anonymous commentator); the second, the world of Brahmā. The commentator reports that ‘some’ hold either Brahmā’s world or the sun’s disk to be her abode (ब्रह्मलोक आदित्यमण्डलं चोभयमपि गायत्र्या अवस्थानं विकल्पेनान्यतरदिति केचिदाचक्षते).
dvijātā
Worshipped by the twice-born, or born in two places, the sun’s disk and Brahmā’s world — the anonymous commentator gives both.
jāte / devī
The base and the Ānandāśrama text read jāte after śikhare; the Tirupati e-text reads devi jāte; Bhāskara’s comment glosses devī with no jāte (देवी देवनशीला क्रीडापरा). The collation classes the Tirupati reading as a witness error; with Bhāskara it may be a recension difference (see flags).

Metre

mixed · syllables counted as written: 16 + 16 + 22 + 24

An anuṣṭubh (Bhāskara: उत्तम इत्याद्य् अनुष्टुप्) followed by a triṣṭubh: the four lines count 16, 16, 22 and 24 syllables; the last line has two more than the triṣṭubh norm (āyuḥ pṛthivyāṃ draviṇaṃ brahmavarcasam has 13).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Purpose and meaning

The rite ends with a farewell, not an abrupt stop: what was invited is thanked and sent on, and the speaker asks for what the day will need — life, means and learning. Closing a practice deliberately, rather than simply leaving it, marks it off from the rest of the day. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

Uncertainties recorded

  • review jāte (base, Ānandāśrama) against devi jāte (Tirupati e-text); Bhāskara’s gloss reads devī without jāte. The collation classes the Tirupati reading as a witness error; with Bhāskara’s support it may be a recension difference, to be checked against the printed sources.
  • review The Ānandāśrama text’s मह्मं for मह्यं and the Tirupati e-text’s दत्वा for दत्त्वा are left open on the sources page.
  • interpretation Where the goddess goes (Meru, the sun’s disk, Brahmā’s world, any high place) is read differently by the commentators.
