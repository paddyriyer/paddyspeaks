Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 70 of 80

Śraddhāyāṃ prāṇe niviśya: the food offered, now nourishing

After the meal, the food that was offered into each of the five breaths is addressed again: in faith it has been offered; let it now nourish the breath it was offered to.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

In faith, entering into the out-breath, I have offered the nectar: nourish the out-breath with this food. In faith, entering into the down-breath, I have offered the nectar: nourish the down-breath with this food. So too for the diffused breath, the up-breath and the breath that equalises: in faith I have offered the nectar; nourish each with this food.

Key concepts प्राण breath, life-force श्रद्धा faith, trust अन्न food अमृत immortality, the deathless

7 Explain it to a beginner

The previous anuvāka gives the words for offering the first mouthfuls of a meal to the five vital breaths, as if the body were a sacrificial fire. This one comes after the meal. For each breath it says: in faith, the nectar has been offered into it; now let the food nourish it. The five breaths are the out-breath, the down-breath, the breath spread through the body, the up-breath and the breath that evens out what is eaten. The commentators call this the formula for addressing the food after eating, and they explain the ‘nectar’ as the food itself, sweet and offered with care. The edition reports the rite as the commentators describe it and makes no claim about effects on health.

An analogy Like a farmer who has sown in five fields and goes back to each to say: grow from what was sown here.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

For each of the five breaths it says that the immortal has been offered in faith, ‘having settled in’ that breath, and asks that the breath be nourished with the food.

Traditional Interpretation

When is it said?

Sāyaṇa: भुक्तस्यानुमन्त्रणे मन्त्रमाह — the mantra for addressing what has been eaten. Bhāskara calls it the mantra of address after the offering and says its words are mostly explained already (गतप्रायः).

Traditional Interpretation

What is the ‘immortal’ that was offered?

For Sāyaṇa it is the food, a sweet oblation, imperishable: अविनश्वरं स्वादुभूतमिदं हविर्मया हुतम्; the anonymous commentator says the same. Faith, for the anonymous commentator, is great trust in the Vedic rite: वैदिककर्मणि विश्वासातिशयः श्रद्धा.

Comparative Vedānta

Nourish whom?

The anonymous commentator asks the deity of the breath to make the breath that moves in mouth and nose grow: त्वं प्राणं मुखनासिकासंचारिणं वायुं हुतेनान्नेनाऽऽप्यायस्व वर्धय. Bhāskara: तस्मात् पाणं मदीयां प्राणवृत्तिम् अनेन अन्नेन आप्यायस्व वर्धय हे प्राणात्मन्, and he notes the verb’s causative sense: अन्तर्भावितण्यर्थः प्यायतिर्द्रष्टव्यः.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • निविश्यामृतं niviśyāmṛtaṃ → निविश्य + अमृतम्

    a + a

    niviśya + amṛtam → niviśyāmṛtam: two short a fuse into ā.

  • प्राणमन्नेनाप्यायस्व prāṇamannenāpyāyasva → प्राणम् + अन्नेन + आप्यायस्व

    final m before a vowel; a + ā

    prāṇam + annena: the m is joined to the vowel (prāṇam annena); annena + āpyāyasva → annenāpyāyasva.

  • श्रद्धायामपाने śraddhāyāmapāne → श्रद्धायाम् + अपाने

    final m before a vowel

    śraddhāyām + apāne: the m is joined to the following vowel.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this anuvāka.

Morphology

निविश्य
Absolutive of √viś with ni-, ‘having entered, settled’. With hutam it gives ‘[by me], having settled in the breath, [it is] offered’; the agent is supplied by the commentators (मया). The formula of 10.69 has the finite form niviṣṭo … juhomi, ‘settled in …, I offer’.
आप्यायस्व
Middle imperative of √pyāy with ā-. Bhāskara notes it carries a causative sense within it: ‘make swell, nourish’ (see commentary).
अमृतम्
Neuter nominative, subject of hutam: ‘the immortal [thing]’ — for the commentators, the food offered into the breaths.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

Five pairs of the same shape, one for each breath. The first sentence of each is nominal: an absolutive phrase (śraddhāyāṃ prāṇe niviśya) and amṛtaṃ hutam with ‘is’ understood. The second is an imperative (āpyāyasva) with the breath in the accusative and the instrumental annena.

Ambiguities

who is addressed
The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa address the deity presiding over each breath (हे प्राणाभिमानिनि देवि, हे प्राणाभिमानिदेव); Bhāskara addresses the breath itself as a self (हे प्राणात्मन्).
the repeated śraddhāyām
The base repeats śraddhāyām in every pair. The text printed with Sāyaṇa has it only before prāṇe, and Bhāskara says it is to be carried over to the others (श्रद्धायामित्य् अपानादिष्व् अनुषज्यते).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Gratitude

The formula returns, after the meal, to what was given at its start and asks that it now do its work. Closing a meal with a moment of recognition, rather than simply ending it, is a habit shared by many traditions. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Why might the same words be said five times, once for each breath?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The previous anuvāka says ‘I offer’; this one says ‘it has been offered’. What changes between the beginning and the end of the meal?

A contemplation exercise At the end of a meal, take a moment before standing to register that it is over.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.

Further notes

  • Sāyaṇa’s comment on this anuvāka is his 36; the edition’s Sāyaṇa file holds it under 38, together with his 37 and 38, which the source prints on the same pages. His mūla there ends with ब्रह्मणि म आत्माऽमृतत्वाय, which the base has in 10.69, not here. Bhāskara’s comment is his anuvāka 36.
