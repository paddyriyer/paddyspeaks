Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 41 of 80

Medhā devī: the hymn to understanding

Two verses to Medhā, the goddess of understanding and memory: may she come, and by her favour may we speak of the highest; by her favour one becomes a seer, a Brahmā, prosperous.

5 Faithful English translation

Medhā, the goddess, favouring, has come to us — all-pervading, auspicious, well-disposed. Favoured by you, driving away ill-spoken words, may we speak the great in the assembly, with good sons. Favoured by you one becomes a seer, goddess; by you a Brahmā; one to whom fortune has come, also by you. Favoured by you one gains varied wealth. Such as you are, favour us, Medhā, like the lord of wealth.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

May Medhā, the goddess, come to us gladly — she who reaches everywhere, auspicious and kind. Favoured by you, may we put away idle talk and speak of the highest at the rite, with good sons and pupils around us. By your favour, goddess, one becomes a seer; by your favour, a Brahmā; by your favour, prosperous; by your favour one finds wealth of every kind. Favour us, Medhā, as a lord of riches favours the poor.

Key concepts मेधा medhā, retentive intelligence ऋषि seer देवता deity ब्रह्मन् Brahman विद्या vidyā

7 Explain it to a beginner

This is the opening of the hymn to Medhā — the goddess of understanding, and especially of the power to take in a teaching and keep it. The first verse asks her to come gladly, she who reaches everywhere, and says that with her favour we will stop speaking idle words and speak instead of the highest truth at the rite. The second verse lists what her favour brings: one becomes a seer who sees beyond the senses, one becomes a Brahmā, one becomes prosperous and finds wealth of every kind. It ends by asking her to favour us as a rich patron favours someone in need. The old commentators explain why the hymn stands here: the knowledge the Upaniṣad teaches has to be held in the mind without a break, and that depends on medhā.

An analogy Like asking for a good memory before learning a long poem by heart.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

It asks Medhā to come, and says that by her favour the speakers will drive away ill-spoken words and speak of the great at the rite; that by her favour one becomes a seer, a Brahmā, prosperous, and gains wealth; and it asks her to favour the speakers.

Traditional Interpretation

Why is medhā prayed for here?

The anonymous commentator: the three trisuparṇa removed the sins that obstruct knowledge; now, since keeping up the knowledge that the self is one with the Lord depends on medhā, the deity of medhā is invoked: इत्थं प्रतिबन्धनिवृत्त्युपायमुक्त्वा जीवात्मन ईश्वरेणाभेदज्ञानं यदस्ति तस्य ज्ञानस्य नैरन्तर्येण साधनं मेधाधीनमतो मेधाभिमानिनीं देवतां प्रार्थयितुमेकामृचमाह. Sāyaṇa gives the same reason in nearly the same words.

Traditional Interpretation

What is medhā?

Both the anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa: the power to retain a text and its meaning, ग्रन्थतदर्थयोर्धारणशक्तिर्मेधा.

Traditional Interpretation

How do the commentators rank the sins of the trisuparṇa?

Introducing this hymn, the anonymous commentator ranks the three sins the trisuparṇa remove: the killing of a brāhmaṇa is the gravest of the five great sins, the killing of an embryo graver, and the killing of a hero graver still; repeating the trisuparṇa for life removes even these: ईदृशानामपि निवर्तको यावज्जीवं त्रिसुपर्णजपः. These are his claims about the text, reported as such.

Traditional Interpretation

What are the ‘ill-spoken words’?

The anonymous commentator: words of no use to the aims of life, outside the Veda, which the favoured speakers drive away, so that, the sacrifice done and their minds purified, they speak of the supreme brahman.

Where they disagree

No Vedānta-school commentary on this text is in the edition’s sources. The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa connect medhā with the knowledge that the self is one with the Lord, an Advaita reading they give in passing; Bhāskara’s comments on this hymn (his anuvākas 39–42) are missing from the edition’s copy, and Raṅgarāmānuja’s commentary on the Mahānārāyaṇa is not among the sources.

Morphology

आगतश्रीः
The base prints brahmā gataśrīr, with a space; the letters are the same as brahmā āgataśrīḥ joined. The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa read āgataśrīḥ, ‘to whom fortune has come’; the Ānandāśrama text prints ब्रह्माऽऽगतश्रीः with the avagraha that marks the merged ā.
आगात्
A root aorist of √gā with ā; the commentators read it as a wish (‘may she come’).
द्रविणः
The anonymous commentator reads draviṇo na as ‘like the lord of wealth’; Sāyaṇa glosses ‘with wealth’ (द्रविणेन धनेन जुषस्व), as if draviṇena, which is the reading of the Tirupati e-text (see flags).

Syntax

Two verses. The first: a third-person statement or wish (āgāt) with the goddess as subject and four qualifying nominatives; then a first-person plural optative (vadema) with participles describing the speakers. The second: four short clauses with tvayā juṣṭaḥ (‘favoured by you’) and a predicate, the verb (bhavati) said once and understood; then an imperative (juṣasva) with a vocative and a comparison (draviṇo na).

Ambiguities

brahmā gataśrīr
‘A Brahmā, to whom fortune has come’ (āgataśrīḥ, the commentators) or ‘a Brahmā who has gone to fortune’ (gataśrīḥ, as printed with a space).
draviṇo na
‘Like the lord of wealth’ (the anonymous commentator) or ‘with wealth’ (Sāyaṇa’s gloss, matching the reading draviṇena).
what medhā is
The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa: the power to retain a text and its meaning, needed for the unbroken practice of the knowledge that the self is one with the Lord (जीवात्मन ईश्वरेणाभेदज्ञानं यदस्ति तस्य ज्ञानस्य नैरन्तर्येण साधनं मेधाधीनम्).

The hymn treats the ability to keep what one has learned as a gift to be asked for, not only a skill. Whatever one makes of the prayer, it marks how much depends on retaining a teaching, and not only on hearing it. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin What is something you learned once and are glad you remembered?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The hymn asks to ‘drive away ill-spoken words’ in order to speak of the highest. What talk would you have to drop to speak of what matters most to you?

A contemplation exercise After reading something worth keeping, close the page and say back to yourself what it said.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.

Further notes

  • Sāyaṇa comments on this hymn as his anuvāka 39; the edition’s concordance maps it correctly. Bhāskara’s anuvākas 39–42, which would hold his comment, are not in the edition’s copy of his work.

Uncertainties recorded

  • review At the end of the hymn the Tirupati e-text reads draviṇena and omits medhe (corrections.json); the base and the Ānandāśrama text read draviṇo na medhe. Sāyaṇa’s gloss द्रविणेन धनेन जुषस्व matches the Tirupati reading; the base is kept.
  • interpretation brahmā gataśrīr is read as brahmā āgataśrīḥ with the commentators.
