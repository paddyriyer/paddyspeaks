Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 25 of 80

Kad rudrāya: a hymn for Rudra in the heart

A Ṛgvedic verse resolving to speak the most comforting praise to Rudra, the wise and bountiful one in the heart, closed by the refrain ‘all this is Rudra’.

5 Faithful English translation

A praiseworthy [hymn] to Rudra, the wise, the most bountiful, the strong, may we speak — most beneficent, to the heart. For all this is Rudra; to him, to Rudra, let there be homage.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Let us speak a hymn worth the praise to Rudra, the wise, the most bountiful, the strong — the most comforting words, to him who dwells in the heart. For all this is Rudra: homage to Rudra.

Key concepts मन्त्र mantra

7 Explain it to a beginner

This verse comes from the oldest Vedic hymns, the Ṛgveda, and is set here after the passage that called all things Rudra. Its speakers resolve to offer Rudra the best words they can: a hymn worth the praise, and the most comforting. They name him wise, bountiful and strong, and they speak to him ‘in the heart’, which the commentators understand as Rudra dwelling within. The commentators differ over small words. The first word, kad, looks like the question ‘what?’, but all of them read it as ‘praiseworthy’; another word means either ‘strong’ or ‘worthy of praise’. The refrain from the preceding passage closes it: all this is Rudra, homage to Rudra. The verse says what its speakers mean to do; it does not claim what reciting it achieves.

An analogy Like choosing the gentlest words for a friend who is already close: the effort is in finding words fit for someone who is near.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

The speakers resolve to speak a praiseworthy and most beneficent word to Rudra, the wise, the most bountiful, the strong, to the heart; and they repeat that all is Rudra, with homage.

Traditional Interpretation

How is it used?

Sāyaṇa calls it the second mantra to Rudra, रुद्रदेवताकं द्वितीयं मन्त्रमाह, of the three anuvākas he groups as the Tvaritarudra (see 10.24 and 10.22). Bhāskara: its use is as before, पूर्ववद्विनियोगः.

Traditional Interpretation

How do the commentators read the epithets?

The anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa take kat as praise, from √katth: कच्छब्दः प्रशंसामाह; mīḍhuṣṭama as the one who pours out what is desired, कामप्रदायेत्यर्थः; and hṛde as Rudra in the heart. Bhāskara takes kat with the hymn, ‘a hymn worth praising’, and hṛde as ‘to him who stands in the heart’, हृदे हृदिस्थिताय.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The Ṛgvedic source

The first two lines are a Ṛgveda verse (RV 1.43.1), recited here with the Taittirīya spelling ḍh where the Ṛgveda text used by this edition writes ḷh. The refrain of 10.24 is added after it.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • कद्रुद्राय kadrudrāya → कत् + रुद्राय

    final t before a voiced consonant

    A final t becomes d before a voiced sound (P. 8.4.53 (झलां जश् झशि)): kat + rudrāya → kad rudrāya.

  • ह्येष रुद्रस् hyeṣa rudras → हि + एषः + रुद्रः

    i before a vowel; eṣaḥ before a consonant

    hi + eṣaḥ → hy eṣaḥ (P. 6.1.77 (इको यणचि)); eṣaḥ loses its visarga before a consonant (P. 6.1.132 (एतत्तदोः सुलोपोऽकोरनञ्समासे हलि)).

Junctions outside the general rules

  • नमः · अस्तु — vedic-prakṛtibhāva: namo · astu: the o is kept before a and the a is not elided, as in Vedic recitation inside a pāda (P. 6.1.115 (प्रकृत्याऽन्तःपादमव्यपरे)); all witnesses print namo astu.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this anuvāka.

Morphology

वोचेम
An aorist optative of √vac with the precative sense, ‘may we speak’. Bhāskara explains the form by two sūtras: P. 3.1.86 (लिङ्याशिष्यङ्) and P. 7.4.20 (वच उम्).
मीढुष्टमाय
The superlative suffix tama (P. 5.3.55 (अतिशायने तमबिष्ठनौ)) on mīḍhuṣ-, the weak stem of mīḍhvas, ‘bountiful’, from √mih, ‘to pour’; the commentators all take it so (“मिह सेचने” इति धातुः).
कत्
In form kad is the interrogative ‘what?’. All three commentators instead take it as a word of praise: the anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa derive it from √katth, ‘to praise’; Bhāskara reports that some call it a particle meaning ‘excellent’, प्रशस्तवाची निपात इति केचित्.

Anvaya

प्रचेतसे मीढुष्टमाय तव्यसे रुद्राय हृदे शन्तमम् कत् वोचेम । सर्वः हि एषः रुद्रः तस्मै रुद्राय नमः अस्तु

pracetase mīḍhuṣṭamāya tavyase rudrāya hṛde śantamam kat vocema | sarvaḥ hi eṣaḥ rudraḥ tasmai rudrāya namaḥ astu

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

One wish with the verb vocema (‘may we speak’) at the head of the second line: its object is kat … śaṃtamam (‘a praiseworthy, most beneficent [word]’), its recipients the datives rudrāya, pracetase, mīḍhuṣṭamāya, tavyase and hṛde. The refrain of 10.24, sarvo hy eṣa rudraḥ …, follows.

Ambiguities

kat
Grammatically the interrogative ‘what [shall we speak]?’; the commentators read it as ‘praiseworthy’, the anonymous commentator as praise of Rudra himself (कद्रुदः प्रशस्तो रुद्रस्तस्मै), Bhāskara as qualifying the hymn.
tavyase
From tavas, ‘strong’ (Bhāskara), or stavya, ‘praiseworthy’, with the s dropped (the anonymous commentator and Sāyaṇa).
hṛde
‘To the heart’: for the commentators, to Rudra as dwelling in the heart. One could also take it as ‘[words] for the heart’, comforting to it; the commentators do not.

Metre

mixed · syllables counted as written: 16 + 8 + 15

Bhāskara: कद्रुद्रायेति गायत्री यजुरन्ता — a gāyatrī ending in a yajus. The checker counts 16, 8 and 15 syllables: the gāyatrī is 8 + 8 + 8 over the first two lines, and the prose refrain (15) follows.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Speech and care

The verse treats choosing words as part of reverence: not any praise, but the most comforting, spoken to the one close at hand. The care taken over what is said, and to whom, is a measure of regard that applies far beyond prayer. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin The verse asks for the ‘most comforting’ words. When did words last comfort you, and why did they?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The commentators read kad, a word that looks like ‘what?’, as ‘praiseworthy’. How would the verse sound if it began with a question?

A contemplation exercise Before speaking to someone today, pause long enough to choose the kindest accurate word.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
