Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 68 of 80

Om tad brahma: you are the sacrifice, you are all

Six short declarations, ‘Om, that is Brahman … the wind … the self …’, then the one that moves hidden in all beings is addressed as the sacrifice, the gods, the waters, light and the vyāhṛtis.

5 Faithful English translation

Om, that [is] Brahman. Om, that [is] the wind. Om, that [is] the self. Om, that [is] the true. Om, that [is] all. Om, that [is] the homage of the vast. Within it moves, in beings, in the cave, in all forms. You [are] the sacrifice, you the vaṣaṭ, you Indra, you Rudra, you Viṣṇu, you Brahman, you Prajāpati. You [are] that, the waters, the waters, light, essence, the immortal, Brahman, bhūḥ, bhuvaḥ, suvaḥ, Om.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Om — that is Brahman; that is the wind; that is the self; that is the true; that is everything; that is the homage of the Vast. It moves within all beings, in the secret place of the heart, in every form. You are the sacrifice and the call that offers it; you are Indra, Rudra, Viṣṇu, Brahmā and Prajāpati. You are that; you are the waters, light, essence, the immortal, Brahman — earth, mid-air, heaven: Om.

Key concepts ओम् Om ब्रह्मन् Brahman वायु breath, air आत्मन् self, Self सत्य truth, the real गुहा the cave

7 Explain it to a beginner

This passage is a string of identifications. First, six times: ‘Om — that is Brahman’, ‘that is the wind’, ‘that is the self’, ‘that is the true’, ‘that is everything’, and ‘that is the homage of the Vast’. Then it speaks of the one who moves hidden inside every being, in the secret place of the heart. Then it turns to address that one directly: you are the sacrifice, you are the gods Indra, Rudra, Viṣṇu, Brahmā and Prajāpati; you are the waters, light, essence, the immortal, the three worlds, Om. The commentators give it different uses: the anonymous one says it is repeated to remove sin; Sāyaṇa uses it in breath-control and in worship after the gāyatrī is bid farewell. The edition reports these uses as theirs.

An analogy Like a list of the many names one person goes by — at home, at work, among friends — read out until it is plain that they are all one person.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

Six times ‘Om, that is …’ (Brahman, wind, self, true, all, the homage of the vast); then that it moves within beings, in the cave, in all forms; then, to ‘you’: the sacrifice, the vaṣaṭ, Indra, Rudra, Viṣṇu, Brahmā, Prajāpati, the waters, light, essence, the immortal, Brahman, the three vyāhṛtis and Om.

Comparative Vedānta

What is it used for?

The anonymous commentator: a mantra that removes sin by repetition alone (जपमात्रेण पापक्षयार्थोऽयं मन्त्रः). Sāyaṇa treats ‘om tad brahma’ as one of three alternative mantras for breath-control, to be chosen according to one’s capacity (प्राणायामे शक्तितारतम्यमनुसृत्य विकल्पितं मन्त्रत्रयमुक्तम्), and ‘om antaś carati’ as the worship of Brahman after the gāyatrī is bid farewell (गायत्रीदेव्या विसर्जनादूर्ध्वं तत्त्वानुस्मरणभूतस्य ब्रह्मण उपस्थानमन्त्रमाह). Bhāskara: the first is another alternative in which Brahman as Om is praised (अयमपि विकल्प एव । प्रणवात्मकं ब्रह्म स्तूयते), the second is used in the meditation on Om (प्रणवोपासने विनियुज्यते).

Traditional Interpretation

Where does it move?

The anonymous commentator: in the cave, within the lotus of the heart (गुहायामन्तर्हृदयपुण्डरीकमध्ये चरति वर्तते), in beings with bodies of every kind. Bhāskara: in the space of the heart, sounding as Om (गुहायां हृदयावकाशे चरति प्रणवात्मना ध्वनति). Sāyaṇa: in the intellect (गुहायां बुद्धावन्तर्मध्य).

Traditional Interpretation

Why first ‘that’, then ‘you’?

The anonymous commentator: having first stated, at a distance, that Brahman is everything, the text now shows the same of the supreme self directly (एवं परोक्षेण सर्वात्मकत्वमुक्त्वेदानीमपरोक्षेण परमात्मनः सर्वात्मकत्वं दर्शयति).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

How do the witnesses differ?

The Ānandāśrama pariśiṣṭa text puts ‘om tat satyam’ first; the texts commented on by Sāyaṇa and Bhāskara do not have it at all. The mūla printed with Sāyaṇa reads Om before antaś carati, as the Tirupati e-text does; the base does not. The pariśiṣṭa text prints the middle of line 8 inside its commentary.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • तद्ब्रह्म tadbrahma → तत् + ब्रह्म

    final t before a voiced consonant

    tat + brahma → tad brahma: t is voiced before a voiced sound.

  • तत्पुरोर्नमः tatpurornamaḥ → तत् + पुरोः + नमः

    visarga after o before a voiced consonant

    puroḥ + namaḥ → puror namaḥ.

  • अन्तश्चरति antaścarati → अन्तः + चरति

    visarga before c

    antaḥ + carati → antaś carati.

  • यज्ञस्त्वं yajñastvaṃ → यज्ञः + त्वम्

    visarga before t

    yajñaḥ + tvam → yajñas tvam; and so through the line.

  • तदाप आपो ज्योती रसोऽमृतं tadāpa āpo jyotī raso'mṛtaṃ → तत् + आपः + आपः + ज्योतिः + रसः + अमृतम्

    several junctions

    tat āpaḥ → tad āpaḥ; āpaḥ āpaḥ → āpa āpaḥ (visarga dropped after ā before a vowel); āpaḥ jyotiḥ → āpo jyotiḥ; jyotiḥ rasaḥ → jyotī rasaḥ (visarga dropped before r, the vowel lengthened); rasaḥ amṛtam → raso ’mṛtam.

  • भूर्भुवस्सुवरोम् bhūrbhuvassuvarom → भूः + भुवः + सुवः + ओम्

    visarga as r and ss

    bhūḥ bhuvaḥ → bhūr bhuvaḥ; bhuvaḥ suvaḥ → bhuvas suvaḥ (the recitation’s ss); suvaḥ om → suvar om (suvar has an r stem).

Morphology

पुरोः
Genitive singular of puru, ‘much, vast’; Bhāskara notes its accent (पुरुशब्द आद्युदात्तः).
ब्रह्म
In the series of deities (line 8) the base reads brahma; the commentators explain Brahmā, the creator, and the text printed with Sāyaṇa reads ब्रह्मा.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

Six nominal sentences of one shape, om tat X, with ‘is’ understood; a sentence in the third person (antaś carati …) with three locatives; and a litany of second-person nominal sentences, tvaṃ X, ending in the vyāhṛtis and Om.

Ambiguities

puror namaḥ
The anonymous commentator: that is the cause of the vast universe, and homage to it. Sāyaṇa reads puraḥ as the bodies, gross, subtle and causal (पुरः पुराणि स्थूलसूक्ष्मकारणशरीराणि), and homage to Brahman. Bhāskara gives two readings: the namaḥ is the name, the word, of the mighty world, or the bowing of the great god (पुरोर्महतो देवस्य यन्नमः प्रह्वीभावः).
brahma or brahmā
In line 8 the base has brahma; the commentators explain Brahmā, the creator, as the series of deities suggests.
one mantra or several
In the 80-anuvāka text this is one anuvāka; in the recensions of Sāyaṇa and Bhāskara ‘om tad brahma’ and ‘om antaś carati’ are separate anuvākas with separate uses (see commentary).

Metre

mixed · syllables counted as written: 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 6 + 16 + 25 + 20

The six om tat lines are prose (4, 4, 4, 4, 4 and 6 syllables). antaś carati … viśvamūrtiṣu is a half-verse of 16; the litany has 25 and the last line 20. Bhāskara names the passage from om antaḥ an āstārapaṅkti (ओमन्त इत्य् अस्तारपङ्क्तिः).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Unity

The passage keeps naming different things — wind, self, truth, gods, waters, light — and says of each ‘that is it’. Looking for the one thread in very different things is a habit of thought as much as a belief. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Why might a text say ‘that is’ six times before it says ‘you are’?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The commentators use the same words for repetition, breath-control and meditation. What does that suggest about how the text was used?

A contemplation exercise Choose two things that seem unrelated in your day and look for one thing they share.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
