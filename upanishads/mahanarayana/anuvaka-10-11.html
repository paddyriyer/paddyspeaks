Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 11 of 80

Yathā vṛkṣasya: the flowering tree and the sword over the pit

Two images: good deeds spread like the scent of a tree in flower; wrongdoing is a sword’s edge over a pit, to be avoided with great care.

5 Faithful English translation

As the fragrance of a tree in full flower wafts from afar, so the fragrance of a meritorious deed wafts from afar. As one might walk along a sword’s edge laid across a pit, [thinking] “if I press, if I press [my foot] I shall be disabled, or I shall fall into the pit” — so should one guard oneself from the deathless [untruth].

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Good deeds, like a flowering tree, send their fragrance far. But wrongdoing is like walking a sword laid over a pit: press down and the blade cuts, step off and you fall. With that same care one should hold oneself back from untruth.

Key concepts कर्मन् action, ritual work सत्य truth, the real अमृत immortality, the deathless

7 Explain it to a beginner

Two pictures. First, a tree in full bloom sends its scent a long way; so do good deeds — the commentators say their good name spreads, even to heaven. Second, imagine walking along the edge of a sword laid across a deep pit. The walker thinks: if I press down, the blade will cut my foot and I will stagger; if I step aside, I will fall into the pit. Either way there is harm. So, the text says, one should hold oneself back. From what is unclear in the printed word: Sāyaṇa and Bhāskara read “from untruth”, while the anonymous commentator reads it as guarding oneself for the sake of release. The commentators explain: done in the open, wrongdoing brings blame here; done in secret, it brings suffering hereafter. The record reports these readings.

An analogy Like a tightrope over a canyon: the rope cuts if you lean on it, the canyon waits if you step off; the only safety is not to start across.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

As the scent of a flowering tree wafts from afar, so does that of a meritorious deed. As one who would walk along a sword’s edge laid over a pit thinks “if I press I shall be disabled, or I shall fall into the pit”, so should one guard oneself — from amṛta as printed, from untruth as the commentators read.

Traditional Interpretation

What is the point of the two images?

Sāyaṇa: the passage praises prescribed meritorious action as a means to knowledge and blames forbidden action as an obstacle to it — अथ विहितानुष्ठानरूपं पुण्यं ज्ञानसाधनतया प्रशंसति। निषिद्धचरणं ज्ञानप्रतिबन्धकतया निन्दति. The fragrance is good repute, which reaches even heaven (सत्कीर्तिः सुगन्धसमाना दूराद्वाति मनुष्यलोकात्स्वर्गे गच्छति).

Traditional Interpretation

How is the sword-walk read?

Sāyaṇa describes a performer who walks a blade over a pit to entertain a king, and reasons as he does: यद्यहं पापं प्रकटं कुर्यां तदानीमिह लोके निन्दितो भविष्यामि तत्परिहारायाप्रकटं कुर्यां तदाऽपि नरके पतिष्यामीति — open wrongdoing brings blame here, hidden wrongdoing brings hell. The anonymous commentator draws the same lesson and concludes ‘so I will do no wrong, in public or in secret’ (तस्माद्बही रहसि च न पापं करिष्य इत्युपरम्यताम्).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Amṛta or anṛta?

All three witnesses read अमृतात्. The anonymous commentator keeps it: one seeking release should steady the mind for the sake of the deathless and shrink from sin (अमृतान्मोक्षप्राप्तिहेतोरात्मानमन्तःकरणं स्थिरीकृत्य पापाज्जुगुप्सेत्). Sāyaṇa glosses पुरुषोऽनृतात्पापादात्मानं जुगुप्सेद्धिङ्मां, and Bhāskara explains the letter as a Vedic change, concluding सर्वथा अनृताद् आत्मानं परिहर्तुं यतेतेति. The base is kept; both readings are given.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • दूराद्गन्धो वात्येवं dūrādgandho vātyevaṃ → दूरात् + गन्धः + वाति + एवम्

    t before a voiced consonant; aḥ before a voiced consonant; i before a dissimilar vowel

    dūrāt → dūrād before g; gandhaḥ → gandho before v; vāti + evam → vāty evam.

  • यथाऽसिधारां yathā'sidhārāṃ → यथा + असिधाराम्

    ā + a

    yathā + asidhārām → yathāsidhārām; the print marks the merged a with avagraha (यथाऽसि).

  • कर्तेऽवहितामवक्रामे karte'vahitāmavakrāme → कर्ते + अवहिताम् + अवक्रामे

    e before a; m before a vowel

    After -e an initial a is elided, marked by avagraha (karte ’vahitām; P. 6.1.109 (एङः पदान्तादति)); avahitām avakrāme.

  • यद्युवे yadyuve → यत् + युवे

    t before a voiced consonant

    yat becomes yad before y.

  • पतिष्यामीत्येवममृतादात्मानं patiṣyāmītyevamamṛtādātmānaṃ → पतिष्यामि + इति + एवम् + अमृतात् + आत्मानम्

    i + i; i before a dissimilar vowel; m before a vowel; t before a vowel

    patiṣyāmi + iti → patiṣyāmīti; iti + evam → ity evam; evam amṛtād ātmānam.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • असिधाराम् asidhārām

    ṣaṣṭhī-tatpuruṣa · असि + धारा

    Vigraha असेः धारा

    the edge of a sword

Morphology

युवे
A Vedic first-person middle form of √yu, ‘join’. Sāyaṇa: the text has ha where y would be expected, which is why some read yuve yuve (हकारो व्यत्ययेन यकारस्थाने पठितः। अत एव केचिद्युवे युव इति पठन्ति); Bhāskara reads ‘whether here or there I join [the edge or the pit]’ (इह वा असिधारां यौमि इह वा कर्तं यौम्य्).
अमृतात्
The base and both witnesses read amṛtāt. Sāyaṇa and Bhāskara explain the word as anṛtāt, ‘from untruth’ (Bhāskara: अस्यापि वर्णान्यत्वं छान्दसम्, a Vedic change of letter); the anonymous commentator keeps amṛtāt, ‘for the sake of the deathless’.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

Two yathā … evam comparisons. The first is a simple statement. The second sets a scene (yathā asidhārām karte avahitām avakrāme), quotes the walker’s thought (yad yuve … patiṣyāmi, closed by iti), and draws the rule with evam and the optative jugupseta.

Ambiguities

amṛtāt
As printed, ‘from the deathless’ reads oddly. The anonymous commentator takes it as ‘for the sake of the deathless, release’, steadying the mind and shrinking from sin; Sāyaṇa and Bhāskara read anṛtāt, ‘from untruth’, and Bhāskara explains the letter as a Vedic variation.
yad yuve yuve ha vā
The commentators struggle with these words: Sāyaṇa reads ha as ‘I’ and huve for yuve; Bhāskara ‘whether here or there I join’; the anonymous commentator ‘if I go, if I go’. All agree on the sense: whichever way one steps, harm follows.

Prose, one printed line of 72 syllables; no metre is claimed.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Reputation and conduct

The first image says good conduct travels on its own, like scent; the second says that with wrongdoing there is no safe way across — exposed or hidden, it costs. Both are about how actions outrun the moment they are done in. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Why might good deeds be compared to a smell rather than to something you can see?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Does “guard oneself from the deathless” make sense as printed, or is the commentators’ “from untruth” needed? What would each imply?

A contemplation exercise Think of one thing you would not want to do even if no one ever found out, and notice why.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
