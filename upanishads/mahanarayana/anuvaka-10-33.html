Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 33 of 80

Om ity ekākṣaraṃ brahma: the seer, deity and metre of Om

Before Om is used in the worship, the text states what every mantra’s use requires one to state: its seer, its deity, its metre and its application.

5 Faithful English translation

‘Om’ — the one syllable — [is] brahman. [Its] deity [is] Agni; [its] ṛṣi [is] brahman; [its] metre the gāyatra; [its] application [is] to union with the supreme self, of the same form.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

The single syllable Om is Brahman. Its deity is Agni, its seer is Brahman, its metre is the gāyatrī, and it is used for union with the supreme Self.

Key concepts ओम् Om अक्षर akṣara, the imperishable; syllable ब्रह्मन् Brahman ऋषि seer देवता deity अग्नि Agni, fire

7 Explain it to a beginner

Every Vedic mantra, when it is used in a rite, is introduced by four facts: who first saw it (its seer), which deity it addresses, its metre, and what it is used for. This short section does that for the most basic sound of all, Om. It says that Om, the one syllable, is Brahman; that its deity is Agni; that its seer is Brahman too; that its metre is the gāyatrī; and that it is used for union with the supreme Self. The old commentator explains that it is placed here because Om is needed in breath control and the other acts of the worship, and he reads it all as one idea: the mantra, its seer and its deity are all Brahman. The section is a heading, not a hymn.

An analogy Like the label on a well-made instrument: maker, purpose and pitch, stated before it is played.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

That the one syllable Om is brahman, and that its deity is Agni, its ṛṣi brahman, its metre the gāyatra, and its application union with the supreme self.

Traditional Interpretation

Why is this said here?

The anonymous commentator: because Om is required everywhere — in breath control and the rest — its seer and the other particulars are stated in passing: प्राणायामादिषु सर्वत्राऽऽवश्यकस्योंकारस्य प्रसङ्गादृष्यादिकमुच्यते. The next two anuvākas invite the gāyatrī and give the breath-control mantra, in which Om is repeated with each vyāhṛti.

Traditional Interpretation

How is Agni the deity, and brahman the seer?

The commentator reads Agni by etymology as the supreme self: अङ्गति सर्वं जगद्व्याप्नोतीत्यग्निः. The seer is brahman indicated by the word Agni, and he concludes that mantra, seer and deity are one: मन्त्रोऽपि ब्रह्म, ऋषिरपि ब्रह्म, देवताऽपि ब्रह्म, सर्वं स्वयमेवेत्यर्थः.

Comparative Vedānta

What does Bhāskara give as the particulars of Om?

Commenting on the breath-control mantra (his anuvāka 27), Bhāskara gives different particulars: the seer of the praṇava is Brahmā the self-born, the metre the divine gāyatrī, the deity the supreme self — प्रणवः स्वयम्भुवो ब्रह्मण आर्षेयम् । देवी गायत्री छन्दः … परमात्मा वाच्या देवता. The text of this anuvāka names Agni as deity; Bhāskara does not comment on it in the copy used, and his recension may lack it.

Traditional Interpretation

Union with what?

The commentator turns the last words into locatives: Om is applied to union, the attainment of the supreme Brahman (सायुज्ये परब्रह्मावाप्तौ), a union characterised as the supreme self and ‘of one form with the whole world’ (सर्वजगत्समानरूपे).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Grammar and witnesses

chandam, paramātmam, sarūpam, sāyujyam and viniyogam stand in cases the sense does not require; the commentator says the cases are exchanged throughout (सर्वत्र व्यत्ययो बोध्यः). The witnesses differ only in spelling: the Ānandāśrama text has ब्रह्मा for ब्रह्म in the first line and the Tirupati e-text keys छ as च्ह.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • ओमित्येकाक्षरं omityekākṣaraṃ → ओम् + इति + एकाक्षरम्

    final m before a vowel; i before a dissimilar vowel

    om iti: m joined to the vowel; iti + ekākṣaram → ity ekākṣaram (P. 6.1.77 (इको यणचि)).

  • अग्निर्देवता agnirdevatā → अग्निः + देवता

    visarga sandhi (after i)

    agniḥ + devatā → agnir devatā: a visarga after i becomes r before a voiced sound.

  • इत्यार्षम् ityārṣam → इति + आर्षम्

    i before a dissimilar vowel

    iti + ārṣam → ity ārṣam (P. 6.1.77 (इको यणचि)).

Junctions outside the general rules

  • ब्रह्म · इति — unjoined hiatus (as printed): brahma · iti: the a of brahma and the i of iti are not joined (classical brahmeti); every witness prints them apart.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • एकाक्षरम् ekākṣaram

    karmadhāraya · एक + अक्षर

    Vigraha एकम् अक्षरम्

    one syllable

  • परमात्मम् paramātmam

    karmadhāraya · परम + आत्मन्

    Vigraha परमः आत्मा

    the supreme self

  • सरूपम् sarūpam

    bahuvrīhi · स + रूप

    Vigraha समानं रूपं यस्य

    having the same form

Morphology

छन्दम्
For the neuter s-stem chandas the expected nominative is chandaḥ; the text prints chandam, as if from an a-stem.
सायुज्यम्
With paramātmam and sarūpam it stands where a locative of purpose is meant (‘[applied] to union …’); the anonymous commentator restores locatives throughout and says the cases are exchanged everywhere: सर्वत्र व्यत्ययो बोध्यः.
विनियोगम्
A nominative is meant (‘the application is …’); the commentator glosses विनियोगं विनियोगः.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

Nominal sentences without a verb. The first identifies Om with brahman; the following give, in the fixed order of a ritual heading, the deity (Agni), the seer (brahman, with iti marking the end of the statement), the metre, and the application. Several words are in the accusative or nominative where the sense requires other cases.

Ambiguities

brahma ity ārṣam
Either ‘the ṛṣi is brahman’ (the anonymous commentator, who takes ārṣa as ṛṣi) or ‘[this is] brahman: thus the ārṣa’ closing the statements so far.
the deity Agni
The commentator refuses the ordinary sense: Agni here is not a particular god but, by etymology, what pervades all (अग्निशब्दोऽत्र रूढ्या देवताविशेषवाचको न भवति, अपि तु योगेन पमात्मवाचकः).

Metre

prose · syllables counted as written: 8 + 10 + 12 + 7

A prose heading; the four lines count 8, 10, 12 and 7 syllables, and the first line (om ity ekākṣaraṃ brahma) is also the first pāda of Bhagavad Gītā 8.13.

Textual variations between witnesses

  • Witness error Ānandāśrama pariśiṣṭa text (1927): ब्रह्मा। अग्निर्देवता — The Ānandāśrama pariśiṣṭa text alone reads ा for —; the base and the Tirupati e-text agree.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Attention

Before a mantra is used, the tradition requires one to state what it is: who saw it, whom it addresses, its metre, its purpose. Saying plainly what one is about to do, and why, before doing it is a form of attention. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin If you had to state the ‘seer, deity, metre and use’ of something you do every day, what would they be?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The commentator says mantra, seer and deity are all brahman. What changes when the one who speaks, the words and their addressee are said to be one?

A contemplation exercise Before a routine task, name to yourself what it is for, then do it.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
