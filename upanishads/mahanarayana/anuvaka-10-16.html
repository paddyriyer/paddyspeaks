Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad · Prapāṭhaka 10 · Anuvāka 16 of 80

Nidhanapataye namaḥ: the names used in installing a liṅga

Twenty-two names of Śiva in pairs — the god and his liṅga — with which, the text says, the liṅga of all is installed.

7 Explain it to a beginner

This section is a list of names of Śiva, given in pairs: first the god under a name, then his liṅga — the simple upright stone or metal form in which Śiva is worshipped — under the same name. So: homage to the golden one, homage to the golden liṅga; to Śiva, and to the liṅga of Śiva; to the highest, and to the highest liṅga. The final line says what these names are for: with them one installs ‘the liṅga of all’. The old anonymous commentator says the mantras are used when a liṅga is set up in a new temple, or each day when a clay liṅga is made for worship. He also explains some puzzling words, taking ‘suvarṇa’ here as silver because gold has already been named. The text describes a rite; it makes no promise about results.

An analogy Like the words spoken at the laying of a foundation stone, naming both the building and the one it is built for.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

It offers homage to the god under ten names in the first unit and twelve in the second, each paired with the liṅga of that name, and says that with these one installs the liṅga of all, of Soma and Sūrya, whose limbs are mantras, purifying.

Traditional Interpretation

When are these mantras used?

The anonymous commentator: when a liṅga is installed in a newly built Śiva temple and daily when a clay liṅga is installed, इतः परं नूतनशिवालयादिकं कृत्वा तत्र लिङ्गप्रतिष्ठापनावसरे प्रत्यहं पार्थिवलिङ्गप्रतिष्ठापनावसरे च विनियुक्ता; he takes this from the mantra’s own words, सर्वलिङ्गँ स्थापयति. He also says they are for homage to Pārvatī’s lord and for removing sin by recitation alone; that is his claim about their use, not the text’s.

Traditional Interpretation

How does he read the liṅgas?

As forms the god takes in the worlds: the liṅga on high worshipped by the gods in the upper worlds, ऊर्ध्वायोर्ध्वलोकेषु देवतारूपेण विद्यमानायेत्यर्थः; liṅgas of gold and silver; the blazing liṅga as the twelve jyotirliṅgas on earth, ज्वललिङ्गाय भूलोके विद्यमानद्वादशज्योतिर्लिङ्गरूपाय.

Traditional Interpretation

What is pāṇimantra?

The anonymous commentator: the liṅga whose limbs, of which the hand stands for all, are the Vedic mantras, पाण्युपलक्षितसर्वावयवायमाना मन्त्रा वेदवाक्यविशेषा यस्य तत्पाणिमन्त्रम् — in short, whose body is the three Vedas, त्रयीमयशरीरमिति यावत्.

Traditional Interpretation

Who may install it?

The commentator says every member of the three twice-born classes installs it; that restriction is his gloss, not the text’s, which names no one.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • 10.16.33 ऊर्ध्वाय नमः ūrdhvāya namaḥ → ऊर्ध्वाय + नमः

    no sandhi

    Each name ends in a vowel before n: the words stand side by side; namaḥ before the pause keeps its visarga.

  • 10.16.34 एतत्सोमस्य etatsomasya → एतत् + सोमस्य

    t before s

    etat + somasya → etat somasya (no change; written together).

  • 10.16.34 सर्वलिङ्गग्ग् स्थापयति sarvaliṅgagg sthāpayati → सर्वलिङ्गम् + स्थापयति

    m before s (Taittirīya spelling)

    sarvaliṅgam + sthāpayati → sarvaliṅgaṃ sthāpayati (P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः)); the recitation writes this anusvāra before a sibilant as ग्ग्.

  • 10.16.34 पाणिमन्त्रं पवित्रम् pāṇimantraṃ pavitram → पाणिमन्त्रम् + पवित्रम्

    m before a consonant

    m becomes anusvāra (P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः)).

nidhana usually means ‘end, death’; the anonymous commentator reads it as ni + dhana, ‘abundant wealth’, and the lord as Kubera’s form. The text gives no gloss.
10.16.33 निधनपतान्तिकाय
Analysed by the anonymous commentator as nidhana-pa + ta + antika: ‘guardian of treasure’, ‘who spreads [it] to devotees’, and ‘near’ — a learned etymology of an unusual word.
10.16.34 आत्माय
An a-stem dative made from ātman (for the regular ātmane), as the list’s rhythm of -āya datives requires.
10.16.34 सर्वलिङ्गम्
The base writes सर्वलिङ्गग्ग्, the Taittirīya recitation’s spelling of the anusvāra before s; the other witnesses write ँ or ꣳ.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

A litany of datives with namaḥ, arranged in pairs: a name of the god and the same name with liṅga (‘to Bhava, to Bhava’s liṅga’). The last line is a sentence of a different kind, a brāhmaṇa-like statement of use: etat (‘this’) … sthāpayati (‘[one] sets up’), with sarvaliṅgam as object, qualified by the genitives somasya sūryasya and by pāṇimantram pavitram.

Ambiguities

nidhanapati
nidhana ordinarily means ‘end, death’, so ‘lord of the end’ is a possible reading; the anonymous commentator reads ‘lord of treasures’, as Kubera’s form. No other commentary on this anuvāka is in the edition’s sources.
suvarṇa
Since gold (hiraṇya) has just been named, the anonymous commentator takes suvarṇa here as silver, by etymology (‘of fine colour’): हिरण्यस्योक्तत्वात्सुवर्णशब्दो रूढिमुत्सृज्य योगेन रजतं वदति. The text itself may simply repeat ‘gold’ in another word.
the last line
Who sets up the liṅga is not said; the commentator supplies the worshipper. somasya sūryasya may qualify the liṅga as belonging to the moon and the sun, or, as he takes it, stand for all the deities.

Metre

prose · syllables counted as written: 8 + 10 + 5 + 7 + 6 + 8 + 6 + 8 + 5 + 7 + 5 + 7 + 5 + 7 + 5 + 7 + 5 + 7 + 5 + 7 + 6 + 8 + 23

A yajus litany: the name-lines count 5 to 10 syllables (8, 10, 5, 7, 6, 8, 6, 8, 5, 7 in the first unit; 5, 7, … 6, 8 in the second), and the last line 23.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Ritual

Each name is given twice, once for the god and once for the form in which he is worshipped. The pairing keeps in view that the visible object and what it stands for are both honoured, and are not simply the same. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Why might a list name both the god and his liṅga each time?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The commentator gives learned etymologies for difficult words (nidhanapata, suvarṇa). When is an etymology an explanation, and when a new meaning?

A contemplation exercise Choose an object that stands for something you value, and say plainly what it stands for.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
