This syllable is Brahman

This syllable is Brahman, this syllable is the highest; whoever knows this syllable has whatever he desires.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

This syllable is Brahman; this syllable is the highest. Whoever knows this syllable will have whatever they desire.

Key concepts अक्षर akṣara, the imperishable; syllable ओम् Om ब्रह्मन् Brahman उपासना worship, meditation

7 Explain it to a beginner

Having named the goal as Om, Death now praises the syllable itself. The Sanskrit word for syllable also means imperishable, and the verse uses both meanings at once: this syllable, this imperishable thing, is Brahman, the ultimate reality, and it is the highest. Whoever truly knows it receives whatever they seek. Śaṅkara explains the double statement carefully. There is a higher and a lower form of Brahman, and Om is the symbol of both. Someone who meditates on Om seeking the highest comes to know it; someone seeking the lower attains that. Raṅgarāmānuja says the syllable is called Brahman because it is the means by which Brahman is reached, and it is the highest of all things to recite and meditate on. Madhva cites a verse that names the imperishable as Viṣṇu.

An analogy Like a key that is also the name of the house it opens.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does Death say of the syllable?

That this syllable is Brahman and the highest, and that whoever knows it has whatever they desire.

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara: two Brahmans, one symbol

एतद्ध्येवाक्षरं ब्रह्म अपरम् एतद्ध्येवाक्षरं परं च । तयोर्हि प्रतीकमेतदक्षरम् — the syllable is the symbol of both the lower and the higher Brahman. Knowing it means meditating on it as Brahman, उपास्य ब्रह्मेति; then what one seeks follows: the higher is to be known, परं चेत् ज्ञातव्यम्, the lower to be reached.

Traditional Interpretation

Raṅgarāmānuja: the word that names Brahman

In this verse and the next Death praises the word that names Brahman: एवं वाचकं प्रणवं द्वाभ्यां मन्त्राभ्यां स्तौति. It is called Brahman as the means to Brahman, ब्रह्मप्राप्तिसाधनत्वात् ब्रह्म, and is the highest among things recited and meditated on, जप्येषु ध्येयेषु च श्रेष्ठम् इत्यर्थः. One who meditates on it wishing for a fruit receives that fruit.

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva: the imperishable is Viṣṇu

Madhva’s whole comment on 1.2.14–17 is a verse he cites without naming its source (इति च): एतद्ध्येवाक्षरं ब्रह्म विष्ण्वाख्यं परमव्ययम् — ‘this imperishable Brahman, called Viṣṇu, highest, unchanging’.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

brahma etad

The base, Śaṅkara’s quotation of the verse and Raṅgarāmānuja write brahma etad apart; Madhva’s text joins brahmaitad (corrections.json). The hiatus stands at the pāda boundary.

Comparative Vedānta

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, Kaṭhopaniṣat-prakāśikā 1.2.16

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Om is called Brahman as the means to it — ब्रह्मप्राप्तिसाधनत्वात् ब्रह्म — and is the best support of recitation and meditation.

Dvaita · Madhva, Kāṭhakopaniṣad-bhāṣya 1.2.14–17

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The imperishable is Viṣṇu, on a verse Madhva cites: विष्ण्वाख्यं परमव्ययम्.

Where they disagree

Śaṅkara distinguishes a higher and a lower Brahman, both symbolised by Om; Raṅgarāmānuja and Madhva know one Brahman, the Lord, whom Om names and through whom meditation finds its support.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • एतद्ध्येवाक्षरं etaddhyevākṣaraṃ → एतत् + हि + एव + अक्षरम्

    t + h; yaṇ; a + a

    etat before h becomes etad, P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते), and the h after d may become dh, P. 8.4.62 (झयो होऽन्यतरस्याम्). hi + eva give hy eva, P. 6.1.77 (इको यणचि); eva + akṣaram join long, P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः).

  • यो यदिच्छति yo yadicchati → यः + यत् + इच्छति

    aḥ before a voiced consonant; t voiced

    yaḥ before y becomes yo, P. 6.1.114 (हशि च) and P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः); yat before a vowel becomes yad, P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते).

Junctions outside the general rules

  • ब्रह्म · एतत् — unjoined hiatus (as printed): The base prints brahma etad unjoined at the pāda boundary, as Śaṅkara’s and Raṅgarāmānuja’s quotations of the verse do; Madhva’s text joins brahmaitad (corrections.json). Unjoined, each pāda counts eight syllables.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this mantra.

Morphology

अक्षरम्
‘Imperishable’ (a- with √kṣar ‘flow away’) and ‘syllable’: the verse uses both senses at once. Śaṅkara: the syllable is the symbol of both Brahmans, तयोर्हि प्रतीकमेतदक्षरम्.
ब्रह्म
Neuter nominative singular of brahman.

Anvaya

एतत् हि एव अक्षरम् ब्रह्म, एतत् हि एव अक्षरम् परम् । एतत् हि एव अक्षरम् ज्ञात्वा यः यत् इच्छति, तस्य तत् [भवति] ।

etat hi eva akṣaram brahma, etat hi eva akṣaram param | etat hi eva akṣaram jñātvā yaḥ yat icchati, tasya tat bhavati |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Three statements, each opening etad dhy evākṣaram. The first two are nominal sentences (‘this syllable is Brahman’, ‘this syllable is the highest’). The third has an absolutive (jñātvā) inside a relative clause (yo yad icchati), and the main clause tasya tat (‘his is that’) leaves ‘be’ to be supplied.

Ambiguities

brahma … param: two Brahmans or one?
Śaṅkara supplies ‘lower’ to the first statement: एतद्ध्येवाक्षरं ब्रह्म अपरम् एतद्ध्येवाक्षरं परं च — the syllable is the symbol of both the lower and the higher Brahman, so that one who meditates on it attains whichever he seeks: the higher is to be known, परं चेत् ज्ञातव्यम्, the lower to be reached, अपरं चेत् प्राप्तव्यम्. Raṅgarāmānuja: the syllable is called Brahman because it is the means of reaching Brahman — ब्रह्मप्राप्तिसाधनत्वात् ब्रह्म — and ‘highest’ means best among what is recited and meditated on: जप्येषु ध्येयेषु च श्रेष्ठम् इत्यर्थः.

Metre

anuṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 16 + 16

Anuṣṭubh, 16 + 16, regular. The hiatus brahma etad falls at the boundary of the first two pādas and keeps each at eight; Madhva’s joined brahmaitad would count fifteen for the line.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Technology and digital distractions

The verse gives its whole weight to a single syllable. In a day of thousands of words, most of them read and forgotten, it is worth noticing what it would mean to give that kind of attention to one. This is a reflection, not a claim made by the text.

A question to begin Is there a word that means far more to you than its dictionary meaning?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Why might the verse say both ‘Brahman’ and ‘the highest’? Does Śaṅkara’s distinction between a higher and a lower Brahman help, or does it make the verse say less than it seems to?
