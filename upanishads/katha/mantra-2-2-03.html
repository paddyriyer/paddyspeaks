Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 2 · Vallī 2 · Mantra 3 of 15

The small one in the middle

He who sends the breath up and down sits small in the middle of the body, and all the gods attend him.

5 Faithful English translation

[He who] leads the out-breath upward [and] casts the in-breath back — him, the small one seated in the middle, all the gods attend.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

He sends the breath upward and drives the downward breath back. He sits in the middle, small and worthy of love, and all the gods wait upon him.

Key concepts प्राण breath, life-force आत्मन् self, Self देवता deity इन्द्रिय indriya, a sense उपासना worship, meditation

7 Explain it to a beginner

Breathing goes on without our deciding it: the breath goes out and the breath comes in. The verse asks who does this. Something sits in the middle of the body, in the heart, small and close, and it moves the breath up and down. All the gods attend on it. Śaṅkara explains the gods as the senses: the eye, the ear and the rest keep working and bring what they find to this one, as people bring gifts to a king. Their work is for someone, so that someone must be other than they are. Raṅgarāmānuja reads the one in the middle as the supreme Self in every heart, and the word for small also as ‘worthy of love’.

An analogy Like a conductor in the middle of an orchestra, unseen by the audience, for whom every player keeps time.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse say?

Someone leads the breath up and casts the down-breath back; seated in the middle, small, he is attended by all the gods.

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara: a sign of the self

The verse gives a sign by which the self is known, आत्मनः स्वरूपाधिगमे लिङ्गमुच्यते. The senses serve it ceaselessly, bringing their perceptions like subjects bringing tribute, रूपादिविज्ञानं बलिमुपाहरन्तो विश इव राजानम् उपासते. That for whose sake all the work of breath and senses goes on is other than they, यदर्था यत्प्रयुक्ताश्च सर्वे वायुकरणव्यापाराः, सोऽन्यः सिद्ध इति वाक्यार्थः.

Traditional Interpretation

Raṅgarāmānuja: the Lord in the heart

The one who moves the breaths is the supreme Self in the heart of all, सर्वेषां हृदयगतः परमात्मा, seated in the heart-lotus, हृदयपुण्डरीकमध्ये आसीनं वामनम्, and served by all beings of sattva nature.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Breath in the Kaṭha

prāṇa and apāna here are the two movements of breath, upward and downward. The verse gives no subject to the first line; Śaṅkara supplies the relative ‘he who’, which the accusatives of the second line take up.

Comparative Vedānta

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, Kaṭhopaniṣat-prakāśikā 2.2.3

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The mover of the breaths is the supreme Self in every heart, सर्वेषां हृदयगतः परमात्मा.

Dvaita

No position is given for this school here: the edition does not force one.

Where they disagree

Both read the one in the middle as the self that the breaths and senses serve: for Śaṅkara, the self known as other than them; for Raṅgarāmānuja, the supreme Self dwelling in the heart. Madhva’s bhāṣya comments once on 2.2.3–5, on breath upheld by Viṣṇu; that comment is quoted at 2.2.5.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • ऊर्ध्वं प्राणम् ūrdhvaṃ prāṇam → ऊर्ध्वम् + प्राणम्

    m before a consonant

    A final m becomes anusvāra before a consonant, P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः).

  • उन्नयति unnayati → उत् + नयति

    d before a nasal

    The t of the preverb ud becomes the nasal n before n, P. 8.4.45 (यरोऽनुनासिकेऽनुनासिको वा).

  • प्रत्यगस्यति pratyagasyati → प्रत्यक् + अस्यति

    k before a vowel

    A final k becomes g before a voiced sound, P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते).

  • देवा उपासते devā upāsate → देवाः + उपासते

    āḥ before a vowel

    After ā the visarga becomes y, P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि), which is dropped, P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य), leaving the vowels apart.

Junctions outside the general rules

  • उन्नयति · अपानम् — pāda-boundary hiatus: unnayati apānam stands unjoined at the pāda boundary in the base (AS); the vishvAsa mūla and Madhva’s text join it (unnayaty apānam), an orthographic difference (corrections.json). Unjoined, the first pāda keeps its eight syllables.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this mantra.

Morphology

उन्नयति
ud-√nī, ‘lead up’, present. Raṅgarāmānuja’s printed mūla has unnamayati, ‘raises’, from ud-√nam, which no witness of this edition reads.
वामनम्
vāmana is ordinarily ‘dwarf, small’; Śaṅkara derives it from √van, ‘love, serve’: ‘the one to be resorted to’. Raṅgarāmānuja gives both senses.
अस्यति
The fourth-class present of √as, ‘throw’ (asyati), distinct from √as, ‘be’ (asti).

Anvaya

[यः] प्राणम् ऊर्ध्वम् उन्नयति अपानम् प्रत्यक् अस्यति [तम्] मध्ये आसीनम् वामनम् विश्वे देवाः उपासते ।

yaḥ prāṇam ūrdhvam unnayati apānam pratyak asyati tam madhye āsīnam vāmanam viśve devāḥ upāsate |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

The first line has two verbs without a stated subject; Śaṅkara supplies the relative yaḥ, ‘he who’, यः इति वाक्यशेषः, picked up by the accusatives of the second line. The second line is a full sentence: all the gods (subject) attend (upāsate) the small one seated in the middle (objects vāmanam āsīnam).

Ambiguities

Who moves the breaths?
Śaṅkara: the self, which is shown by this as a sign, आत्मनः स्वरूपाधिगमे लिङ्गमुच्यते. Raṅgarāmānuja: the supreme Self dwelling in the heart of all, सर्वेषां हृदयगतः परमात्मा.
The gods
Śaṅkara: the senses, which bring their perceptions to him as subjects bring tribute to a king, विश इव राजानम्. Raṅgarāmānuja: all beings of sattva nature.
vāmana
Small, the size of the heart, or ‘to be loved’ (Śaṅkara, Raṅgarāmānuja); the two senses are not exclusive.

Metre

anuṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 16 + 16

16 and 16 syllables, counting unnayati apānam unjoined as the base prints it; joined it would leave the first half-line with 15.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Psychology and inner life

Most of what keeps us alive happens without our choosing — breath, heartbeat, the work of the senses. The verse turns that ordinary fact into a question about who all this activity is for. This is a reflection, not a claim made by the text.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Can you notice your breath going on without your deciding it?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Why does Śaṅkara think that service by the senses proves there is someone other than them?

A contemplation exercise Sit for two minutes and simply watch the breath rise and fall, without changing it.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
