Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 1 · Vallī 3 · Mantra 15 of 17

Without sound, without touch

Having discerned that which is without sound, touch, form, taste or smell, unchanging and eternal, without beginning or end, beyond the great, steady — one is freed from the mouth of death.

5 Faithful English translation

That which [is] soundless, touchless, formless, undecaying, likewise tasteless, eternal and smell-less, without beginning or end, beyond the great, steady — having discerned it, one is freed from the mouth of death.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

What has no sound, no touch, no form, no taste and no smell, what does not decay and is eternal, without beginning or end, beyond the great and unmoving: one who discerns it is freed from the jaws of death.

Key concepts ब्रह्मन् Brahman आत्मन् self, Self मृत्यु death मोक्ष liberation

7 Explain it to a beginner

Death now sums up what he has been describing. It has no sound, no touch, no shape, no taste and no smell: none of the five senses can find it. It does not wear away; it is eternal; it has no beginning and no end. It is beyond even the great principle at the top of the earlier ladder, and it stands still. Whoever comes to perceive it is freed from the mouth of death. Śaṅkara explains why the list of absences matters: everything we can sense is made of parts and changes, so what has none of those qualities cannot change or end. The mouth of death, he says, is ignorance, desire and the actions they produce. Raṅgarāmānuja takes the mouth of death as frightening saṃsāra, the round of birth and death, and the discerning as a meditation that sees it clearly.

An analogy Like describing space by what it is not — not wood, not stone, not water — until what is left is the room in which all of them stand.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse say?

It describes that which has none of the five qualities the senses perceive, which does not decay, is eternal, without beginning or end, beyond the great and fixed, and says that one who discerns it is freed from the mouth of death.

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara: why the absence of qualities makes it eternal

Gross things are made of sound, touch, form, taste and smell, and as these fall away, from earth to space, things become subtler and more lasting. What has none of them is subtle without limit: एतद्व्याख्यातं ब्रह्म. It does not decay because it has no such qualities, इदं त्वशब्दादिमत्त्वादव्ययं न व्येति न क्षीयते, and it is eternal because it has no cause and no effect. It is beyond the great because it is consciousness itself, नित्यविज्ञप्तिस्वरूपत्वात्, and steady because it is unchanging, कूटस्थं नित्यं. The mouth of death is ignorance, desire and action: मृत्युगोचरादविद्याकामकर्मलक्षणात्.

Traditional Interpretation

Raṅgarāmānuja: each quality is eternal; the great is the self

He reads the verse as a conclusion (उपसंहरति). ‘Eternal’ goes with each of the negatives, इत्यादौ प्रत्येकम् अभिसम्बध्यते, and the Supreme is undecaying like time, कालवद् अव्ययम्. ‘The great’ is the individual self of 1.3.13, so the Supreme is beyond the self. To discern it is to make it an object by meditation like seeing, and the mouth of death is fearful saṃsāra: भीषणात् संसारात् इत्यर्थः.

Traditional Interpretation

Five senses, five absences

The five negatives follow the five sense-qualities: sound, touch, form, taste, smell. Śaṅkara explains the list by the elements: earth has all five, and from water up to space each has one quality fewer and is correspondingly subtler and more lasting: तत्रैकैकगुणापकर्षेण गन्धादीनां सूक्ष्मत्वमहत्त्वविशुद्धत्वनित्यत्वादितारतम्यं दृष्टमबादिषु यावदाकाशमिति. What has none of them is subtle without limit.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 1.3.15

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The verse defines Brahman by the absence of every sensible quality: एतद्व्याख्यातं ब्रह्म; knowing it as the Self frees one from ignorance, desire and action, the domain of death.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, Kaṭhopaniṣat-prakāśikā 1.3.15

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The Supreme is beyond the individual self named ‘great’ in 1.3.13, and is reached by meditation like seeing: दर्शन-समानाकारोपासनेन विषयीकृत्य इत्यर्थः; the release is from saṃsāra.

Dvaita

No position is given for this school here: the edition does not force one.

Where they disagree

Whether ‘beyond the great’ means beyond the cosmic intellect, the reality being one's own Self (Śaṅkara), or beyond the individual self, the Supreme being other than it (Raṅgarāmānuja). Madhva's bhāṣya, in our source, has no comment on this verse.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • अशब्दमस्पर्शमरूपमव्ययं aśabdamasparśamarūpamavyayaṃ → अशब्दम् + अस्पर्शम् + अरूपम् + अव्ययम्

    m before a vowel; m before a consonant

    A final m before a vowel is kept and joined to it; before a consonant it becomes anusvāra, P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः).

  • तथारसं tathārasaṃ → तथा + अरसम्

    ā + a

    Like vowels join long, P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः).

  • अगन्धवच्च agandhavacca → अगन्धवत् + च

    t before c

    A final t becomes c before c, P. 8.4.40 (स्तोः श्चुना श्चुः).

  • मृत्युमुखात्प्रमुच्यते mṛtyumukhātpramucyate → मृत्युमुखात् + प्रमुच्यते

    t before p

    The final t stays before p.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • अनाद्यनन्तम् anādyanantam

    karmadhāraya of two bahuvrīhis · अनादि + अनन्त

    Vigraha अनादि च तत् अनन्तं च

    without beginning and without end; Śaṅkara: अविद्यमानः आदिः कारणमस्य तदिदमनादि, अविद्यमानोऽन्तः कार्यमस्य तदनन्तम्

  • मृत्युमुखात् mṛtyumukhāt

    ṣaṣṭhī-tatpuruṣa · मृत्यु + मुख

    Vigraha मृत्योः मुखम्

    the mouth of death

  • अशब्दम् aśabdam

    nañ-bahuvrīhi · अ + शब्द

    Vigraha अविद्यमानः शब्दः यस्य तत्

    that which has no sound; so also asparśam, arūpam, arasam

Morphology

अगन्धवत्
agandha, ‘without smell’, with the possessive -vat — literally ‘having no-smell’. Śaṅkara repeats it in his summary of the five: अशब्दमस्पर्शमरूपमरसमगन्धवच्च यत्.
तम्
Masculine, though the relative yat and the adjectives are neuter: the neuter ‘that which’ is the masculine Self (Śaṅkara: तम् आत्मानं).
निचाय्य
Absolutive of ni-√cāy, ‘perceive, discern’; the same form as in 1.1.17 (nicāyyemām, corrected there in this edition).

Anvaya

यत् अशब्दम् अस्पर्शम् अरूपम् अव्ययम् तथा अरसम् नित्यम् अगन्धवत् च [अस्ति], तम् अनाद्यनन्तम् महतः परम् ध्रुवम् निचाय्य मृत्युमुखात् प्रमुच्यते ।

yat aśabdam asparśam arūpam avyayam tathā arasam nityam agandhavat ca asti, tam anādyanantam mahataḥ param dhruvam nicāyya mṛtyumukhāt pramucyate |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

A relative clause with yat and a string of neuter adjectives describes the object; the second line adds more qualifications in the accusative, takes it up with tam, and gives the result: nicāyya … pramucyate, ‘having discerned it, [one] is freed’. The subject of the passive is left unexpressed.

Ambiguities

‘Beyond the great’
Śaṅkara: beyond the principle called intellect, महत्तत्त्वाद्बुद्ध्याख्यात्, as in 1.3.10–11. Raṅgarāmānuja: mahataḥ refers to the individual self named in 1.3.13 (‘the great self’): इति पूर्वमन्त्रनिर्दिष्टो जीवो गृह्यते.
How is it ‘discerned’?
Śaṅkara: अवगम्य, having understood. Raṅgarāmānuja: having made it an object by meditation like seeing, दर्शन-समानाकारोपासनेन विषयीकृत्य इत्यर्थः.

Metre

Jagatī, 4 × 12 (24 + 24), regular.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Science and consciousness

Instruments extend the senses; they do not replace them. Whatever they measure still arrives as something like sound, touch or form. The verse points to what would remain if every such quality were taken away, and asks whether that is nothing or the one who perceives. Questions about consciousness still meet that difficulty. This is a reflection, not a claim made by the text.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Try to imagine something with no sound, touch, shape, taste or smell. What is left in your imagination?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Why does the verse describe the goal almost entirely by what it is not? What does such a description do that a positive one cannot?

A contemplation exercise Sit quietly and notice, one by one, what you hear, feel, see, taste and smell. Then notice what is aware of all five.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
