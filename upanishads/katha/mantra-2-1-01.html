Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 2 · Vallī 1 · Mantra 1 of 15

The openings pierced outward

The self-existent made the senses face outward, so people see outward and not the inner self; a rare wise one, wanting immortality, turned his eyes back and saw the self within.

5 Faithful English translation

‘The self-existent pierced the openings [so that they go] outward; therefore one sees outward, not the inner self. A certain wise one, wishing for immortality, with eyes turned back, saw the inward self.’

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

‘The self-existent made the senses open outward, so people look outward and not at the self within. Now and then a wise person, longing for what does not die, turns the gaze back and sees the self within.’

Key concepts प्रत्यगात्मन् pratyagātman, the inward self इन्द्रिय indriya, a sense आत्मन् self, Self धीर the wise अमृत immortality, the deathless स्वयम्भू self-existent ईश्वर the Lord

7 Explain it to a beginner

The second half of the Kaṭha opens with a simple observation about how we are made. Our senses — eyes, ears, touch, taste, smell — are like openings that face outward. The verse says the one who made us made them that way, so we naturally look at the world around us and not at the one who is doing the looking. That is not a fault; it is how the senses work. But now and then someone wise, wanting something that does not pass away, turns the attention around and looks inward, and sees the self within. Śaṅkara compares this to making a river flow back against its current: possible, but unusual and hard. The verse explains why the self is so easily missed: not because it is far away, but because everything in us points the other way.

An analogy A camera records everything in front of it but never its own lens; to see the lens you have to turn the camera around.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse say?

The self-existent made the senses face outward, so people see what is outside and not the inner self. A rare wise one, wishing for immortality, turned his eyes back and saw the self within.

Traditional Interpretation

Against the current

Śaṅkara: the senses by their nature go out to show sound and the rest, शब्दादिविषयप्रकाशनाय प्रवर्तन्ते; the wise one who turns back is like someone making a river flow upstream, नद्याः प्रतिस्रोतःप्रवर्तनमिव धीरः. ‘Eyes turned back’ means all the senses withdrawn from every object, आवृत्तं व्यावृत्तं चक्षुः श्रोत्रादिकमिन्द्रियजातमशेषविषयात् यस्य स आवृत्तचक्षुः, because one cannot both attend to outer objects and see the inward self, न हि बाह्यविषयालोचनपरत्वं प्रत्यगात्मेक्षणं चैकस्य सम्भवति.

Traditional Interpretation

Why ‘self’ means the inward one

Śaṅkara says the word ātman is used of the inward self, and cites a verse giving its derivation, यच्चाप्नोति यदादत्ते यच्चात्ति विषयानिह and यच्चास्य सन्ततो भावस्तस्मादात्मेति कीर्त्यते, introducing it only as remembered tradition, इत्यात्मशब्दव्युत्पत्तिस्मरणात्; he does not name its source.

Comparative Vedānta

Raṅgarāmānuja on the seeker

He reads the verse as Death’s lament: even after the call to rise and wake, people stay turned away, इति प्रोत्साहनेऽपि आत्मस्वरूपविमुखान् पश्यन् शोचति. The one who turns back is a seeker of release whose senses are withdrawn each from its object, स्वस्वविषयव्यावृत्तेन्द्रियो मुमुक्षुः इत्यर्थः; ‘eye’ stands for all the senses, चक्षुश्शब्दः इन्द्रियमात्रपरः.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The verb and the witnesses

vyatṛṇat is the imperfect of tṛd ‘pierce’; Raṅgarāmānuja takes the root as tṛh ‘injure’, तृह् हिंसायाम्, and Madhva’s one comment on the verse is a root note, तृणु कात्करणे इति धातुः. Madhva’s text reads vyatṛṇāt and writes parāk; aikṣat is a Vedic active form, छान्दसं परस्मैपदम् (Raṅgarāmānuja).

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 2.1.1

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The inward self is the seer of all; it is missed because the senses go outward, and seen when they are withdrawn: स एवं संस्कृतः प्रत्यगात्मानं पश्यति. The immortality wished for is the self’s own eternal nature, अमरणधर्मत्वं नित्यस्वभावताम्.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, Kaṭhopaniṣat-prakāśikā 2.1.1

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The senses were made to show other things, not the self, परप्रकाशकानि, न तु आत्मप्रकाशकानि; the wise one is the seeker of release who withdraws them, स्वस्वविषयव्यावृत्तेन्द्रियो मुमुक्षुः.

Dvaita

No position is given for this school here: the edition does not force one.

Where they disagree

Śaṅkara and Raṅgarāmānuja agree on the picture — outward senses, the rare inward turn — and differ only on the root of vyatṛṇat and on the mood (Raṅgarāmānuja hears Death’s lament). In our source Madhva’s only comment on this verse is a note on the root, so no Dvaita reading of it is given here.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • व्यतृणत्स्वयम्भूस्तस्मात् vyatṛṇatsvayambhūstasmāt → व्यतृणत् + स्वयम्भूः + तस्मात्

    visarga → s

    Final t stays before s; the visarga of svayambhūḥ becomes s before t, P. 8.3.34 (विसर्जनीयस्य सः).

  • कश्चिद्धीरः kaściddhīraḥ → कश्चित् + धीरः

    voicing

    Final t becomes d before a voiced sound, P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते).

  • ऐक्षदावृत्तचक्षुरमृतत्वमिच्छन् aikṣadāvṛttacakṣuramṛtatvamicchan → ऐक्षत् + आवृत्तचक्षुः + अमृतत्वम् + इच्छन्

    t → d; s → r; m before a vowel

    aikṣat → aikṣad before a vowel, P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते); the final s of cakṣus becomes ru, P. 8.2.66 (ससजुषो रुँः), heard as r before a vowel; final m stays m before a vowel.

    bahuvrīhi · आवृत्त + चक्षुस्

    one whose eye is turned back

Morphology

व्यतृणत्
Imperfect of tṛd ‘split, pierce’ (seventh class, nasal infix: tṛṇatti, imperfect atṛṇat), with vi. The commentators name other roots: Raṅgarāmānuja तृह् हिंसायाम्, ‘injure’, and alternatively ‘created’, परार्थप्रकाशकानि इन्द्रियाणि सृष्टवान् इत्यर्थः; Madhva’s only comment on the verse is a root note, तृणु कात्करणे इति धातुः. Madhva’s text reads vyatṛṇāt (corrections.json).
अन्तरात्मन्
Joined with na as nāntarātman (a + a → ā, P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः)). The stem form. Grammatically it can be the Vedic locative singular without ending (-an for -ani): ‘[one sees] not in the inner self’. Both commentators gloss it as the accusative, ‘the inner self’: Śaṅkara नान्तरात्मानमित्यर्थः.
ऐक्षत्
Imperfect of īkṣ ‘see’, which is otherwise middle (aikṣata); Raṅgarāmānuja: a Vedic active ending, छान्दसं परस्मैपदम्. Both take the past as present: Śaṅkara पश्यतीत्यर्थः, छन्दसि कालानियमात्.
पराङ्
Nominative singular masculine of parāñc ‘turned away, outward’, used as an adverb. Madhva’s text writes parāk before paśyati (an orthographic difference).

Anvaya

स्वयम्भूः खानि पराञ्चि व्यतृणत् तस्मात् [लोकः] पराङ् पश्यति न अन्तरात्मन् । कश्चित् धीरः अमृतत्वम् इच्छन् आवृत्तचक्षुः [सन्] प्रत्यगात्मानम् ऐक्षत् ॥

svayambhūḥ khāni parāñci vyatṛṇat tasmāt lokaḥ parāṅ paśyati na antarātman | kaścit dhīraḥ amṛtatvam icchan āvṛttacakṣuḥ san pratyagātmānam aikṣat ||

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Three clauses: a cause (svayambhūḥ … vyatṛṇat), its consequence (tasmāt parāṅ paśyati, with no stated subject — ‘one’, people at large), and the exception (kaścid dhīraḥ … aikṣat). The predicative parāñci says how the openings were made; āvṛttacakṣuḥ and amṛtatvam icchan describe the wise one.

Ambiguities

Who pierced the openings, and what does ‘pierced’ mean?
Śaṅkara: the Supreme Lord, independent, परमेश्वरः, who ‘injured’ the senses by making them face outward, हिंसितवान्. Raṅgarāmānuja: the independent Lord, स्वतन्त्र ईश्वरः; either ‘injured’ or, since roots have many senses, simply ‘created’ the senses to show other things, परार्थप्रकाशकानि इन्द्रियाणि सृष्टवान् इत्यर्थः.
Who speaks, and in what mood?
Raṅgarāmānuja: Death, having urged people to rise and wake (1.3.14), grieves to see them still turned away from the self, आत्मस्वरूपविमुखान् पश्यन् शोचति. Śaṅkara’s introduction to the vallī says it shows what obstructs the ‘foremost intellect’ named at 1.3.12, कः पुनः प्रतिबन्धोऽग्र्याया बुद्धेः, since an obstacle must be known before one can try to remove it, विज्ञाते हि श्रेयःप्रतिबन्धकारणे तदपनयनाय यत्न आरब्धुं शक्यते.
paśyati or paśyanti
Raṅgarāmānuja comments on a plural, ‘they see’, and explains the singular of the other reading as referring to people at large: पराङ् पश्यति इति पाठे लोकाभिप्रायम् एकवचनम्. All our witnesses read paśyati.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Attention

Most of what competes for attention is outside us, and the verse treats that as natural rather than as a failing. What it asks is whether one ever turns the attention round to the one attending. (A modern reflection.)

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin When did you last notice the one who was noticing?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Why does the verse say the self is missed because of how the senses are made, not because it is far away?

A contemplation exercise For one minute, let the sounds around you continue, and attend instead to the awareness in which they are heard.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
