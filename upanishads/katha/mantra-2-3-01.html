Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 2 · Vallī 3 · Mantra 1 of 18

The eternal aśvattha tree

Roots above, branches below: the beginningless aśvattha tree of the world; its root is the bright one, Brahman, the immortal, on which all worlds rest and which none goes beyond.

5 Faithful English translation

With root above, branches below, this aśvattha [is] eternal. That alone [is] the bright, that [is] Brahman, that alone is called the immortal. In that all worlds rest; that indeed none goes beyond. This truly [is] that.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Its roots above and its branches below, this aśvattha tree is beginningless. Its root is the bright one, Brahman; it alone is called the immortal. All worlds rest on it, and nothing goes beyond it. This is that.

Key concepts अश्वत्थ aśvattha, the sacred fig ब्रह्मन् Brahman अमृत immortality, the deathless संसार saṃsāra, the round of births लोक world, realm

7 Explain it to a beginner

The last section of the Kaṭha opens with a picture: an upside-down tree. Its roots are high above and its branches spread down towards us. The tree is old beyond counting; it stands for the whole world of living beings and their round of birth and death. The verse then says that the root of this tree is what truly matters. It is bright and pure, it is Brahman, and it is the deathless one. All the worlds rest on it, and nothing can go beyond it. The last words, ‘this is that’, repeat a refrain we have met several times before. Śaṅkara says we learn about the hidden root by studying the visible tree, just as people trace a real tree down to its root. Raṅgarāmānuja and Madhva each read the root differently, but all three agree that the world depends on what is above it.

An analogy A banyan’s hanging roots look like branches; to find what feeds it, you follow them back to where they start.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse say?

The world is an eternal aśvattha tree with its root above and its branches below. Its root is the bright one, Brahman, the immortal; all worlds rest in it and none goes beyond it.

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara: the tree traced to its root

The vallī begins this way because, as one finds a tree’s root by examining the tree, so the root, Brahman, is found by examining the tree of saṃsāra, एवं संसारकार्यवृक्षावधारणेन तन्मूलस्य ब्रह्मणः स्वरूपावदिधारयिषया इयं षष्ठी वल्ली आरभ्यते. The tree reaches from the unmanifest to the immovable; it is called a tree because it is cut down, वृक्षश्च व्रश्चनात् विनश्वरत्वात्, and is felled at last by the weapon of non-attachment, वेदान्तविहितब्रह्मात्मदर्शनासङ्गशस्त्रकृतोच्छेदः. Its root alone is real, so the worlds rest in it as pots in clay: तदु तद्ब्रह्म नात्येति नातिवर्तते मृदादिकमिव घटादिकार्यं कश्चन कश्चिदपि विकारः.

Traditional Interpretation

Raṅgarāmānuja: the root is Brahmā

The first line, he says, was explained by the Bhāṣyakāra in commenting on the Gītā (‘ūrdhvamūlam adhaḥśākham’), and he quotes that explanation: the tree’s root is above because it begins with the four-faced Brahmā set above the seven worlds, सप्तलोकोपरिनिविष्ट चतुर्मुखादित्वेनतस्य ऊर्ध्वमूलत्वम्, and its branches are below because it ends in every creature on earth, पृथिवीनिवासि सकलनरपशुमृगक्रिमिकीटपतङ्गस्थावरान्ततया अधश्शाखत्वम्. The rest of the verse shows that Brahman is different from this tree, तद्विलक्षणमेव ब्रह्मेति दर्शयति; it was already explained at 2.2.8, पूर्वमेव व्याकृतोऽयं मन्त्रः.

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva: Viṣṇu the ground of the tree

In verses with no source named, Madhva makes Viṣṇu the ground in which the tree of the world stands, the gods its branches and lower beings below them: सर्वोच्चो भगवान् विष्णुर्मूलं भूमिवदस्य तु । जगदाख्यस्य वृक्षस्य शाखा देवास्ततोऽधमाः. Ramā is the root of the tree; Hari, ‘the swift’, gives it its name, वृक्षमूलं रमादेवी सोऽश्व आशुगतेर्हरिः. The world is eternal only as a stream; Hari alone is the immortal in the chief sense, मुख्यामृतस्स एवैको जगन्नित्यं प्रवाहतः.

Comparative Vedānta

The Gītā’s tree

The Gītā takes up the same image, ऊर्ध्वमूलमधःशाखमश्वत्थं प्राहुरव्ययम्, and tells the reader to cut the well-rooted tree with the strong weapon of non-attachment, असङ्गशस्त्रेण दृढेन छित्त्वा, the image Śaṅkara uses here.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

A repeated half-verse

The second and third lines repeat the second half of 2.2.8, तदेव शुक्रं तद्ब्रह्म तदेवामृतमुच्यते, and the refrain etad vai tat closes both verses. Raṅgarāmānuja notes that the passage has been explained already.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 2.3.1

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The tree of saṃsāra is unreal like a mirage or a city of the gandharvas; its root, Brahman, alone is real, and the worlds rest in it as effects in their material: तस्मिन् परमार्थसत्ये ब्रह्मणि लोकाः गन्धर्वनगरमरीच्युदकमायासमाः.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, Kaṭhopaniṣat-prakāśikā 2.3.1

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The tree begins with Brahmā above and ends in all creatures below; Brahman is different from it, तद्विलक्षणमेव ब्रह्मेति दर्शयति.

Dvaita · Madhva, Kāṭhakopaniṣad-bhāṣya 2.3.1

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Viṣṇu is the ground of the tree of the world, Ramā its root, the gods its branches; the world is eternal only as a stream, Hari the immortal in the chief sense: मुख्यामृतस्स एवैको जगन्नित्यं प्रवाहतः.

Where they disagree

All three read the tree as the world of beings. For Śaṅkara its root is Brahman and the tree is unreal apart from it; for Raṅgarāmānuja, through the Gītā commentary he quotes, the root above is Brahmā, and Brahman is different from the whole tree; for Madhva Viṣṇu is the ground, Ramā the root.

Morphology

अवाक्शाखः
avāc- (‘turned downward’) in compound with śākhā; the final c of avāc becomes k at the end of the first member, and the compound ends in -a as a bahuvrīhi.
अत्येति
ati + √i, present 3rd singular: ‘goes beyond, passes over’.
कश्चन
The interrogative kaḥ with the particle cana, ‘anyone at all’; with na, ‘no one’.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

The first line is a nominal sentence: aśvatthaḥ with three qualifiers, the verb ‘is’ understood. The second and third lines repeat, word for word, the second half of 2.2.8: three equations with tad eva, then two clauses about the worlds resting in it and none passing beyond it. The refrain etad vai tat closes the verse.

Ambiguities

What is the root above?
Śaṅkara: the highest place of Viṣṇu, Brahman, with the tree reaching from the unmanifest down to the immovable, सोऽयमव्यक्तादिस्थावरान्तः संसारवृक्षः ऊर्ध्वमूलः. Raṅgarāmānuja, quoting the Bhāṣyakāra on the Gītā, takes the root as the four-faced Brahmā set above the seven worlds, सप्तलोकोपरिनिविष्ट चतुर्मुखादित्वेनतस्य ऊर्ध्वमूलत्वम्, and Brahman as different from the tree. Madhva makes Viṣṇu the ground in which the root stands and Ramā the root of the tree.
The name aśvattha
Śaṅkara takes the aśvattha as the restless tree, shaken by the wind of desire and action. Madhva derives it from aśva, ‘the swift-going’ Hari, सोऽश्व आशुगतेर्हरिः; the commentators do not agree on the etymology.

Metre

anuṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 15 + 16 + 16 + 4

Counted as written, 15, 16, 16 and 4 syllables. The first pāda has seven because ūrdhvamūlo ’vākśākha merges the initial a of avākśākha; written apart, as GRETIL does, it has eight. The closing etad vai tat (4) is the refrain outside the metre.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Science and wonder

Pictures of the tree of life usually put the root at the bottom. Turning it upside down asks where anything draws its life from. This is a reflection, not a claim made by the text.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin What would it mean for a tree to have its roots in the sky?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Śaṅkara finds the root by studying the tree. Can the source of something be known from what grows out of it?

A contemplation exercise Sit by a tree and follow its trunk down with your eyes to where it meets the ground.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.

  • interpretation The root above is Brahman (Śaṅkara), the four-faced Brahmā, with Brahman different from the tree (Raṅgarāmānuja, quoting the Gītā commentary), or Ramā, with Viṣṇu as the ground (Madhva).
