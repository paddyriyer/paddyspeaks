Naciketas at the House of Death

What remains of a person after death? Why is the good not the same as the pleasant? What is it that is ‘not born and does not die’? Who is fit to be taught it, and why can it not be learnt by cleverness or much hearing alone?

The name and its place

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The Upaniṣad belongs to the Kaṭha school of the Kṛṣṇa Yajurveda; its parts are called vallīs, ‘creepers’, as in the Taittirīya. Śaṅkara begins his commentary by calling it ‘the vallīs of the Kāṭhaka Upaniṣad’ and promising a short explanation: अथ काठकोपनिषद्वल्लीनां सुखार्थप्रबोधनार्थमल्पग्रन्था वृत्तिरारभ्यते.

He also explains the word upaniṣad itself, from the root sad with upa and ni: सदेर्धातोर्विशरणगत्यवसादनार्थस्योपनिपूर्वस्य क्विप्प्रत्ययान्तस्य रूपमिदम् — the knowledge that loosens, leads and destroys the seed of rebirth.

Why the Kaṭha matters

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara’s first words salute the two figures of the dialogue as teacher and pupil of the knowledge of Brahman: ॐ नमो भगवते वैवस्वताय मृत्यवे ब्रह्मविद्याचार्याय, नचिकेतसे च — ‘to Death, son of Vivasvat, teacher of the knowledge of Brahman, and to Naciketas’.

He names the goal from the Upaniṣad’s own words: one who has perceived the self is freed from the mouth of Death — निचाय्य तं मृत्युमुखात्प्रमुच्यते — and Naciketas, at the end, attains Brahman — ब्रह्म प्राप्तो विरजोऽभूद्विमृत्युः. Raṅgarāmānuja opens his Prakāśikā with the modest promise to explain ‘the Kaṭha vallīs as best he can’: व्याख्यास्ये विदुषां प्रीत्यै कठवल्लीं यथामति.

A narrative introduction

Direct Textual Meaning

A father gives away all he owns at a sacrifice, but the cows are worn out. His son asks to whom he himself will be given, and the father, angry, answers: to Death. The boy waits three nights at Death’s house without food; Death offers three boons. The boy asks for his father’s good will, for the fire that leads to heaven, and for knowledge of what remains of a man after death. Death tries to withdraw the last, offering every pleasure instead; the boy refuses them all.

Then Death teaches. The good and the pleasant are different, and the wise choose the good: श्रेयश्च प्रेयश्च मनुष्यमेतः. The self is not born and does not die. It is the rider of a chariot whose horses are the senses: आत्मानं रथिनं विद्धि शरीरं रथमेव तु. The senses face outward, and the wise turn within. The world is a tree whose root is above: ऊर्ध्वमूलोऽवाक्शाख एषोऽश्वत्थः सनातनः. The last mantra says that Naciketas, having received this knowledge from Death, attained Brahman: मृत्युप्रोक्तां नचिकेतोऽथ लब्ध्वा.

The Kaṭha and the Gītā

Comparative Vedānta

Two mantras of the second vallī recur almost word for word in the second chapter of the Bhagavad Gītā. The second half of 1.2.18, अजो नित्यः शाश्वतोऽयं पुराणो न हन्यते हन्यमाने शरीरे, is the second half of Gītā 2.20; and 1.2.19 ends, as Gītā 2.19 does, उभौ तौ न विजानीतो नायं हन्ति न हन्यते.

The first halves differ, and the shared wording does not by itself show which text drew on which. Both mantras are analysed with the second vallī; the sixth study sets the two texts side by side.

  • Bhagavad Gītā 2.20: अजो नित्यः शाश्वतोऽयं पुराणो न हन्यते हन्यमाने शरीरे ajo nityaḥ śāśvato'yaṃ purāṇo na hanyate hanyamāne śarīre
  • Bhagavad Gītā 2.19: उभौ तौ न विजानीतो नायं हन्ति न हन्यते ubhau tau na vijānīto nāyaṃ hanti na hanyate

Three commentaries, three questions

Comparative Vedānta

The commentators read the same words as asking different things. At the third boon, Śaṅkara takes the doubt to be whether a self exists beyond body, senses and mind, and says the teaching that follows is the knowledge of the oneness of self and Brahman — ब्रह्मात्मैकत्वविज्ञानं. Raṅgarāmānuja takes ‘one who has gone’ to be the released self: सर्वबन्धविनिर्मोक्षाभिप्रायम्. Madhva glosses the question as about the Lord present in the released as their controller: प्रेते मुक्ते मनुष्ये नियामकत्वेन भगवानस्तीति ज्ञानिनो वदन्ति.

This edition gives each reading in the commentator’s own words, says where they differ, and does not make any one of them the meaning of the text.

What you will learn

  • Read the first adhyāya in Sanskrit with a transliteration you can trust, and know what each word is doing.
  • Tell the story of Naciketas — the sacrifice, the gift to Death, the three nights and the three boons — and say what each boon asks for.
  • Explain how Śaṅkara, Raṅgarāmānuja and Madhva’s school read the third question differently: a self beyond the body, the released self, or the Lord in the released.
  • Say why the commentators read Death’s offers as a test, and what the boy’s refusal shows about who is fit to be taught.
  • Say what separates the good from the pleasant, why the teaching is said to need a teacher and not reasoning alone, and how the commentators read ‘whom he chooses’.
  • Explain the image of the chariot and the ladder from the senses to the person, and say how Śaṅkara, Raṅgarāmānuja and Madhva read the two in the cave and the ‘unmanifest’ — and why none of them takes it as Sāṅkhya’s primal matter.
  • Tell the ancient text from the modern voice explaining it, on any page.

A beginner’s reading guide

  1. Adhyāya 1, vallī 1: Naciketas and the three boons (analysed). The sacrifice, the gift to Death, three nights at Death’s door, the three boons, and the refusal of every reward.
  2. Adhyāya 1, vallī 2: the good and the pleasant (analysed). Death begins to teach: the two paths, the teacher and the limits of reasoning, Om, the self that is not born and does not die, and how it is — and is not — obtained.
  3. Adhyāya 1, vallī 3: the chariot (analysed). The self as the rider, the body as the chariot, the senses as horses; the ladder from the senses to the person beyond which there is nothing; ‘arise, awake’.
  4. Adhyāya 2, vallī 1: turning inward. The senses face outward; the wise turn their gaze within; what is here is there.
  5. Adhyāya 2, vallī 2: the city of eleven gates. The self in the body, the one in the many, and the light by which all else shines.
  6. Adhyāya 2, vallī 3: the eternal tree. The aśvattha with its roots above, the discipline of yoga, and Naciketas’ attaining of Brahman.

Special philosophical studies

  1. I. Naciketas: the story and its frame — Mantras 1.1.1–1.1.9: a sacrifice of everything, a gift of worn-out cows, a boy given to Death, and three nights without food at Death’s door (mantras 1.1.1, 1.1.2, 1.1.3, 1.1.4, 1.1.5, 1.1.6, 1.1.7, 1.1.8, 1.1.9)
  2. II. The three boons — Mantras 1.1.10–1.1.20: peace with the father, the fire that leads to heaven and why it bears Naciketas’ name, and the question of what remains after death (mantras 1.1.10, 1.1.11, 1.1.12, 1.1.13, 1.1.14, 1.1.15, 1.1.16, 1.1.17, 1.1.18, 1.1.19, 1.1.20)
  3. III. The refusal: tests of the seeker — Mantras 1.1.21–1.1.29: Death’s reluctance, his offers of sons, wealth, long life and pleasures, and the boy’s answers (mantras 1.1.21, 1.1.22, 1.1.23, 1.1.24, 1.1.25, 1.1.26, 1.1.27, 1.1.28, 1.1.29)
  4. IV. The good and the pleasant — Mantras 1.2.1–1.2.9: the two that bind a person, knowledge and ignorance, the blind leading the blind, and why this teaching cannot be reached by reasoning alone (mantras 1.2.1, 1.2.2, 1.2.3, 1.2.4, 1.2.5, 1.2.6, 1.2.7, 1.2.8, 1.2.9)
  5. V. The hidden one and the syllable Om — Mantras 1.2.10–1.2.17: the Nāciketa fire and the permanent, what the boy let go, the god hard to see in the cave, the question beyond dharma and adharma, and the word all the Vedas proclaim (mantras 1.2.10, 1.2.11, 1.2.12, 1.2.13, 1.2.14, 1.2.15, 1.2.16, 1.2.17)
  6. VI. The unborn self and the one he chooses — Mantras 1.2.18–1.2.25: the self that is not born and does not die, the Gītā’s version, smaller than the small, the calm of the faculties or the grace of the Creator, and ‘not by instruction… whom he chooses’ (mantras 1.2.18, 1.2.19, 1.2.20, 1.2.21, 1.2.22, 1.2.23, 1.2.24, 1.2.25)
  7. VII. Two in the cave — Mantras 1.3.1–1.3.2: the two who drink the truth, shade and sunlight, and the bridge for those who would cross (mantras 1.3.1, 1.3.2)
  8. . The chariot — Mantras 1.3.3–1.3.9: the self as the rider, the body as the chariot, the senses as horses, the one who enjoys, the trained and the untrained horses, and the end of the road (mantras 1.3.3, 1.3.4, 1.3.5, 1.3.6, 1.3.7, 1.3.8, 1.3.9)
  9. . The ladder and the razor’s edge — Mantras 1.3.10–1.3.17: from the senses to the person beyond which there is nothing, the ‘unmanifest’ and the Sāṅkhya question, the self hidden in all beings, restraint stage by stage, ‘arise, awake’, the soundless, and the close of the first adhyāya (mantras 1.3.10, 1.3.11, 1.3.12, 1.3.13, 1.3.14, 1.3.15, 1.3.16, 1.3.17)
