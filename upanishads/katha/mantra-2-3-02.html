Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 2 · Vallī 3 · Mantra 2 of 18

The great fear, the raised thunderbolt

Whatever there is, the whole world, has come out of the breath and trembles in it; it is a great fear, a raised thunderbolt; those who know it become immortal.

5 Faithful English translation

Whatever this whole world [is], having come forth, trembles in the breath. A great fear, a raised thunderbolt [is it]; those who know this become immortal.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

This whole world, whatever there is, has come out of the breath and trembles in it. It is a great terror, a thunderbolt raised to strike. Those who know it become immortal.

Key concepts प्राण breath, life-force ब्रह्मन् Brahman जगत् world, ‘the moving’ अमृत immortality, the deathless

7 Explain it to a beginner

This verse says why the world keeps to its order. Everything that exists came out of the ‘breath’, which the commentators all take to mean the highest reality, not ordinary breathing, and everything trembles in it. That reality is called a great terror, like a thunderbolt raised to strike. Śaṅkara explains with an image: servants keep to their duties when they see their master standing with a raised weapon, and in the same way the sun, moon, planets and stars keep to their courses without ever resting. The fear is not cruelty; it is the steady order that holds everything in place. The verse ends with a promise found elsewhere in the Upaniṣads: those who know this become immortal.

An analogy An orchestra keeps time because the conductor’s baton is raised, even before it falls.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse say?

The whole world has come out of the breath and trembles in it; that is a great fear, a raised thunderbolt; those who know it become immortal.

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara: the master with the raised weapon

The verse answers the view that the world comes from nothing: it trembles because Brahman, the prāṇa, exists, प्राणे परस्मिन्ब्रह्मणि सति एजति कम्पते. Brahman is the great fear, that from which all fear, like a raised thunderbolt: as servants keep to their master’s orders when he faces them with a thunderbolt in his raised hand, यथा वज्रोद्यतकरं स्वामिनम् अभिमुखीभूतं दृष्ट्वा भृत्या नियमेन तच्छासने वर्तन्ते, so the world with its moon, sun, planets and stars keeps to its order without a moment’s rest. Those who know this one Brahman, witness of all that moves in them, become immortal.

Traditional Interpretation

Raṅgarāmānuja: the world trembles from fear

He reports that this verse was explained by the Bhāṣyakāra under the sūtra kampanāt, and quotes that explanation and the Śrutaprakāśikā on it, as quoted by him. The whole world, standing in the thumb-sized person called prāṇa and come out of him, trembles from great fear: कृत्स्नस्य जगतः अस्मिन् अङ्गुष्ठमात्रे पुरुषे प्राणशब्दनिर्दिष्टेस्थितानां. The trembling is each thing going about its own work for fear of failing, प्रत्यवायभयात् स्वस्वकार्येषु प्रवृत्तिः कम्पनम्. He also reports another view, that the Supreme himself makes the world tremble, केचित्तु.

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva: fear of overstepping one’s dharma

Madhva identifies the prāṇa with Hari, in whom all moves and from whom all has come forth; he is like a thunderbolt, giving fear when one oversteps one’s own dharma: प्राणाख्ये तु हरौ सर्वमेजत्यस्माच्च निस्सृतम् । वज्रवद्भयदं चैव स्वधर्मस्यातिलङ्घने.

Comparative Vedānta

The sūtra kampanāt

The Brahmasūtra discusses this verse under the sūtra कम्पनात्, and Śaṅkara’s Brahmasūtra-bhāṣya quotes it there and asks what the prāṇa and the thunderbolt are: कोऽसौ प्राणः, किं तद्भयानकं वज्रम्. Raṅgarāmānuja numbers the sūtra 1.3.40.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 2.3.2

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The prāṇa is the supreme Brahman, cause of the world; the world keeps its order as servants before a master with a raised thunderbolt: तथेदं चन्द्रादित्यग्रहनक्षत्रतारकादिलक्षणं जगत्सेश्वरं नियमेन क्षणमप्यविश्रान्तं वर्तत इत्युक्तं भवति.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, Kaṭhopaniṣat-prakāśikā 2.3.2

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The prāṇa is the supreme Self; mahad bhayaṃ vajram udyatam are nominatives in the sense of the ablative, so the whole world trembles from great fear of him, as from a raised thunderbolt: उद्यतवज्रादिव परमपुरुषात् सञ्जातेन भयेन, कृत्स्नं जगत् कम्पते इत्यर्थः.

Dvaita · Madhva, Kāṭhakopaniṣad-bhāṣya 2.3.2

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The prāṇa is Hari, in whom all moves; like a thunderbolt he gives fear when one oversteps one’s dharma: वज्रवद्भयदं चैव स्वधर्मस्यातिलङ्घने.

Where they disagree

All three take the prāṇa as the supreme. They differ in grammar and emphasis: Śaṅkara makes the four nominatives describe Brahman as the fear-giver; Raṅgarāmānuja reads them as ablatives, ‘from great fear’; Madhva ties the fear to transgressing one’s own dharma.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • यदिदं yadidaṃ → यत् + इदम्

    t before a vowel; m before a consonant

    The final t becomes d before a vowel, P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते); final m before a consonant becomes anusvāra, P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः).

  • किञ्च kiñca → किम् + च

    m before c

    Final m becomes anusvāra, P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः), and the anusvāra takes the place of the following stop, ñ before c, P. 8.4.58 (अनुस्वारस्य ययि परसवर्णः).

  • जगत्सर्वं jagatsarvaṃ → जगत् + सर्वम्

    t before s; m before a consonant

    A final t stays before s; final m before a consonant becomes anusvāra, P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः).

  • प्राण एजति prāṇa ejati → प्राणे + एजति

    e before a vowel

    Final e before a vowel becomes ay, P. 6.1.78 (एचोऽयवायावः), and the y is dropped, P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य); the hiatus that remains is not joined.

  • महद्भयं mahadbhayaṃ → महत् + भयम्

    t before a voiced consonant

    A final t becomes d before a voiced sound, P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते).

  • वज्रमुद्यतं य vajramudyataṃ ya → वज्रम् + उद्यतम् + ये

    m before a vowel; m before a consonant; e before a vowel

    Final m stays before a vowel and is joined to it; before a consonant it becomes anusvāra, P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः). Final e of ye before e becomes ay with the y dropped, P. 6.1.78 (एचोऽयवायावः), P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य).

  • एतद्विदुरमृतास्ते etadviduramṛtāste → एतत् + विदुः + अमृताः + ते

    t before a voiced consonant; uḥ before a vowel; āḥ before t

    The final t becomes d, P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते); after u the visarga becomes r before a voiced sound, P. 8.2.66 (ससजुषो रुँः); before t the visarga becomes s, P. 8.3.34 (विसर्जनीयस्य सः).

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this mantra.

Morphology

एजति
√ej, ‘to stir, tremble’; Raṅgarāmānuja cites the root list: एजृ कम्पने.
निःसृतम्
nis + √sṛ, past participle: ‘gone out, issued forth’.
विदुः
The perfect of √vid used with present meaning, 3rd plural: ‘they know’.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

The first line is one clause: yad idaṃ kiṃ ca jagat sarvam (subject), prāṇe (locative) ejati, with niḥsṛtam qualifying the subject. The second line begins with four nominatives, mahad bhayaṃ vajram udyatam, that the commentators construe differently (see ambiguities), then a relative clause, ye etad viduḥ, answered by amṛtās te bhavanti.

Ambiguities

How are mahad bhayaṃ vajram udyatam construed?
Śaṅkara takes them as predicates of the Brahman just named: it is a great fear, that from which all fear, बिभेत्यस्मादिति महद्भयम्, like a raised thunderbolt. Raṅgarāmānuja reports, from the Bhāṣyakāra’s comment on the sūtra kampanāt as he quotes it, that the four words are nominatives used for the ablative, अत्र महद्भयं वज्रमुद्यतम् इति चत्वारि पदानि पञ्चम्यर्थप्रथमान्तानि: the world trembles from great fear, as from a raised thunderbolt.
prāṇe: what is the breath?
All three commentators take prāṇa here as the supreme, not the vital air. Śaṅkara: प्राणे परस्मिन्ब्रह्मणि सति; Raṅgarāmānuja: the word is a locative, and the prāṇa is the supreme Self; Madhva: Hari called Prāṇa, प्राणाख्ये तु हरौ.

Metre

irregular · syllables counted as written: 17 + 21

Counted as written, 17 and 21 syllables. The first line is an anuṣṭubh half-verse with a ninth syllable in the first pāda (yad idaṃ kiñca jagat sarvam). The second line, mahad bhayaṃ vajram udyatam (9) with ya etad vidur amṛtās te bhavanti (12), does not scan; the last words recur at 2.3.9.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Science and wonder

Physics describes a universe that keeps to its laws without exception. The verse pictures that regularity as a world held in awe. This is a reflection, not a claim made by the text.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin What keeps the sun and the seasons to their order?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Is the ‘fear’ here a threat, or another name for an order that cannot be broken?

A contemplation exercise Watch something regular today, a clock, a tide table, the sunset, and notice how much depends on its keeping time.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
