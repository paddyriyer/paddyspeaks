Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 1 · Vallī 3 · Mantra 16 of 17

The story of Naciketas, told and heard

The wise one who tells and who hears this ancient story of Naciketas, told by Death, is honoured in the world of Brahman.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

The wise one who tells, or hears, this ancient story of Naciketas as Death told it is honoured in the world of Brahman.

Key concepts मेधा medhā, retentive intelligence ब्रह्मन् Brahman लोक world, realm मृत्यु death

7 Explain it to a beginner

The first half of the Kaṭha is ending, and the text steps back to praise the story it has told. It calls it the story of Naciketas, as told by Death, and says it is ancient. A wise person who tells it to others, or hears it from a teacher, is honoured in the world of Brahman. Śaṅkara says the verse is there to praise the knowledge just taught, and he reads ‘the world of Brahman’ as Brahman itself: such a person becomes one with it. Raṅgarāmānuja adds that the story is ancient in a strong sense: it was not made up by anyone, not even by Death, who only passes it on. Verses like this one, and the next, are a traditional way of closing a section and inviting the hearer to keep the teaching alive.

An analogy Like a family story passed down for generations: no one remembers who first told it, and each telling keeps it alive.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse say?

It praises the story of Naciketas told by Death as ancient, and says that the wise one who tells or hears it is honoured in the world of Brahman.

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara: praise of the knowledge taught

The verse is spoken to praise the knowledge just taught: प्रस्तुतविज्ञानस्तुत्यर्थमाह श्रुतिः. The story is the three vallīs, वल्लीत्रयलक्षणं, ancient because Vedic; one tells it to brāhmaṇas and hears it from teachers. The world of Brahman is Brahman itself, and the wise one becomes one with it, the object of worship.

Traditional Interpretation

Raṅgarāmānuja: Death is only the speaker

He calls the verse a conclusion, उपसंहरति. ‘Told by Death’ means Death is its speaker, not its author: मृत्योः प्रवक्तृत्वमेव न स्वतन्त्रवक्तृत्वम्. So it is ancient, eternal as a stream, because it has no human author: अपौरुषेयत्वात् प्रवाहरूपेण नित्यमित्यर्थः.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Where the first adhyāya ends

This verse and the next close the first adhyāya (the first three vallīs); Śaṅkara's colophon after 1.3.17 marks its end. The commentaries used in this edition go on to comment on the second adhyāya as the same text.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • नाचिकेतमुपाख्यानं nāciketamupākhyānaṃ → नाचिकेतम् + उपाख्यानम्

    m before a vowel; m before a consonant

    A final m before a vowel is kept and joined to it; before a consonant it becomes anusvāra, P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः).

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • मृत्युप्रोक्तम् mṛtyuproktam

    tṛtīyā-tatpuruṣa · मृत्यु + प्रोक्त

    Vigraha मृत्युना प्रोक्तम्

    told by Death

  • ब्रह्मलोके brahmaloke

    karmadhāraya (as Śaṅkara analyses it) · ब्रह्मन् + लोक

    Vigraha ब्रह्म एव लोकः

    the world that is Brahman

Morphology

नाचिकेतम्
A derivative of the name Naciketas with vṛddhi of the first syllable: ‘belonging to Naciketas’. The same form names the Nāciketa fire in 1.1.18 and 1.2.10; here it qualifies the story.
महीयते
From the stem mahīya-, ‘be great, be honoured’, related to √mah; the ending is middle and the sense passive or intransitive.

Anvaya

मेधावी मृत्युप्रोक्तम् सनातनम् नाचिकेतम् उपाख्यानम् उक्त्वा श्रुत्वा च ब्रह्मलोके महीयते ।

medhāvī mṛtyuproktam sanātanam nāciketam upākhyānam uktvā śrutvā ca brahmaloke mahīyate |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

One sentence: the object (the story, with three adjectives) stands first, then two absolutives joined by ca, the subject medhāvī, the locative and the verb.

Ambiguities

What is the ‘world of Brahman’?
Śaṅkara takes the compound as ‘the world that is Brahman’, ब्रह्मैव लोको ब्रह्मलोकः, and ‘is honoured’ as becoming one with it, the object of worship: आत्मभूत उपास्यो भवतीत्यभिप्रायः. The phrase can also be read as the world of Brahmā; Raṅgarāmānuja's surviving comment does not discuss it.
‘Ancient’ in what sense?
Śaṅkara: ancient because it is Vedic, चिरन्तनं वैदिकत्वात्. Raṅgarāmānuja: eternal as a stream, because it is not of human authorship, and Death only its speaker: मृत्योः प्रवक्तृत्वमेव न स्वतन्त्रवक्तृत्वम्.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Learning and apprenticeship

The verse honours two acts that keep a teaching alive: telling and hearing. A teaching that is only read in private is easily lost; one that is told and listened to is carried. Many learning traditions still rely on this. This is a reflection, not a claim made by the text.
