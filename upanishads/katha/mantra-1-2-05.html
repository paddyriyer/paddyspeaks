Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 1 · Vallī 2 · Mantra 5 of 25

The blind leading the blind

Those in the midst of ignorance, wise in their own eyes, wander crookedly like the blind led by the blind.

5 Faithful English translation

‘Dwelling in the midst of ignorance, wise by their own [reckoning], thinking [themselves] learned, the deluded wander about, running crookedly to and fro, like the blind led by a blind man.’

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

‘Living in the thick of ignorance, wise in their own eyes and sure of their learning, the deluded go round and round, stumbling this way and that, like blind people led by someone who is blind.’

Key concepts अविद्या avidyā धीर the wise मोह delusion तमस् darkness

7 Explain it to a beginner

Having praised Naciketas, Death describes the opposite kind of person. These people live in the middle of ignorance, as if in thick darkness, but they do not know it. They think they are wise and well read. So they go round and round, stumbling from one trouble to another, through old age, sickness and death, and back again. Death compares them to a line of blind people being led by someone who is also blind: everyone is sure of the way, and nobody can see it. The sharpest point of the verse is not that these people lack knowledge, but that they are certain they have it. That certainty is what keeps them from looking for a teacher who can see.

An analogy Like a line of blind travellers, each holding the shoulder of the one in front, led confidently by a man who cannot see either.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse describe?

People who live within ignorance yet think themselves wise and learned; they wander crookedly, like the blind led by the blind.

Traditional Interpretation

Who are these people?

Śaṅkara: those bound to worldly existence, enmeshed by hundreds of nooses of craving for sons, cattle and the rest, वेष्ट्यमानाः पुत्रपश्वादितृष्णापाशशतैः; like many blind people led on a rough road by one who is blind, they come to great harm: विषमे पथि यथा बहवः अन्धाः महान्तमनर्थमृच्छन्ति, तद्वत्.

Traditional Interpretation

How does Raṅgarāmānuja place the verse?

As the censure of the path of ignorance, the first of the two paths named in 1.2.4: ‘अविद्या या च विद्येति ज्ञाता’ इत्युपात्तमार्गद्वये अविद्यामार्गं निन्दति. Ignorance here is desire-prompted ritual and the like, काम्यकर्मादिलक्षणायाम् अविद्यायां.

Comparative Vedānta

Where else does this verse occur?

Almost word for word in the Muṇḍaka (1.2.8), where it is said of those who trust ritual alone; the Muṇḍaka reads jaṅghanyamānāḥ for dandramyamāṇāḥ. Both close with अन्धेनैव नीयमाना यथान्धाः.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 1.2.5

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The deluded are those caught in the darkness of ignorance, who think themselves learned in scripture and so never seek one who sees: स्वयं धीराः प्रज्ञावन्तः पण्डिताः शास्त्रकुशलाश्चेति मन्यमानाः.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, Kaṭhopaniṣat-prakāśikā 1.2.5

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Ignorance is desire-prompted ritual; those inside it, thinking themselves learned, wander afflicted by age and disease: जरारोगादि दुःखपीडिताः अविवेकिनः परिभ्रमन्ति.

Dvaita

No position is given for this school here: the edition does not force one.

Where they disagree

No real disagreement: both read the verse as the censure of the life of the pleasant. Raṅgarāmānuja, like the Muṇḍaka’s context, identifies that life with desire-prompted ritual. Madhva comments on the passage 1.2.4–8 only at 1.2.8.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • अविद्यायामन्तरे avidyāyāmantare → अविद्यायाम् + अन्तरे

    final m before a vowel

    The m stays before a vowel.

  • स्वयं धीराः svayaṃ dhīrāḥ → स्वयम् + धीराः

    final m before a consonant

    P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः).

  • पण्डितम्मन्यमानाः paṇḍitammanyamānāḥ → पण्डितम् + मन्यमानाः

    final m before m

    Before a consonant the m may become anusvāra, P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः); before m the anusvāra is pronounced as m, and the base writes it so, joined to the next word. GRETIL writes paṇḍitaṃ manyamānāḥ apart (corrections.json): the same letters.

  • मूढा अन्धेनैव mūḍhā andhenaiva → मूढाः + अन्धेन + एव

    āḥ before a vowel; vṛddhi

    mūḍhāḥ before a: the visarga becomes y, P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि), and is dropped, P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य); the hiatus stays. andhena + eva: a + e give ai, P. 6.1.88 (वृद्धिरेचि).

  • नीयमाना यथान्धाः nīyamānā yathāndhāḥ → नीयमानाः + यथा + अन्धाः

    āḥ before a voiced consonant; savarṇa-dīrgha

    nīyamānāḥ before y: the visarga becomes y, P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि), dropped before a consonant, P. 8.3.22 (हलि सर्वेषाम्). yathā + andhāḥ: ā + a give ā, P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः).

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this mantra.

Morphology

दन्द्रम्यमाणाः
Present participle of the intensive of dram ‘run about’. The intensive of a verb of motion means crooked motion, P. 3.1.23 (नित्यं कौटिल्ये गतौ) — Śaṅkara’s gloss कुटिलामनेकरूपां गतिं says the same — and the reduplicating syllable of a root ending in a nasal takes n, P. 7.4.85 (नुगतोऽनुनासिकान्तस्य): dan-dram-ya-māṇa.
पण्डितम्
An accusative singular with a plural subject: each of them thinks himself learned. The base writes it joined to manyamānāḥ.
नीयमानाः
Present passive participle of nī ‘lead’: ‘being led’, with the agent in the instrumental, andhena.

Anvaya

अविद्यायाम् अन्तरे वर्तमानाः, स्वयम् धीराः पण्डितम् मन्यमानाः मूढाः, अन्धेन एव नीयमानाः अन्धाः यथा, दन्द्रम्यमाणाः परियन्ति ।

avidyāyām antare vartamānāḥ, svayam dhīrāḥ paṇḍitam manyamānāḥ mūḍhāḥ, andhena eva nīyamānāḥ andhāḥ yathā, dandramyamāṇāḥ pariyanti |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

One sentence. The subject mūḍhāḥ and the verb pariyanti are surrounded by participles: two describing their state (vartamānāḥ, manyamānāḥ), one their manner of going (dandramyamāṇāḥ). The comparison closes the verse: yathā andhāḥ andhena eva nīyamānāḥ, ‘as the blind [are] led by a blind man’.

Ambiguities

dandramyamāṇāḥ and its variants
Śaṅkara reads dandramyamāṇāḥ and explains crooked, many-shaped wandering through the pains of age, death and disease: अत्यर्थं कुटिलामनेकरूपां गतिं गच्छन्तः जरामरणरोगादिदुःखैः. Raṅgarāmānuja’s printed text has दन्द्रभ्यमाणाः (a reading or an error of the 1948 print or its OCR), glossed जरारोगादि दुःखपीडिताः, ‘afflicted by age, disease and pain’; he reports that others read दन्द्रव्यमाणाः and explain it as ‘with minds melted by the fire of desire for objects’: विषयकामाग्निना द्रुतचित्ताः. The Muṇḍaka’s version of the verse reads jaṅghanyamānāḥ, ‘being struck again and again’.
svayaṃ dhīrāḥ
svayam goes with the self-estimate: they are wise ‘by themselves’, in their own view. Śaṅkara: स्वयं धीराः प्रज्ञावन्तः पण्डिताः शास्त्रकुशलाश्चेति मन्यमानाः.

Metre

triṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 22 + 22

Two half-lines of 22 syllables (11 + 11), regular. The hiatus mūḍhā andhena is counted as written.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Ego and self-image

Ordinary experience bears out a point the verse makes sharply: the people most certain they understand are often the least inclined to check. A confident guide who cannot see is more dangerous than an uncertain one, because others follow. The verse turns the warning inward: the question is not whether others are blind, but whether I am sure of a road I have never seen. This is a reflection, not part of the commentaries.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Have you ever followed someone confidently in the wrong direction?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Why might believing oneself learned be a greater obstacle than simply not knowing?

A contemplation exercise Pick one opinion you hold firmly and ask where you first learned it, and whether that source could see.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
