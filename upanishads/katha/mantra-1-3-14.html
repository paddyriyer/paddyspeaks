Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 1 · Vallī 3 · Mantra 14 of 17

Arise, awake

Arise, awake; having reached the excellent ones, understand. The wise call that path hard to cross, a sharpened razor's edge.

5 Faithful English translation

Arise, wake; having approached the excellent ones, understand. A razor's edge, sharpened, hard to cross — that path [is] hard to go, the wise say.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Arise, awake; go to those who are excellent and learn. The wise say this path is hard to tread, like the sharpened edge of a razor, hard to cross.

Key concepts आचार्य ācārya, teacher ज्ञान knowledge कवि seer, poet अविद्या avidyā

7 Explain it to a beginner

This is one of the best-known lines of the Upaniṣads. Death stops explaining for a moment and calls out to everyone listening, not only to the boy: Get up. Wake up. Go to people who truly know, and learn from them. Then he says honestly that the way is not easy. The wise compare it to walking along the sharpened edge of a razor. Śaṅkara explains the sleep as ignorance, the root of all trouble, and says the scripture calls out like a mother calling her child, because what has to be known is so fine that it is easily missed. Raṅgarāmānuja adds that on a razor's edge a moment's carelessness means a fall, and so on this path a little inattention can undo everything. Both say: do not be careless about it.

An analogy Like a mountain guide's warning before a narrow ridge: the view is worth it, but every step needs attention.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse say?

It calls on its hearers to arise, wake and learn from the excellent, and reports what the wise say of the path: hard to go, like a sharpened razor's edge, hard to cross.

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara: wake from the sleep of ignorance

Since the one who has dissolved everything into the Self is at rest and has done what is to be done, the hearers are told to wake for that seeing: उत्तिष्ठत हे जन्तवः, आत्मज्ञानाभिमुखा भवत. Waking is ending the sleep of ignorance, अज्ञाननिद्राया घोररूपायाः सर्वानर्थबीजभूतायाः क्षयं कुरुत; the excellent are teachers who know the truth. The śruti speaks out of compassion, like a mother: श्रुतिरनुकम्पया आह मातृवत्. The razor's edge shows how hard the way of knowledge is, because its object is so subtle.

Traditional Interpretation

Raṅgarāmānuja: the qualified are addressed; a moment's inattention

Having taught how the faculties are brought under control, the text turns to those qualified: एवं वशीकरणप्रकारमुपदिश्य अधिकारिपुरुषान् अभिमुखी करोति. The varāḥ are excellent teachers, or the boons. On the image he writes: तीक्ष्णक्षुराग्रे संचरतः पुंसो यथा कियत्यप्यनवधाने आत्मनाशो भवति, as one walking on a sharp razor's edge is destroyed by a little inattention, so here.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The plural

Death has spoken to Naciketas in the singular; here the three imperatives are plural (uttiṣṭhata, jāgrata, nibodhata). The verse addresses every hearer of the teaching.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 1.3.14

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

A call to wake from ignorance, the seed of all harm, and to know the inmost Self as ‘I am’ from teachers who know the truth: सर्वान्तरमात्मानमहमस्मीति निबोधत अवगच्छत.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, Kaṭhopaniṣat-prakāśikā 1.3.14

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

A call to those qualified to learn the truth of the self from excellent teachers, on a path where the least inattention is ruin: स्वल्पेऽपि अनवधानापराधे आत्मनाशो भवतीति भावः.

Dvaita

No position is given for this school here: the edition does not force one.

Where they disagree

The two agree on the call and on the difficulty; they differ on what is to be known — the inmost Self as one's own ‘I’ (Śaṅkara) or the truth of the self in relation to the Lord (Raṅgarāmānuja). Madhva's bhāṣya, in our source, has no comment on this verse.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • वरान्निबोधत varānnibodhata → वरान् + निबोधत

    n before n

    The final n of the accusative plural meets the initial n and both are written; nothing changes.

  • दुर्गं पथस्तत्कवयो वदन्ति durgaṃ pathastatkavayo vadanti → दुर्गम् + पथः + तत् + कवयः + वदन्ति

    m before a consonant; aḥ before t; aḥ before a voiced consonant

    The final m becomes anusvāra, P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः); the visarga of pathaḥ becomes s before t, P. 8.3.34 (विसर्जनीयस्य सः); that of kavayaḥ becomes u before the voiced v, P. 6.1.114 (हशि च), and a + u give o, P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः).

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • दुरत्यया duratyayā

    bahuvrīhi · दुस् + अत्यय

    Vigraha दुःखेन अत्ययः यस्याः सा

    whose crossing is hard (Śaṅkara's analysis)

Morphology

जाग्रत
Imperative second plural of the reduplicated √jāgṛ, ‘be awake’. All three verbs of the first line are second plural: the verse turns from Naciketas to every hearer.
पथः
From pathin, ‘path’, whose weak stem is path-; pathaḥ can be accusative plural or genitive singular. Śaṅkara takes it as the object, ‘the path’ (pantham), with tat.

Anvaya

उत्तिष्ठत, जाग्रत, वरान् प्राप्य निबोधत । कवयः तत् पथः दुर्गम् — क्षुरस्य निशिता दुरत्यया धारा [इव] — वदन्ति ।

uttiṣṭhata, jāgrata, varān prāpya nibodhata | kavayaḥ tat pathaḥ durgam — kṣurasya niśitā duratyayā dhārā iva — vadanti |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

The first line is three plural imperatives with an absolutive (prāpya varān). The second line is an image and a statement: the razor's edge (nominative, with two adjectives) and the clause kavayaḥ … vadanti, whose object is tat pathaḥ durgam. The comparison is implied, not marked by iva.

Ambiguities

Who are the varāḥ?
Śaṅkara: excellent teachers who know the truth, प्रकृष्टानाचार्यांस्तत्त्वविदः. Raṅgarāmānuja gives that reading, श्रेष्ठान् आचार्यान्, and a second: the boons — that is, the grace of the Lord or of those who know Brahman.
Who is addressed?
The verbs are plural. Śaṅkara takes the śruti itself as speaking to all beings, out of compassion, like a mother: श्रुतिरनुकम्पया आह मातृवत्. Raṅgarāmānuja: after teaching the means of control, the text turns to those qualified for it, अधिकारिपुरुषान् अभिमुखी करोति.
Why is the path like a razor's edge?
Śaṅkara: because what is to be known is so subtle, the way of knowledge is hard to accomplish: ज्ञेयस्यातिसूक्ष्मत्वात्तद्विषयस्य ज्ञानमार्गस्य दुःसम्पाद्यत्वं वदन्तीत्यभिप्रायः. Raṅgarāmānuja: because, as on a razor's edge, the slightest inattention is ruin.

Metre

irregular · syllables counted as written: 15 + 23

Counted as written, 15 and 23 syllables. The call of the first line (uttiṣṭhata jāgrata prāpya varān nibodhata) has fifteen and fits no regular pāda; the second line has a twelve-syllable pāda (kṣurasya dhārā niśitā duratyayā) and an eleven-syllable one (durgaṃ pathas tat kavayo vadanti), triṣṭubh-like.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Learning and apprenticeship

The verse puts three things together that are often separated: waking up to what matters, going to people who know, and accepting that the way is hard. Much modern learning offers the first and second without the third. The verse is frank that care and attention are part of the path. This is a reflection, not a claim made by the text.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin What would ‘waking up’ mean for you this week, in one concrete thing?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Why might a teaching about the self need a teacher at all? What can another person give that reasoning alone cannot (see 1.2.8–9)?

A contemplation exercise Choose one thing you want to understand well and find one person who knows it better than you. Ask them one good question.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
