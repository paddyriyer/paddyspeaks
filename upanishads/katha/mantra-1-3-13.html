Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 1 · Vallī 3 · Mantra 13 of 17

Speech into mind, mind into the self

The wise one should hold speech in the mind, the mind in the knowing self, the knowing self in the great self, and that in the peaceful self.

5 Faithful English translation

The wise one should hold speech in the mind; that [mind] he should hold in the knowing self; the knowing he should hold down in the great self; that he should hold in the peaceful self.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Let the wise one draw speech into the mind, the mind into the knowing self, the knowing self into the great self, and that into the self at peace.

Key concepts मनस् mind विज्ञान understanding, the knowing self आत्मन् self, Self शान्ति peace अध्यात्मयोग inward yoga

7 Explain it to a beginner

This verse gives a practice. The previous verse said the self is hidden and seen only by a fine, steady mind. Here is how such a mind is made: step by step, from the outside in. First, let speech settle into the mind: stop talking, outwardly and then inwardly. Then let the mind settle into the part of us that knows and decides. Let that settle into the great self, and let that come to rest in the self that is at peace. The steps are the same ladder as a few verses earlier, climbed back down from the outside toward the inside. Śaṅkara takes speech as standing for all the senses, and the peaceful self as the inmost Self, the witness of every thought. Raṅgarāmānuja takes the last step as resting the individual self in the Lord who dwells within it.

An analogy Like closing a set of nested doors from the outermost inward, until one stands in the quiet inner room.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse say?

It gives four steps of restraint, each held in the next: speech in the mind, the mind in the knowing self, the knowing self in the great self, and that in the peaceful self.

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara: the means of reaching it

The verse gives the means to the knowledge of 1.3.12: तत्प्रतिपत्त्युपायमाह. Speech stands for all the senses, वागत्रोपलक्षणार्था सर्वेषामिन्द्रियाणाम्. The mind is held in the intellect, ज्ञाने प्रकाशस्वरूपे बुद्धावात्मनि; the intellect in the first-born, आत्मनि महति प्रथमजे; and that in the peaceful Self, without any distinction or change, the witness of every thought.

Traditional Interpretation

Raṅgarāmānuja: the practice of inward yoga

He reads the verse as the practice promised by ‘inward yoga’ in 1.2.12: 'अध्यात्मयोगाधिगमेन' इति निर्दिष्ट जीवस्वरूपज्ञानप्रकारं दर्शयति. Quoting the Bhāṣyakāra, the last step holds the individual self as agent in the supreme Brahman, the inner controller of all, परस्मिन् ब्रह्मणि सर्वान्तर्यामिणि नियच्छेत्, and the chariot so driven is to reach Viṣṇu's place. He then reports a work he names, the Śrutaprakāśikā, on what each step means, and defends the Bhāṣyakāra's grammar against an objection.

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva: restraint as meditation on dependence

His comment holds each deity in the one above, up to Viṣṇu: तौ रमायां परानन्दे तां विष्णौ परमात्मनि. Restraint, it says, is nothing but meditating on each as dependent on the one above: तद्वशत्वेन तद्ध्यानं नियमो नाम नापरः. A verse he cites (इति च) asks how a man could restrain the gods who restrain him: कुतस्तु मानुषो देवान्नियच्छेद्विनियामकान्.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Grammar the commentators agree on

Both read vāk as an accusative without its ending and manasī as a Vedic locative: Śaṅkara, छान्दसं दैर्घ्यम्; Raṅgarāmānuja, quoting the Bhāṣyakāra, सप्तम्याश्छान्दसो दीर्घः. The extra tad yacchet makes the last pāda eleven syllables; Madhva's text lacks it.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 1.3.13

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The senses are withdrawn into the mind, the mind into the intellect, the intellect into Hiraṇyagarbha, and that into the peaceful Self, the witness of all thought: सर्वबुद्धिप्रत्ययसाक्षिणि मुख्ये आत्मनि.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, quoting the Bhāṣyakāra, Kaṭhopaniṣat-prakāśikā 1.3.13

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The practice of inward yoga, ending with the individual self held in the supreme Brahman, the inner controller: परस्मिन् ब्रह्मणि सर्वान्तर्यामिणि नियच्छेत्.

Dvaita · Madhva, Kāṭhakopaniṣad-bhāṣya 1.3.12–13

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Each presiding deity is held in the one above, up to Viṣṇu, and restraint is meditation on that dependence: तद्वशत्वेन तद्ध्यानं नियमो नाम नापरः.

Where they disagree

Whether the last step dissolves all into the one peaceful Self (Śaṅkara), rests the individual self in the Lord within it (Raṅgarāmānuja), or is meditation on a hierarchy of dependent deities up to Viṣṇu (Madhva).

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • यच्छेद्वाङ्मनसी yacchedvāṅmanasī → यच्छेत् + वाक् + मनसी

    t before a voiced consonant; k before a nasal

    The final t becomes d before v, P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते); the final k becomes the nasal ṅ before m, P. 8.4.45 (यरोऽनुनासिकेऽनुनासिको वा).

  • प्राज्ञस्तद् prājñastad → प्राज्ञः + तत्

    aḥ before t

    The visarga becomes s before t, P. 8.3.34 (विसर्जनीयस्य सः).

  • यच्छेज्ज्ञान आत्मनि yacchejjñāna ātmani → यच्छेत् + ज्ञाने + आत्मनि

    t before j; e before a vowel

    The final t becomes j before j, P. 8.4.40 (स्तोः श्चुना श्चुः); the e of jñāne becomes ay before ā and the y is dropped, P. 6.1.78 (एचोऽयवायावः) with P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य), leaving the hiatus jñāna ātmani.

  • नियच्छेत्तद्यच्छेच्छान्त आत्मनि niyacchettadyacchecchānta ātmani → नियच्छेत् + तत् + यच्छेत् + शान्ते + आत्मनि

    t before ś; e before a vowel

    The final t becomes c before ś, P. 8.4.40 (स्तोः श्चुना श्चुः), and the ś may become ch, P. 8.4.63 (शश्छोऽटि); śānte becomes śānta before ātmani as above.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this mantra.

Morphology

वाक्
Both commentators read vāk as the accusative vācam with its ending dropped. Raṅgarāmānuja, quoting the Bhāṣyakāra, cites P. 7.1.39 (सुपां सुलुक्पूर्वसवर्णाऽऽच्छेयाडाड्यायाजालः) for the dropping. Read as one word, vāṅmanasī would be a dual dvandva ‘speech and mind’; neither commentary takes it so.
मनसी
For the locative manasi, with the final i lengthened — a Vedic licence, as Śaṅkara (छान्दसं दैर्घ्यम्) and Raṅgarāmānuja (सप्तम्याश्छान्दसो दीर्घः) both say. Madhva's text reads manasi.
तत्
The second tat stands for the great self (masculine) but is neuter; Raṅgarāmānuja calls this an exchange of gender, a Vedic licence.

Anvaya

प्राज्ञः वाक् मनसी यच्छेत्, तत् ज्ञाने आत्मनि यच्छेत्, ज्ञानम् महति आत्मनि नियच्छेत्, तत् शान्ते आत्मनि यच्छेत् ।

prājñaḥ vāk manasī yacchet, tat jñāne ātmani yacchet, jñānam mahati ātmani niyacchet, tat śānte ātmani yacchet |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Four optative clauses with one subject, prājñaḥ. Each has an accusative (what is held) and a locative (where it is held); each locative becomes the next accusative, so the sentence climbs the ladder of 1.3.10–11 in reverse, from outer to inner.

Ambiguities

vāṅmanasī: one word or two?
Both commentators divide vāk + manasī: speech (accusative, ending dropped) into the mind (locative, lengthened). A reading as one word, ‘speech and mind’ (dual), would make speech and mind together the object; neither commentary adopts it, and Madhva's text reads manasi.
What are the four places?
Śaṅkara: the mind into the intellect, the intellect into the first-born Hiraṇyagarbha, that into the peaceful self, शान्ते सर्वविशेषप्रत्यस्तमितरूपे अविक्रिये सर्वान्तरे सर्वबुद्धिप्रत्ययसाक्षिणि मुख्ये आत्मनि. Raṅgarāmānuja, quoting the Bhāṣyakāra: the mind into the intellect, the intellect into the individual self as agent, and that self into the supreme Brahman, परस्मिन् ब्रह्मणि सर्वान्तर्यामिणि नियच्छेत्. Madhva reads a hierarchy of deities, each held in the one above.

Metre

anuṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 16 + 19

Counted as written, 16 and 19 syllables: the last pāda, niyacchet tad yacchec chānta ātmani, has eleven. Madhva's text lacks tad yacchet there, which gives eight (corrections.json).

Textual variations between witnesses

  • Variant reading Madhva’s mūla: यच्छेद् वाङ्मनसि प्राज्ञस्तद् — Madhva’s text reads vāṅmanasi, ‘[speech] into the mind’; the base’s vāṅmanasī means the same, the long ī being Vedic, as Śaṅkara notes (मनसी मनसि । छान्दसं दैर्घ्यम्).
  • Variant reading Madhva’s mūla: ज्ञानमात्मनि महति नियच्छेच्छान्त — Madhva’s text lacks tad yacchet in the third line (… niyacchec chānta ātmani for … niyacchet tad yacchec chānta ātmani).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Self-mastery

The verse describes settling the mind as a sequence rather than a single act: first stop speaking, then let thinking slow, then let deciding rest. People who try to silence the mind all at once often find this kind of stepwise approach easier. This is a reflection, not a claim made by the text.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Which is easier for you to quiet: your speech, your thoughts, or your decisions?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The verse runs the ladder of 1.3.10–11 from the outside in. Why might the way back be described as restraint rather than as climbing?

A contemplation exercise Sit for five minutes. First stop speaking aloud, then notice inner speech and let it slow; do not force it.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
