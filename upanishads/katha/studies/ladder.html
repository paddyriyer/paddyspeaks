The ladder and the razor’s edge

Mantras 1.3.10–1.3.17: from the senses to the person beyond which there is nothing, the ‘unmanifest’ and the Sāṅkhya question, the self hidden in all beings, restraint stage by stage, ‘arise, awake’, the soundless, and the close of the first adhyāya

Anuvākas 1.3.10, 1.3.11, 1.3.12, 1.3.13, 1.3.14, 1.3.15, 1.3.16, 1.3.17

The chariot is now read upward: beyond the senses are their objects, beyond those the mind, the intellect, the great self, the unmanifest, and the person — ‘beyond the person there is nothing’. The self is hidden in all beings and is seen only by a sharpened, subtle intellect; the way to it is to draw speech into mind, mind into intellect, intellect into the great self, and that into the self at peace. ‘Arise, awake’, says the text: the path is a razor’s edge. The vallī closes with the soundless, touchless, deathless self and a promise to those who tell the story. The commentators differ on almost every rung — and on whether the ‘unmanifest’ is Sāṅkhya’s primal matter, which the Brahmasūtra denies.

The ladder

Direct Textual Meaning

इन्द्रियेभ्यः परा ह्यर्था अर्थेभ्यश्च परं मनः — ‘beyond the senses are the objects, and beyond the objects the mind’; मनसस्तु परा बुद्धिर्बुद्धेरात्मा महान्परः — ‘beyond the mind the intellect, beyond the intellect the great self’. महतः परमव्यक्तमव्यक्तात्पुरुषः परः — ‘beyond the great, the unmanifest; beyond the unmanifest, the person’.

And then the ladder stops: पुरुषान्न परं किञ्चित्सा काष्ठा सा परा गतिः — ‘beyond the person there is nothing; that is the end, that is the highest goal’. Most of the rungs are the parts of the chariot named again. Two are new: the ‘great self’ and the ‘unmanifest’.

Subtler and more inward (Śaṅkara)

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara reads the series as a way of reaching the goal as one’s own inner self, rung by rung from the gross to the subtle: स्थूलान्यारभ्य सूक्ष्मतारतम्यक्रमेण प्रत्यगात्मतयाधिगमः कर्तव्य. Each rung is subtler, greater and more inward than the last — सूक्ष्मा महान्तश्च प्रत्यगात्मभूताश्च. The great self is the first-born principle of Hiraṇyagarbha: अव्यक्ताद्यत्प्रथमं जातं हैरण्यगर्भं तत्त्वं.

The unmanifest is the seed of the whole world, name and form not yet unfolded — सर्वस्य जगतो बीजभूतमव्याकृतनामरूपं — resting in the supreme self as the power of the banyan rests in its seed: वटकणिकायामिव वटवृक्षशक्तिः. The person is so called because he fills all, सर्वपूरणात्. With him subtlety, greatness and inwardness reach their end — सा काष्ठा निष्ठा पर्यवसानम् — and he is the highest goal, as the Gītā says of the place यद्गत्वा न निवर्तन्ते, ‘having gone to which they do not return’.

  • Bhagavad Gītā 15.6: यद्गत्वा न निवर्तन्ते तद्धाम परमं मम yadgatvā na nivartante taddhāma paramaṃ mama

Is the ‘unmanifest’ Sāṅkhya’s primal matter?

Academic History

The words mahat, avyakta and puruṣa, in that order, are also the names of the Sāṅkhya system, whose ‘unmanifest’ is primal matter, pradhāna, the independent cause of the world. The Brahmasūtra takes up the claim that this mantra teaches it, and denies it: आनुमानिकमप्येकेषामिति चेन्न शरीररूपकविन्यस्तगृहीतेर्दर्शयति च — ‘if it is said that the inferred [pradhāna] is found in the text of some [schools], no: what is meant is what was set in the image of the body’.

Śaṅkara’s commentary on the sūtra states the objection — the same names in the same order are recognised here, य एव यन्नामानो यत्क्रमाश्च महदव्यक्तपुरुषाः स्मृतिप्रसिद्धाः, त एवेह प्रत्यभिज्ञायन्ते — and answers that only the word is recognised, शब्दमात्रं ह्यत्राव्यक्तमिति प्रत्यभिज्ञायते: seeing a cow where a horse should be, no one in his senses takes it for a horse, न ह्यश्वस्थाने गां पश्यन्नश्वोऽयमित्यमूढोऽध्यवस्यति. Every other rung names a part of the chariot already given; the one part left over is the body, the chariot, and that is what ‘unmanifest’ means here: शरीरं ह्यत्र रथरूपकविन्यस्तमव्यक्तशब्देन परिगृह्यते, by context and by elimination, प्रकरणात् परिशेषाच्च. So there is no room here for the primal matter others imagine: नास्त्यत्र परपरिकल्पितस्य प्रधानस्यावकाशः.

Raṅgarāmānuja reaches the same answer through the commentary he calls the Bhāṣya — the meaning of these two mantras, he says, भगवता भाष्यकृता आनुमानिकाधिकरणे उक्तः — which he quotes at length: the senses and the rest are recognised by their own names, and by elimination the body is what the word ‘unmanifest’ names: परिशेषात् तत् अव्यक्तशब्देन उच्यते. In his commentary on the Kaṭha itself Śaṅkara glosses the unmanifest as the seed of the world, not yet unfolded; under the sūtra he takes it as the body. This edition records both and does not reconcile them on his behalf.

  • Brahmasūtra 1.4.1: आनुमानिकमप्येकेषामिति चेन्न शरीररूपकविन्यस्तगृहीतेर्दर्शयति च ānumānikamapyekeṣāmiti cenna śarīrarūpakavinyastagṛhīterdarśayati ca
  • Śaṅkara on Brahmasūtra 1.4.1: शरीरं ह्यत्र रथरूपकविन्यस्तमव्यक्तशब्देन परिगृह्यते śarīraṃ hyatra ratharūpakavinyastamavyaktaśabdena parigṛhyate

A ladder of control (Raṅgarāmānuja)

Traditional Interpretation

In the passage Raṅgarāmānuja quotes, the series ranks the parts of the chariot by what must be brought under control: वशीकार्यतायां प्रधानानि. Objects are ‘beyond’ the senses because even tamed senses are hard to hold in the presence of their objects; the mind is beyond the objects; the intellect beyond the mind; and the self pictured as the rider is beyond the intellect, because it is the agent: रथित्वेन रूपित आत्मा, कर्तृत्वेन प्राधान्यात्परः.

Beyond them all is the inner controller of all, the Supreme Person who is the end of the road: सर्वान्तरात्मभूतः अन्तर्यामी अध्वनः पारभूतः परमपुरुषः. And the way to ‘control’ him is to take refuge in him: तस्य च वशीकरणं तच्छरणागतिरेव — a point the passage supports from the Gītā’s ‘go to him alone for refuge’, तमेव शरणं गच्छ, as quoted there.

A ladder of presiding deities (Madhva)

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva reads the rungs as the deities who preside over them, each higher than the last. The deities of the objects are greater than those of the senses: देवेभ्य इन्द्रियात्मभ्यो ज्यायांसोऽर्थाभिमानिनः. Above the deities of the mind stands Sarasvatī as the intellect — तेभ्यो बुद्धिः सरस्वती — above her Brahmā as the great self, तस्या ब्रह्मा महानात्मा, then Ramā, called the unmanifest, ततोऽव्यक्ताभिधा रमा, and above her the person, Viṣṇu, whom none equals because he is complete: तस्यास्तु पुरुषो विष्णुः पूर्णत्वान्नैव तत्समः.

Where Śaṅkara’s ladder leads inward to one self and Raṅgarāmānuja’s ranks what is to be controlled, Madhva’s is a hierarchy of beings, ending in the one who has no equal.

Hidden in all beings

Comparative Vedānta

एष सर्वेषु भूतेषु गूढोऽत्मा न प्रकाशते — ‘this self, hidden in all beings, does not shine forth’; it is seen by those who see what is subtle, with a sharp and subtle intellect.

Śaṅkara: it is covered by ignorance and māyā, अविद्यामायाच्छन्नः, and he marvels at it — अहो अतिगम्भीरा दुरवगाह्या विचित्रा चेयं माया — that every creature, told it is the supreme self, does not take it in, yet takes the body for itself without being told. The intellect that sees is one that is one-pointed — एकाग्रतयोपेतयेत्येतत् — and those who see the subtle are those who have followed the ladder of subtlety just given. Raṅgarāmānuja: it does not shine as it is for those who have not mastered the outer and inner instruments, अजितबाह्यान्तःकरणानां, न यथावत् प्रकाशते; it is seen by a power of discerning subtle things, सूक्ष्मार्थविवेचनशक्त्या.

Restraint, stage by stage

Comparative Vedānta

यच्छेद्वाङ्मनसी प्राज्ञस्तद्यच्छेज्ज्ञान आत्मनि — ‘the wise one should hold speech in mind, and hold that in the knowing self’; ‘the knowing [self] he should hold in the great self, and that in the self at peace’. It is the ladder again, climbed down into the self: each rung is drawn into the one beyond it. Śaṅkara’s Brahmasūtra commentary calls this the yoga for knowing that highest place: तदवगमार्थं योगं दर्शयति.

Śaṅkara: ‘speech’ stands for all the senses — वागत्रोपलक्षणार्था सर्वेषामिन्द्रियाणाम् — and the long vowel of manasī is Vedic, छान्दसं दैर्घ्यम्. The great self is the first-born; the self at peace is the one in which all distinctions have set, the witness of every thought: सर्वबुद्धिप्रत्ययसाक्षिणि मुख्ये आत्मनि.

Raṅgarāmānuja takes the mantra to show the way of knowing the individual self named ‘inward yoga’ earlier (1.2.12): 'अध्यात्मयोगाधिगमेन' इति निर्दिष्ट जीवस्वरूपज्ञानप्रकारं दर्शयति. He quotes the Bhāṣya — this is how the senses pictured as horses are brought under control, हयादिरूपितानाम् इन्द्रियाणां वशीकरणप्रकारोऽयम् उच्यते — and the self at peace is the Supreme, the inner controller of all, परस्मिन् ब्रह्मणि सर्वान्तर्यामिणि नियच्छेत्. From a commentary he names the Śrutaprakāśikā he quotes that holding the individual self in that self means recognising that it belongs to him: तच्छेषताप्रतिपत्तिः.

Madhva cites a verse (with ‘iti ca’, naming no source) that turns the restraint into meditation on the presiding deities, each held in the one above it, up to Viṣṇu; ‘restraint’ is only to meditate on each as under his control — तद्वशत्वेन तद्ध्यानं नियमो नाम नापरः — for how could a human being restrain the gods who restrain him? कुतस्तु मानुषो देवान्नियच्छेद्विनियामकान्.

  • Śaṅkara on Brahmasūtra 1.4.1: तदवगमार्थं योगं दर्शयति tadavagamārthaṃ yogaṃ darśayati

Arise, awake: the razor’s edge

Traditional Interpretation

उत्तिष्ठत जाग्रत प्राप्य वरान्निबोधत — ‘arise, awake; having reached the excellent ones, understand’. क्षुरस्य धारा निशिता दुरत्यया दुर्गं पथस्तत्कवयो वदन्ति — ‘a razor’s sharpened edge, hard to cross: a hard path, the poets say’. The verbs are plural; the call is to everyone.

Śaṅkara: it is addressed to creatures asleep in beginningless ignorance — अनाद्यविद्याप्रसुप्ताः उत्तिष्ठत हे जन्तवः — and the ‘excellent ones’ are teachers who know the truth: प्रकृष्टानाचार्यांस्तत्त्वविदः. The text speaks out of compassion, like a mother: श्रुतिरनुकम्पया आह मातृवत्. The path is a razor’s edge because what is to be known is so subtle that the way of knowledge is hard to accomplish: ज्ञेयस्यातिसूक्ष्मत्वात्तद्विषयस्य ज्ञानमार्गस्य दुःसम्पाद्यत्वं वदन्तीत्यभिप्रायः.

Raṅgarāmānuja also reads ‘the excellent ones’ as teachers — वरान् - श्रेष्ठान् आचार्यान् उपसङ्गम्य आत्मतत्त्वं निबोधत — and the edge as a warning about attention: as one walking on a razor is ruined by the least slip, so here even a little inattention destroys the self’s progress: स्वल्पेऽपि अनवधानापराधे आत्मनाशो भवतीति भावः. Madhva’s bhāṣya has no comment on this mantra or on the rest of the vallī.

Soundless, touchless

Traditional Interpretation

अशब्दमस्पर्शमरूपमव्ययं तथारसं नित्यमगन्धवच्च यत् — ‘that which is without sound, without touch, without form, undecaying, and without taste, constant, without smell’; ‘without beginning or end, beyond the great, fixed — perceiving that, one is freed from the mouth of death’: निचाय्य तं मृत्युमुखात्प्रमुच्यते.

Śaṅkara argues from the elements. Earth has all five qualities; each subtler element has one fewer, down to space; what has none of them must be subtle beyond all degree: किमु तस्य सूक्ष्मत्वादिनिरतिशयत्वं वक्तव्यमित्येतद्दर्शयति श्रुतिः. ‘Beyond the great’ is beyond the principle called intellect, and the mouth of death is ignorance, desire and action: अविद्याकामकर्मलक्षणात्.

Raṅgarāmānuja reads ‘the great’ as the individual self of the previous mantra — पूर्वमन्त्रनिर्दिष्टो जीवो गृह्यते — so that what is beyond it is the Supreme; ‘perceiving’ is meditation as vivid as seeing, and the mouth of death is the terrible round of saṃsāra: भीषणात् संसारात्.

The close of the first adhyāya

Traditional Interpretation

Two mantras praise the story itself: नाचिकेतमुपाख्यानं मृत्युप्रोक्तं सनातनम् — ‘the ancient tale of Naciketas, told by Death’ — and promise that the wise one who tells it or hears it is honoured in the world of Brahman, and that one who recites it, purified, before an assembly of brahmins or at the rite for the ancestors, gains what is endless: तदानन्त्याय कल्पते. The last words are said twice.

Śaṅkara: the text says this to praise the knowledge just taught, प्रस्तुतविज्ञानस्तुत्यर्थमाह श्रुतिः; the tale is the three vallīs, वल्लीत्रयलक्षणं; ‘the world of Brahman’ is Brahman itself, ब्रह्मैव लोको ब्रह्मलोकः; and the repetition marks the end of the adhyāya, द्विर्वचनमध्यायपरिसमाप्त्यर्थम्. Raṅgarāmānuja: Death only speaks it and is not its free author — मृत्योः प्रवक्तृत्वमेव न स्वतन्त्रवक्तृत्वम् — and it is ancient because it has no human author, eternal as a stream is: अपौरुषेयत्वात् प्रवाहरूपेण नित्यमित्यर्थः. These are the text’s and the commentators’ claims about its own recitation; this edition reports them and makes none of its own.

Order before ascent

Contemporary Reflection

A modern reflection. The vallī gives the same set of things three times: as a chariot to be driven, as a ladder to be climbed, and as a series to be drawn inward, speech into mind and mind into understanding. What changes is not the parts but their order — which leads, which follows, which is held in which.

The commentators disagree on what stands at the top and on what ‘holding’ means. This is a reflection, not a claim of any commentator, and not advice about any practice.

Open questions

  • What the ‘unmanifest’ of 1.3.11 is: the seed of the world, not yet unfolded (Śaṅkara on the Kaṭha), the body pictured as the chariot (the Brahmasūtra commentaries of Śaṅkara and of the Bhāṣya Raṅgarāmānuja quotes), or Ramā (Madhva) — and in every reading, not Sāṅkhya’s independent primal matter.
  • Whether the ‘great self’ is Hiraṇyagarbha (Śaṅkara), the individual self as the rider (the passage Raṅgarāmānuja quotes), or Brahmā (Madhva).
  • Whether the restraint of 1.3.13 ends in the self that is Brahman (Śaṅkara), in recognising that the self belongs to the Supreme (Raṅgarāmānuja), or in meditation on the presiding deities as governed by Viṣṇu (the verse Madhva cites).
  • Madhva’s bhāṣya has no comment on 1.3.14–1.3.17.
