Study VII · Kaṭha Upaniṣad

Two in the cave

Mantras 1.3.1–1.3.2: the two who drink the truth, shade and sunlight, and the bridge for those who would cross

Anuvākas 1.3.1, 1.3.2

The third vallī opens with two who ‘drink the truth’ in the world of good deeds, both entered into the cave, the highest place — as unlike as shade and sunlight. The next mantra names a bridge for sacrificers and a fearless far shore for those who wish to cross. The commentators agree that two selves are meant, but not on which two, on whether both drink, or on what shade and sunlight mark: the self that wanders and the self that does not (Śaṅkara), the soul that knows little and the Lord who knows all (Raṅgarāmānuja), or one Lord in two forms (Madhva).

Two who drink

Direct Textual Meaning

ऋतं पिबन्तौ सुकृतस्य लोके गुहां प्रविष्टौ परमे परार्धे — ‘two drinking the truth in the world of good deeds, entered into the cave, into the highest, the farthest place’. The verb is a dual: there are two, and both are said to drink. छायातपौ ब्रह्मविदो वदन्ति — ‘knowers of Brahman call them shade and sunlight’ — and so do householders who keep the five fires and those who have built the Nāciketa fire three times.

The second mantra turns to what the boy has already been given: यः सेतुरीजानानामक्षरं ब्रह्म यत्परम् — ‘that which is the bridge for those who sacrifice, the imperishable, the highest Brahman’ — the fearless far shore for those who wish to cross: अभयं तितीर्षतां पारं नाचिकेतं शकेमहि, ‘may we master the Nāciketa’. The text does not say who the two are. That is left to the commentators.

Only one drinks (Śaṅkara)

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara ties the vallī to the last: knowledge and ignorance were said to be far apart, but their fruits were not yet settled, and the image of the chariot is made to settle them — तन्निर्णयार्था रथरूपककल्पना — to tell apart the one who goes from the goal: प्राप्तृप्राप्यगन्तृगन्तव्यविवेकार्थं. So two selves are introduced: द्वावात्मानावुपन्यस्येते.

Only one of them drinks the fruit of action — एकस्तत्र कर्मफलं पिबति भुङ्क्ते नेतरः — yet both are called drinkers, as a party is called ‘umbrella-bearers’ when one of them carries the umbrella: च्छत्रिन्यायेन. The cave is the intellect (गुहां गुहायां बुद्धौ प्रविष्टौ); the highest place is the space within the heart, हार्दाकाशम्. They are shade and sunlight because one wanders in saṃsāra and the other does not: संसारित्वासंसारित्वेन.

In his commentary on the Brahmasūtra (1.2.11) Śaṅkara weighs a second reading — the intellect and the individual self — and rejects it: the two are the individual self and the supreme self, विज्ञानात्मपरमात्मानाविहोच्येयाताम्. When a number is heard, the second is looked for among things of the same kind: अस्य गोर्द्वितीयोऽन्वेष्टव्यः — told to find ‘a second to this cow’, one looks for a cow. He adds another way the dual can stand: जीवस्तावत्पिबति ; ईश्वरस्तु पाययति — the soul drinks, the Lord makes it drink. Shade and sunlight fit because wandering is made by ignorance and freedom from it is real: अविद्याकृतत्वात्संसारित्वस्य पारमार्थिकत्वाच्चासंसारित्वस्य.

  • Śaṅkara on Brahmasūtra 1.2.11: विज्ञानात्मपरमात्मानाविहोच्येयाताम् vijñānātmaparamātmānāvihocyeyātām

The soul and the Lord in one cave (Raṅgarāmānuja)

Traditional Interpretation

Raṅgarāmānuja reads the two mantras as an answer to someone discouraged by the last vallī — who thinks the Supreme too hard to know and to meditate on. Because the one meditated on and the one who meditates share one cave, उपास्योपासकयोः एकगुहानुप्रवेशेन, the Supreme is close at hand, and ‘we too can meditate on him’: वयमपि उपासितुं शक्ता.

Shade and sunlight, for him, mark the one who knows little and the one who knows all: छायातपशब्दाभ्यां किञ्चिज्ज्ञसर्वज्ञयोः प्रतिपादनसंभवात्. He takes the mantra as being about the individual self and the Supreme, as the Brahmasūtra decides — गुहां प्रविष्टौ आत्मानौ — and the Supreme can be said to drink as the one who causes the action: परमात्मनः प्रयोजककर्तृतया.

He argues at length against reading the two as the intellect and the soul, with the same example of the second cow — अस्य गोर्द्वितीयो अन्वेष्टव्यः — and against a reading in which the Supreme enters the cave as the soul. He ends the argument himself: अलमतिचर्चया, ‘enough of this long discussion’.

  • Brahmasūtra 1.2.11: गुहां प्रविष्टावात्मानौ हि तद्दर्शनात् guhāṃ praviṣṭāvātmānau hi taddarśanāt

One Lord in two forms (Madhva)

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva does not make the two a soul and a Lord. The one all-pervading Lord stands in two ways, as the self and the inner self: आत्मान्तरात्मेति विभुरेक एव द्विधा स्थितः, and that is Viṣṇu — स विष्णुः. He drinks the auspicious enjoyments: शुभान् पिबति भोगान्. He is shade to the wise — च्छायेव विदुषां प्रभुः — and sunlight, the burning kind, to the wicked: आतपः पापिनां नित्यं.

On the bridge, the Lord is the boundary for those who sacrifice to Viṣṇu — मर्यादा विष्णुयाजिनाम् — and he stands on the far shore of saṃsāra in two forms: संसारस्य च पारस्थस्स विष्णुर्द्विस्वरूपकः. The digital text has gaps on both sides of these lines; this edition quotes only what it prints whole.

The two birds

Comparative Vedānta

The Muṇḍaka has a pair too: द्वा सुपर्णा सयुजा सखाया समानं वृक्षं परिषस्वजाते — ‘two birds, companions and friends, cling to the same tree’ — and there the difference is drawn sharply: तयोरन्यः पिप्पलं स्वाद्वत्ति अनश्नन्नन्योऽभिचाकशीति, ‘one of them eats the sweet fruit; the other, not eating, looks on’. The Kaṭha says both drink.

The commentators read the two passages together. In Śaṅkara’s Brahmasūtra commentary the objector quotes the Muṇḍaka line — अनश्नन्नन्योऽभिचाकशीति — to argue that the supreme self cannot be one of the drinkers. Raṅgarāmānuja reports a reading of the Muṇḍaka verse as the intellect and the soul, तयोरन्यः पिप्पलं स्वाद्वत्तीति सत्त्वम्, which he attributes to the Paiṅgi-rahasya Brāhmaṇa, and holds that the Kaṭha verse is not to be read that way. The resemblance is clear; how far the two verses teach the same thing is what the schools dispute.

  • Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad 3.1.1: तयोरन्यः पिप्पलं स्वाद्वत्ति अनश्नन्नन्योऽभिचाकशीति tayoranyaḥ pippalaṃ svādvatti anaśnannanyo'bhicākaśīti

The bridge and the far shore

Comparative Vedānta

Śaṅkara takes the bridge to be the Nāciketa fire, a bridge for those who perform rites because it carries them across sorrow — दुःखसन्तरणार्थत्वात् — and the far shore to be the imperishable Brahman, the refuge of those who know. The sentence means that both are to be known, the lower and the higher Brahman: परापरे ब्रह्मणी कर्मिब्रह्मविदाश्रये वेदितव्ये इति वाक्यार्थः. These, he says, are the very two introduced by ‘drinking the truth’.

Raṅgarāmānuja takes the bridge as the support that gives the fruit of rites — आधारभूतः, कर्मफलप्रदः इत्यर्थः — and śakemahi as ‘we are able to meditate on what the Nāciketa fire leads to’: नाचिकेताग्निप्राप्यमुपासितुं शक्ताः स्म. So, he concludes, no one need fear that the Supreme is too hard to meditate on: अतः दुरुपास्यत्वबुद्धया न भेतव्यम्.

Shade and sunlight

Contemporary Reflection

A modern reflection. The image is of two that share one place and are still unlike — not light and darkness, which exclude each other, but shade and sunlight, which lie side by side on the same ground. Whatever the two are taken to be, the verse asks the reader to hold closeness and difference at once.

The commentators settle the pair in three different ways, and each reading carries a whole account of the self and the Lord. This is a reflection, not a claim of any commentator.

Open questions

  • Who the two are: the individual self and the supreme self, of whom only one drinks (Śaṅkara); the soul and the Lord, the Lord ‘drinking’ as the one who causes the action (Raṅgarāmānuja); or one Lord in two forms (Madhva).
  • How the Kaṭha’s pair, where both drink, relates to the Muṇḍaka’s two birds, where one does not eat.
