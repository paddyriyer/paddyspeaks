Study XIX · Kaṭha Upaniṣad

The Kaṭha as one teaching

Mantras 1.1.1–2.3.18: the story that frames Death’s teaching, the two adhyāyas, and how Śaṅkara, Raṅgarāmānuja and Madhva read the whole

Anuvākas 1.1.1, 1.1.20, 1.1.29, 1.3.16, 1.3.17, 2.1.1, 2.3.18

The Kaṭha begins as a story — a father’s gift, a son sent to Death, three boons — and ends by returning to it: Naciketas, having received Death’s knowledge and the whole rule of yoga, reaches Brahman, and so does anyone who knows the self as he did. Between, the first adhyāya closes with a praise of the tale itself, and the second opens without it. The three commentators read the six vallīs as one work: for Śaṅkara the story praises the knowledge of the inner self; for Raṅgarāmānuja the whole Upaniṣad, as the Brahmasūtra decides, is about the Supreme; for Madhva it establishes that Viṣṇu is the highest.

A story that holds a teaching

Direct Textual Meaning

The Kaṭha opens as a story, not a discourse: उशन्ह वै वाजश्रवसः सर्ववेदसं ददौ — ‘Vājaśravasa, desiring [its fruit], gave away all he owned.’ His son Naciketas is sent to Death, waits three nights, and is granted three boons. The third is a question about death itself: येयं प्रेते विचिकित्सा मनुष्ये — ‘this doubt about a man who has died’.

Everything after that is Death’s answer, and the story does not reappear until the last mantra, which turns back to the boy: मृत्युप्रोक्तां नचिकेतोऽथ लब्ध्वा विद्यामेतां योगविधिं च कृत्स्नम् — ‘then Naciketas, having received from Death this knowledge and the whole rule of yoga’ — ब्रह्म प्राप्तो विरजोऽभूद्विमृत्युः, ‘reached Brahman, free of passion, free of death’. The same verse opens the story to every reader: अन्योऽप्येवं यो विदध्यात्ममेव, ‘so too anyone else who knows the self in this way’.

The frame matters for how the teaching is read. Naciketas refused every gift offered in place of his question — नान्यं तस्मान्नचिकेता वृणीते, ‘Naciketas chooses no other’ — and the teaching that follows is given to one who has already shown he chooses the good over the pleasant.

Two adhyāyas: what the text shows

Direct Textual Meaning

The first adhyāya ends as if the work were complete. After ‘arise, awake’ (1.3.14) and the soundless self (1.3.15) come two mantras that praise the telling of the story itself: नाचिकेतमुपाख्यानं मृत्युप्रोक्तं सनातनम् — ‘the ancient tale of Naciketas, told by Death’ — and its recitation at a gathering of brahmins or at a śrāddha, closing with its last words said twice: तदानन्त्याय कल्पत इति.

The second adhyāya begins without the story. Its first words are a teaching in Death’s voice with no address to Naciketas: पराञ्चि खानि व्यतृणत्स्वयम्भूः — ‘the self-existent pierced the openings outward’. Naciketas is named again only in the final mantra, 2.3.18, which closes the frame opened in 1.1.

So the text itself shows two closings: a praise of the tale at 1.3.16–17, and a return to the tale at 2.3.18. Whether the second adhyāya was composed with the first or joined to it later is a question the sources of this edition do not answer; the three commentators read the six vallīs as one work.

Śaṅkara: one knowledge, and a story told in its praise

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara sets out the plan in his introduction. The word upaniṣad, he says, names the knowledge itself, because for those who approach it it loosens and destroys the seed of saṃsāra: अविद्यादेः संसारबीजस्य विशरणाद्धिंसनाद्विनाशनादित्यनेनार्थयोगेन विद्योपनिषदित्युच्यते. Its subject is परं ब्रह्म प्रत्यगात्मभूतम्, the highest Brahman that is one’s own inner self; its fruit is आत्यन्तिकी संसारनिवृत्तिर्ब्रह्मप्राप्तिलक्षणा, the final end of saṃsāra, which is the attainment of Brahman. The vallīs, he says, make this as plain as a fruit held in the palm: करतलन्यस्तामलकवत्प्रकाशकत्वेन.

He joins the two adhyāyas by a question. The first ended by saying the self is seen by a subtle intellect; the second begins by asking what stands in its way: कः पुनः प्रतिबन्धोऽग्र्याया बुद्धेः — ‘what then obstructs that subtle intellect?’ — and the vallī is begun to show the cause of not seeing, since only an obstacle that is known can be removed.

Of the last mantra he says: विद्यास्तुत्यर्थोऽयमाख्यायिकार्थोपसंहारः — ‘this conclusion of the story is in praise of the knowledge’. Naciketas obtained the knowledge and the rule of yoga कृत्स्नं समस्तं सोपकरणं सफलमित्येतत्, entire, with its aids and its fruit, and became free; anyone who knows the inner self in the same way becomes free as he did.

Raṅgarāmānuja: the whole Upaniṣad is about the Supreme

Traditional Interpretation

Raṅgarāmānuja begins with verses of homage and a modest statement of purpose: व्याख्यास्ये विदुषां प्रीत्यै कठवल्लीं यथामति — ‘I shall explain the Kaṭha vallīs as I understand them, for the pleasure of the learned’.

He too joins the adhyāyas, but differently: after the exhortation of 1.3.14 people still turn away from the self, and the text grieves over them — इति प्रोत्साहनेऽपि आत्मस्वरूपविमुखान् पश्यन् शोचति.

At the end he reads the closing mantra through the Chāndogya: Naciketas reached Brahman and his eight qualities became manifest, आविर्भूतगुणाष्टकोऽभूत्. The ‘rule of yoga’ is what was taught from 2.3.10 onward: इत्यादिना उक्तं योगविधिं च.

Then he steps back to the whole. The Upaniṣad, he says, is about the Lord alone, as Bādarāyaṇa settled in the first chapter of the Brahmasūtra in three adhikaraṇas: इयञ्च उपनिषत् भगवत्परैव इति भगवता बादरायणेन समन्वयाध्याये त्रिभिः अधिकरणैः निर्णीता. He works through those sūtras — the eater of 1.2.25, the two in the cave of 1.3.1, the unmanifest of 1.3.11 — and concludes: इयमुपनिषत् सर्वाऽपि परमात्मपरा. His last verse honours Rāmānuja for the bhāṣya on the Brahmasūtra; Rāmānuja himself wrote no commentary on the Kaṭha.

Madhva: Viṣṇu the highest

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva’s bhāṣya opens not with a statement of plan but with verses he cites from a work he names the Brahmasāra, on the Nāciketa fire: the one who builds it while always meditating on Viṣṇu in the fire — अग्नौ विष्णुं सदा ध्यायन् — reaches heaven and, in time, immortality. He reports them as cited, and they are given here as cited by him.

His conclusion to the whole is one sentence: अतः सर्वोत्तमो विष्णुरिति सिद्धम् — ‘therefore it is established that Viṣṇu is the highest of all’. A verse of homage follows: नमो भगवते तस्मै विष्णवे प्रभविष्णवे. In the digital text used here his comments are brief and leave many mantras without a comment of their own (among them 1.2.22–25, 1.3.3–9, 1.3.14–17 and 2.2.15); the records say so where it applies.

One teaching, three readings

Comparative Vedānta

All three read the Kaṭha as one work with one subject, and all three take the last mantra as the fulfilment of the story. They differ on what Naciketas reached. For Śaṅkara he reached the Brahman that was always his own inner self, and the story serves to praise the knowledge. For Raṅgarāmānuja he reached the Supreme and his own qualities shone forth, and the whole Upaniṣad, as Bādarāyaṇa decided, is about the Lord. For Madhva the outcome of the whole is that Viṣṇu is supreme over all.

The differences visible across the edition — who chooses in 1.2.23, who the two in the cave are in 1.3.1, what ‘no difference’ denies in 2.1.10–11, what the pure water poured into pure water becomes in 2.1.15 — follow from these three readings of the whole. The text itself states the frame they share: a question asked at Death’s door, and an answer that returns the questioner, free of death, to the story.

A reflection: a teaching given to a refusal

Contemporary Reflection

Read as a whole, the Kaṭha is less a lecture than a test passed. Death teaches nothing until the boy has refused everything that could be given instead, and the teaching itself begins with the choice the boy has already made. A modern reader may find in this an old observation about learning: what one is ready to give up decides what one is able to hear.

This is a modern reflection on the shape of the text, not a reading any commentator gives.

Open questions

  • Whether the second adhyāya was composed with the first or joined to it later: the text shows two closings (1.3.16–17 and 2.3.18), but the sources used in this edition do not settle the history.
  • Madhva’s digital text leaves many mantras without a comment of their own; whether a printed edition of his bhāṣya comments on them needs checking.
  • Raṅgarāmānuja’s text has an extra mantra after 1.1.18, which he says some manuscripts lack; how widely it was transmitted is not settled here.
