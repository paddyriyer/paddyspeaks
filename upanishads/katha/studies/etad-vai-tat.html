This, truly, is that

Mantras 2.1.3–2.1.9: the refrain ‘etad vai tat’; the knower of form and taste; dream and waking; the honey-eater near at hand; the first-born, Aditi and the fire in the fire-sticks; whence the sun rises

Anuvākas 2.1.3, 2.1.4, 2.1.5, 2.1.6, 2.1.7, 2.1.8, 2.1.9

Six of these seven mantras end with the same three words, etad vai tat — ‘this, truly, is that’. Each names something close at hand: the one by whom form and taste are known, the one who sees both dream and waking, the honey-eater, the first-born, Aditi, the fire hidden in the fire-sticks, that from which the sun rises. Each time the refrain says: this is what you asked about. The three commentators agree that ‘that’ is what Naciketas asked for, and disagree on what ‘is’ means: identity for Śaṅkara, ‘has that as its self’ for Raṅgarāmānuja, and for Madhva a series of statements about Viṣṇu.

A refrain

Direct Textual Meaning

The first of the series: येन रूपं रसं गन्धं शब्दान्स्पर्शांश्च मैथुनान् — ‘that by which one knows form, taste, smell, sounds, touches and the pleasures of union’ — एतेनैव विजानाति किमत्र परिशिष्यते — ‘by this alone one knows; what remains here?’ — and then the refrain, एतद्वै तत्, ‘this, truly, is that’.

The same three words close 2.1.5, 2.1.6, 2.1.7, 2.1.8 and 2.1.9. Only 2.1.4, स्वप्नान्तं जागरितान्तं चोभौ येनानुपश्यति, ends differently: knowing the great, all-pervading self, the wise one does not grieve. The images widen as the series goes on — from the knower in each person, to the first-born of creation, to the fire kept alive daily at the sacrifice, to the place where the sun rises and sets.

What ‘this’ and ‘that’ are

Comparative Vedānta

Śaṅkara says ‘that’ is what Naciketas asked about, what even the gods doubted, other than dharma and the rest, the highest place of Viṣṇu — and ‘this’ is it, now understood: किं तत् यन्नचिकेतसा पृष्टं देवादिभिरपि विचिकित्सितं धर्मादिभ्योऽन्यत् विष्णोः परमं पदं यस्मात्परं नास्ति तद्वै एतत् अधिगतमित्यर्थः. At 2.1.8 he says it shortly: एतद्वै तत् तदेव प्रकृतं ब्रह्म.

Raṅgarāmānuja also takes ‘that’ to be the highest place named before as the goal: पूर्वं प्राप्यतया निर्दिष्टं तत्परमं पदं. But from 2.1.7 on he reads ‘that’ as ‘having that as its self’ — तत् - तदात्मकम् इत्यर्थः — and so at 2.1.8 the fire is ‘this’ and ‘that’ means ‘having the Brahman spoken of before as its self’: एतत् - अग्निस्वरूपम्, तत् - पूर्वोक्तब्रह्मात्मकम् इत्यर्थः. He defends the reading from grammar: a word for an inseparable attribute can stand for what bears it, and a word for what bears it can stand for its attribute — अपृथक्सिद्धविशेषणवाचिशब्दस्य विशेष्ये इव, अपृथक्सिद्धविशेष्यवाचिशब्दस्यापि विशेषणे निरूढत्वात् — and he points to the Bhāṣyakāra’s gloss of ‘me’ as ‘having me as its self’ in the Gītā.

Madhva’s comments in this stretch do not gloss the refrain; they say, mantra by mantra, that what is described is Viṣṇu (below). The three readings agree on where ‘that’ points and disagree on what the little word ‘is’ asserts.

The knower who is not known

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara argues that the body and its parts cannot be the knower, because they are made of what is known: यदि हि देहादिसङ्घातो रूपाद्यात्मकः सन् रूपादीन्विजानीयात् , तर्हि बाह्या अपि रूपादयोऽन्योन्यं स्वं स्वं रूपं च विजानीयुः. The knower is a self distinct from the body, and nothing remains that it does not know: यस्यात्मनोऽविज्ञेयं न किञ्चित्परिशिष्यते, स आत्मा सर्वज्ञः. At 2.1.4 the wise one, knowing the great self as ‘I am the supreme self’, does not grieve: साक्षादहमस्मि परमात्मेति धीरः न शोचति.

Raṅgarāmānuja reads ‘by this’ as ‘by this means’ — एतेनैव साधनेन जानाति इत्यर्थः — because the senses that show form and the rest can act only when favoured by it: रूपादिप्रकाशकानाम् इन्द्रियाणां तदनुगृहीतानामेव कार्यारम्भकत्वात् इति भावः. At 2.1.4 the one by whom people see dream and waking is the supreme self, which has become the mind and senses as their inner self: मन-आदि इन्द्रियभावमापन्नेन येन परमात्मना, लोकः पश्यति इत्यर्थः.

The honey-eater, near at hand

Comparative Vedānta

2.1.5 speaks of one who knows ‘this honey-eater, the living self, near at hand, as lord of past and future’; its second line, ईशानं भूतभव्यस्य न ततो विजुगुप्सते, recurs in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka (4.4.15).

Śaṅkara: the honey-eater is the living self that enjoys the fruit of action, and the knower recognises it as the lord of the three times; afterwards he no longer wishes to guard himself, having reached fearlessness: न गोपायितुमिच्छति, अभयप्राप्तत्वात्.

Raṅgarāmānuja: the honey-eater is the individual self of 1.3.1, ‘drinking the truth’, and near it is the Lord of all that is conscious and unconscious in the three times; he takes the verb in the sense of reproach — तं दुष्कृतकारिणमपि न निन्देदित्यर्थः.

Madhva reads ‘near’ as proof of two: the Lord is near the living self, and nothing is near to itself — न हि स्वस्य स्वयं जीवोऽन्तिके भवति — since nearness and distance belong to another thing: वस्त्वन्तरस्य हि दूरत्वमन्तिकत्वं वा.

  • Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.4.15: ईशानं भूतभव्यस्य न ततो विजुगुप्सते īśānaṃ bhūtabhavyasya na tato vijugupsate

The first-born, Aditi and the fire

Comparative Vedānta

Śaṅkara: the one born first, before the waters, from tapas, is Hiraṇyagarbha — जातम् उत्पन्नं हिरण्यगर्भम् — and whoever sees him seated in the cave of the heart sees the Brahman under discussion: य एवं पश्यति, स एतदेव पश्यति — यत्तत्प्रकृतं ब्रह्म. Aditi is so called from ‘eating’ sound and the other objects: शब्दादीनामदनात् अदितिः. The fire in the fire-sticks is the fire of the sacrifice and, inwardly, of the yogins: अधियज्ञम् उत्तराधरारण्योर्निहितः … अध्यात्मं च योगिभिः.

Raṅgarāmānuja makes the Lord the one who sees: he looked upon four-faced Brahmā, seated in the cave, and willed him to be the creator of the world — अयं सकलजगत्स्रष्टा स्यात् इति कटाक्षेण ऐक्षत इत्यर्थः. For Aditi he follows the explanation the Bhāṣyakāra gave of this mantra under the Brahmasūtra on ‘the two in the cave’: इति सूत्रे, भगवता भाष्यकृता व्याख्यातः.

Madhva reads the verb causatively: Viṣṇu did not merely precede but brought forth the first-born — अजायतेति जज्ञे बहुज्ञम् इतिवदन्तर्णीतणिच्. In the verses he cites with each mantra, Aditi is Viṣṇu — अदनादितिर्विष्णुर्यः प्राणसहितः स्थितः — and the two fire-sticks are teacher and pupil: विष्णुररण्योर्गुरुशिष्ययोः. These are reported as verses cited by Madhva.

Whence the sun rises

Comparative Vedānta

यतश्चोदेति सूर्यः अस्तं यत्र च गच्छति — ‘that from which the sun rises and into which it sets’ — तं देवाः सर्वे अर्पितास्तदु नात्येति कश्चन — ‘in it all the gods are fixed; no one passes beyond it’.

Śaṅkara: that from which the sun rises is prāṇa, and prāṇa too is Brahman — यस्मात्प्राणात् उदेति उत्तिष्ठति सूर्यः … सोऽपि ब्रह्मैव. The gods are fixed in it like spokes in the hub of a wheel. Raṅgarāmānuja: it is Brahman, the self in which the gods stand, and no one can step past it, as no one can step over his own shadow, because it is the inner controller: छायावत् अन्तर्यामिण: दुर्लङ्घत्वात् इति भावः.

Pointing, not defining

Contemporary Reflection

A modern reflection. The refrain does not define anything. It points — at the knower in each act of knowing, at the fire tended every day, at the sunrise — and says each time: this. The teaching works by showing the same thing from many directions rather than by stating what it is.

What ‘this is that’ finally asserts is exactly where the commentators part: identity, inner self, or the Lord within. This is a reflection, not a claim of any commentator.

Open questions

  • Whether ‘etad vai tat’ asserts identity (Śaṅkara), ‘having that as its self’ (Raṅgarāmānuja, from 2.1.7), or simply refers each description to Viṣṇu (Madhva).
  • Who ‘saw’ the first-born in 2.1.6: the seeker (Śaṅkara), or the Lord who looked on Brahmā (Raṅgarāmānuja); and whether ‘was born’ is causative (Madhva).
  • The passage that stands after 2.1.3 in Madhva’s text glosses words of 2.2.6; it is not used here as his comment on 2.1.3.
