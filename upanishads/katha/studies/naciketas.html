Study I · Kaṭha Upaniṣad

Naciketas: the story and its frame

Mantras 1.1.1–1.1.9: a sacrifice of everything, a gift of worn-out cows, a boy given to Death, and three nights without food at Death’s door

Anuvākas 1.1.1, 1.1.2, 1.1.3, 1.1.4, 1.1.5, 1.1.6, 1.1.7, 1.1.8, 1.1.9

The Kaṭha begins as a story. A father gives away everything at a sacrifice; his son sees that the cows are worthless and offers himself instead; the father, provoked, gives him to Death. The boy waits three nights at Death’s house without food, and Death, returning, offers three boons. This study sets out what the text says, how Śaṅkara, Raṅgarāmānuja and Madhva’s school read the frame, and where they differ.

The gift of everything

Direct Textual Meaning

The first words set the scene in a single sentence: उशन्ह वै वाजश्रवसः सर्ववेदसं ददौ — ‘wishing [for its fruit], Vājaśravasa gave away all he possessed’. The next sentence names the son on whom the whole Upaniṣad turns: तस्य ह नचिकेता नाम पुत्र आस — ‘he had a son named Naciketas’.

Nothing is said yet about Death or the self. The text moves in narrative: a rite, a gift, a boy watching. The teaching the Upaniṣad is known for begins only at the end of this vallī, with the third boon.

Why the story is told

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara opens his comment by saying what kind of passage this is: तत्राख्यायिका विद्यास्तुत्यर्था — ‘the story here is for the praise of the knowledge’. The narrative is not history for its own sake; it shows how hard the knowledge is to obtain and how much it is worth.

He names the rite as the Viśvajit, the sacrifice in which everything is given: विश्वजिता सर्वमेधेनेजे तत्फलं कामयमानः — ‘he performed the Viśvajit, the all-sacrifice, desiring its fruit’.

What the father gave

Traditional Interpretation

Mantra 1.1.3 describes the cows as having drunk their last water, eaten their last grass, given their last milk: पीतोदका जग्धतृणादुग्धदोहा निरिन्द्रियाः — and says that whoever gives such cows goes to worlds ‘without joy’. Śaṅkara glosses निरिन्द्रियाः प्रजननासमर्थाः जीर्णाः, निष्फला गाव इत्यर्थः — ‘unable to bear, worn out: fruitless cows’.

Raṅgarāmānuja draws a ritual point from the first word, uśan, ‘desiring’: because the rite was undertaken for its fruit, the gifts had to be complete and sound — उशन् इत्यनेन कर्मणः काम्यत्वात् दक्षिणासाद्गुण्यम् आवश्यकमिति सूच्यते. A desire-driven rite with defective gifts fails on its own terms.

Faith enters a boy

Traditional Interpretation

The text says simply that, as the gifts were being led away, श्रद्धाविवेश — ‘faith entered’ the boy, though he was still young. Śaṅkara explains the faith as conviction in the scripture, moved by concern for his father: श्रद्धा आस्तिक्यबुद्धिः पितुर्हितकामप्रयुक्ता आविवेश.

Both commentators make the boy’s question a remedy, not a rebuke. Śaṅkara: he would make the rite complete even by giving himself — आत्मप्रदानेनापि क्रतुसम्पत्तिं. Raṅgarāmānuja says the same in nearly the same words: स्वात्मदानेनापि पितुः क्रतुसाद्गुण्यमिच्छन्.

‘To Death I give you’

Direct Textual Meaning

The boy asks three times: कस्मै मां दास्यसीति — ‘to whom will you give me?’ The father answers: मृत्यवे त्वा ददामीति — ‘to Death I give you’.

The text does not say why the father answers so. Śaṅkara supplies the reason from the situation: the father was angry — नायं कुमारस्वभाव इति क्रुद्धः सन्, ‘angry, [thinking] this is not how a boy should behave’.

The boy’s reflection

Comparative Vedānta

Mantra 1.1.5 is spoken by Naciketas to himself: बहूनामेमि प्रथमो बहूनामेमि मध्यमः — ‘of many I go first, of many I go in the middle’. The commentators read the line differently.

Śaṅkara takes it as the boy’s judgement of his own conduct among pupils or sons — first, or at least in the middle, never last: बहूनां शिष्याणां पुत्राणां वा एमि गच्छामि प्रथमः सन् मुख्यया शिष्यादिवृत्त्येत्यर्थः. Raṅgarāmānuja takes it of those who go to Death: the boy is ahead of or among them, not behind — सर्वेषां मृत्युसदनगन्तृणां पुरतो मध्ये वा गच्छामि, न तु पश्चात् — and is untroubled by going.

Both then read the second half the same way: what use can Death, who lacks nothing, have of him?

Let the word stand

Traditional Interpretation

Mantra 1.1.6 turns to the father: look at how those before you lived, and how the good live now; सस्यमिव मर्त्यः पच्यते सस्यमिवाजायते पुनः — ‘like grain a mortal ripens; like grain he is born again’.

Both commentators read it as the boy urging his father to keep his word even though it was spoken in anger. Śaṅkara: पालयात्मनः सत्यम् — ‘keep your own truthfulness’. Raṅgarāmānuja: पालय सत्यम्, प्रेषय मां मृत्यवे — ‘keep the truth; send me to Death’.

Three nights at Death’s door

Direct Textual Meaning

Mantra 1.1.7 is a warning about a neglected guest: वैश्वानरः प्रविशति अतिथिर्ब्राह्मणो गृहान् — ‘as Vaiśvānara, as fire, a Brahmin guest enters a house’; it ends ‘bring water, son of Vivasvat’. Mantra 1.1.8 lists what is lost by the one in whose house a Brahmin stays unfed.

Death then speaks to the boy himself: तिस्रो रात्रीर्यदवात्सीर्गृहे मे अनश्नन् — ‘since you have stayed three nights in my house without food’ — and offers त्रीन्वरान्वृणीष्व, ‘choose three boons’.

Who speaks in 1.1.7

Comparative Vedānta

The text does not name the speaker of 1.1.7. Śaṅkara: Death’s ministers or wife — अमात्या भार्या वा ऊचुः. Raṅgarāmānuja: the elders at the door — वृद्धाः ऊचुः. In Madhva’s commentary it is Yama’s wife, who has honoured the boy and then tells Yama to bring water: आगते तु यमे प्राह यमं सोदकमाहर.

The difference is small but shows each commentator filling a silence of the text from the situation.

The guest and the three boons

Comparative Vedānta

Śaṅkara draws the rule from 1.1.8: तस्मादनुपेक्षणीयः सर्वावस्थास्वप्यतिथिरित्यर्थः — ‘so a guest is never to be neglected, in any circumstance’.

Why three boons? Śaṅkara and Raṅgarāmānuja both count them against the nights: one boon for each night spent unfed — अनशनेनोषितामेकैकां रात्रिं प्रति त्रीन् वरान् वृणीष्व (Śaṅkara); अनशनरात्रिसमसंख्याकान् त्रीन् वरान् (Raṅgarāmānuja).

Madhva cites verses that name the three boons in advance as his school understands them: सौमनस्यं पितुश्चैव नाचिकेताग्निगं हरिम् — the father’s good will, Hari present in the Naciketa fire — and, third, Viṣṇu present in the released. He gives the source as the Gatisāra; the citation is reported as his.

A gift that costs nothing

Contemporary Reflection

A modern reader may recognise the father: generosity in form, economy in substance — giving away what had already stopped being useful. The boy’s question, ‘to whom will you give me?’, makes the point without accusing anyone: if the gift is to be real, it must include what one actually values.

This is a reflection, not the text’s claim. In the Upaniṣad the point serves something else: the boy who sees through a hollow gift is the one who will later refuse every hollow offer Death makes.

Open questions

  • Who speaks 1.1.7 — Death’s ministers or wife (Śaṅkara), the elders at the door (Raṅgarāmānuja), or Yama’s wife (Madhva)? The text does not say.
  • Whether ‘first of many, in the middle of many’ (1.1.5) concerns the boy’s conduct among pupils (Śaṅkara) or his place among those who go to Death (Raṅgarāmānuja).
