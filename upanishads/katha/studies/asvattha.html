Study XVI · Kaṭha Upaniṣad

The eternal aśvattha

Mantras 2.3.1–2.3.3: the tree with its root above and branches below; all that is trembles in breath, a great fear, a raised thunderbolt; from fear of him the fire burns

Anuvākas 2.3.1, 2.3.2, 2.3.3

The last vallī opens with an image: the world is an aśvattha, a fig tree, with its root above and its branches below, and it is eternal. Its root is the bright, Brahman, the immortal; all the worlds rest in it and nothing goes beyond it. Whatever there is moves out of breath and trembles in it — a great fear, a raised thunderbolt — and from fear of him fire burns, the sun gives heat, and Indra, Wind and Death run their courses. The commentators agree that the root is the highest; they differ on what the tree is, what stands at its top, and what the fear is that keeps the world in order.

The words

Direct Textual Meaning

ऊर्ध्वमूलोऽवाक्शाख एषोऽश्वत्थः सनातनः — ‘this eternal aśvattha has its root above and its branches below’. Then तदेव शुक्रं तद्ब्रह्म तदेवामृतमुच्यते — ‘that alone is the bright, that is Brahman, that alone is called the immortal’ — and तस्मिंल्लोकाः श्रिताः सर्वे तदु नात्येति कश्चन — ‘in it all the worlds rest; none goes beyond it’. The verse closes with the refrain of the second adhyāya, एतद्वै तत्, ‘this, truly, is that’.

2.3.2: यदिदं किञ्च जगत्सर्वं प्राण एजति निःसृतम् — ‘whatever there is, this whole moving world, having come out, trembles in breath’ — and then three words in apposition, महद्भयं वज्रमुद्यतं, ‘a great fear, a raised thunderbolt’; those who know this become immortal.

2.3.3 names who keeps to his task: भयादस्याग्निस्तपति भयात्तपति सूर्यः — ‘from fear of him fire burns, from fear the sun gives heat’ — and Indra and Wind, and मृत्युर्धावति पञ्चमः, ‘Death, the fifth, runs’.

The tree of the world, rooted in Brahman (Śaṅkara)

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara opens the vallī with its purpose: as one finds the root of a tree by tracing the tree, तूलावधारणेनैव मूलावधारणं वृक्षस्य यथा क्रियते लोके, so the tree of the world is described to make its root, Brahman, known. The root above is the highest place, ऊर्ध्वं मूलं यत् तद्विष्णोः परमं पदमस्येति, and the tree runs from the unmanifest down to the motionless things. It is called a tree because it is cut down, वृक्षश्च व्रश्चनात् विनश्वरत्वात्, and an aśvattha because, like that tree’s leaves, it is never still, shaken by the wind of desire and action: अश्वत्थवत्कामकर्मवातेरितनित्यप्रचलितस्वभावः.

What is ‘the bright, Brahman, the immortal’ is the tree’s root, not the tree. The worlds rest in it as products rest in their material, मृदादिकमिव घटादिकार्यं, and nothing that changes passes beyond it.

On 2.3.2 he takes prāṇa as the supreme Brahman, in which the world moves, प्राणे परस्मिन्ब्रह्मणि सति एजति कम्पते, and reads the fear as the fear servants have of a master who stands over them with a raised thunderbolt: यथा वज्रोद्यतकरं स्वामिनम् अभिमुखीभूतं दृष्ट्वा भृत्या नियमेन तच्छासने वर्तन्ते. On 2.3.3 the regular courses of fire, sun and the guardians of the world show a ruler over them, as with servants afraid of their master: स्वामिभयभीतानामिव भृत्यानां नियता प्रवृत्तिरुपपद्यते.

The tree of saṃsāra and the one distinct from it (Raṅgarāmānuja)

Traditional Interpretation

Raṅgarāmānuja reads 2.3.1 through the commentary on the Gītā’s tree by the one he calls the Bhāṣyakāra, which he quotes. The tree is saṃsāra. Its root is above because the four-faced creator sits above the seven worlds, सप्तलोकोपरिनिविष्ट चतुर्मुखादित्वेनतस्य ऊर्ध्वमूलत्वम्, and its branches are below because they reach down through every creature that lives on the earth, पृथिवीनिवासि सकलनरपशुमृगक्रिमिकीटपतङ्गस्थावरान्ततया अधश्शाखत्वम्.

‘That alone is the bright’ then turns from the tree to what is other than it: तद्विलक्षणमेव ब्रह्मेति दर्शयति. Where Śaṅkara makes Brahman the tree’s root, Raṅgarāmānuja makes the root the creator at the top of the worlds and Brahman distinct from the whole tree.

On 2.3.2 he points to the Brahmasūtra on ‘trembling’ (kampana; 1.3.39 in the numbering of the edition used here, 1.3.40 in his) and to the Bhāṣyakāra’s comment on it, which reads the four words as nominatives in the sense of the ablative: the world trembles from the great fear, from him as from a raised thunderbolt. He adds that ‘breath’ here certainly means the Supreme: प्राणशब्दस्य परमात्मपरत्वे न विवाद इति द्रष्टव्यम्. On 2.3.3 ‘runs’ means that Indra and the others go about their own work: धावति शब्दः इन्द्रादीनां स्वव्यापारप्रवृत्तिपरः.

  • Brahmasūtra 1.3.39: कम्पनात् kampanāt

The Lord as ground of the tree (Madhva)

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva’s comment on 2.3.1 is in verse. The Lord, highest of all, is the root of the tree called the world, as the ground is: सर्वोच्चो भगवान् विष्णुर्मूलं भूमिवदस्य तु; its branches are the gods and those below them, जगदाख्यस्य वृक्षस्य शाखा देवास्ततोऽधमाः. The tree is named aśvattha because it is pervaded by Hari, the swift ‘horse’ (aśva), and is his food: तद्व्याप्तत्वात् तदन्नत्वादश्वत्थोऽयं प्रकीर्तितः. ‘Eternal’ is said in two senses: the world is beginningless as a stream, Hari immortal in the full sense — प्रवाहतस्त्वनादिश्च मुख्यतस्त्वमृतो हरिः.

On 2.3.2 all moves in Hari called prāṇa and has come out of him, and he is fearful like a thunderbolt to whoever oversteps his own dharma: वज्रवद्भयदं चैव स्वधर्मस्यातिलङ्घने. His bhāṣya has no comment of its own on 2.3.3; the passage filed with it concerns the mirror of 2.3.5.

Two echoes: the Gītā’s tree and the Taittirīya’s fear

Academic History

The Bhagavad Gītā opens its fifteenth chapter with the same tree, ऊर्ध्वमूलमधःशाखमश्वत्थं प्राहुरव्ययम् — ‘they speak of the imperishable aśvattha, root above, branches below’ — and turns the image: the tree is to be cut with the strong axe of non-attachment, अश्वत्थमेनं सुविरूढमूलमसङ्गशस्त्रेण दृढेन छित्त्वा. Śaṅkara’s description of the Kaṭha’s tree already ends with that cutting, by the weapon of non-attachment that is the seeing of Brahman as the self: वेदान्तविहितब्रह्मात्मदर्शनासङ्गशस्त्रकृतोच्छेदः.

The Taittirīya Upaniṣad (2.8.1) has the companion of 2.3.3: भीषास्माद्वातः पवते, ‘from fear of him the wind blows’, the sun rises, Agni and Indra act, and Death runs fifth. The two lists differ in order and in members, and share the line on Death. Neither text says which it took from the other, and this edition does not guess.

  • Bhagavad Gītā 15.1: ऊर्ध्वमूलमधःशाखमश्वत्थं प्राहुरव्ययम् ūrdhvamūlamadhaḥśākhamaśvatthaṃ prāhuravyayam
  • Bhagavad Gītā 15.3: अश्वत्थमेनं सुविरूढमूलमसङ्गशस्त्रेण दृढेन छित्त्वा aśvatthamenaṃ suvirūḍhamūlamasaṅgaśastreṇa dṛḍhena chittvā
  • Taittirīya Upaniṣad 2.8.1: भीषास्माद्वातः पवते bhīṣāsmādvātaḥ pavate

‘A great fear, a raised thunderbolt’

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The four words महत् भयम् वज्रम् उद्यतम् stand in the nominative after a verb, ejati, whose subject is ‘the whole world’. Śaṅkara takes them as describing Brahman itself: it is great, and it is fear because one fears it — महच्च तत् भयं च बिभेत्यस्मादिति महद्भयम् — and it is like a raised thunderbolt.

Raṅgarāmānuja, following the Bhāṣyakāra, takes all four as nominatives standing for ablatives, अत्र महद्भयं वज्रमुद्यतम् इति चत्वारि पदानि पञ्चम्यर्थप्रथमान्तानि: the world trembles ‘from great fear, from a raised thunderbolt’, the first pair naming the fear and the second the Supreme who causes it. He reports another view as well, that bhaya means ‘what is fearsome’, केचित्तु, बिभेति अस्मात् इति भयम्. The grammar does not decide between them; the sense — that the world keeps its order under the Supreme — is the same in both.

A root above

Contemporary Reflection

A modern reflection. An ordinary tree draws what it needs from below; this one hangs from above, and what is visible — the branches spreading downward — depends on what cannot be seen. The verse asks the reader to look for the source of the world not further along its branches but in the other direction.

The fear in 2.3.2–3 is not a threat made to a person; it is the order by which fire burns and the sun gives heat on time. The commentators differ on what stands at the top of the tree; they agree that what keeps the world in its courses is not one of its branches. This paragraph is a reflection, not a reading of the text.

Open questions

  • What stands at the root above: Brahman itself (Śaṅkara), the four-faced creator at the top of the worlds with Brahman distinct from the whole tree (Raṅgarāmānuja, following the Gītā commentary he quotes), or Viṣṇu as the tree’s ground (Madhva).
  • Whether ‘a great fear, a raised thunderbolt’ describes Brahman as what is feared (Śaṅkara) or stands for ablatives — the world trembles from great fear, from a raised thunderbolt (Raṅgarāmānuja, after the Bhāṣyakāra).
  • Madhva’s bhāṣya has no comment of its own on 2.3.3; the passage filed under 2.3.3–2.3.5 in the digital text concerns 2.3.5.
