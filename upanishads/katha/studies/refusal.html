Study III · Kaṭha Upaniṣad

The refusal: tests of the seeker

Mantras 1.1.21–1.1.29: Death’s reluctance, his offers of sons, wealth, long life and pleasures, and the boy’s answers

Anuvākas 1.1.21, 1.1.22, 1.1.23, 1.1.24, 1.1.25, 1.1.26, 1.1.27, 1.1.28, 1.1.29

Death tries to withdraw the third boon: even the gods doubted this, it is subtle, choose something else. When the boy holds firm, Death offers long-lived sons, cattle and gold, a kingdom, life as long as he wishes, and the pleasures of heaven. Naciketas refuses each. The commentators agree that the passage is a test, and differ on what the subtle ‘dharma’ is and on why Death hesitates.

Even the gods doubted

Direct Textual Meaning

Death answers: देवैरत्रापि विचिकित्सितं पुरा — ‘even the gods doubted about this of old’ — न हि सुविज्ञेयमणुरेष धर्मः, ‘it is not easy to know well; this matter is subtle’. Choose another boon.

Naciketas turns Death’s words back on him: if even the gods doubted and you say it is hard to know, then वक्ता चास्य त्वादृगन्यो न लभ्यो — ‘no other teacher like you can be found’ — and no other boon is equal to it.

A test

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara says plainly why Death hesitates: to test whether the boy is fit for the knowledge that alone leads to the highest good — किमयमेकान्ततो निःश्रेयससाधनात्मज्ञानार्हो न वेत्येतत्परीक्षणार्थमाह. The temptations that follow serve the same end: only one who has turned away from the passing fruits of action is entitled to the knowledge of the self, and the offers of sons and the rest are made to show those fruits for what they are — तन्निन्दार्थं पुत्राद्युपन्यासेन प्रलोभनं क्रियते.

Raṅgarāmānuja agrees that the offers are a test, made to see whether the desire for release holds: मुमुक्षास्थैर्यानुवृत्त्यर्थं प्रलोभयन्.

What is the subtle ‘dharma’?

Comparative Vedānta

The word dharma here does not mean duty. Śaṅkara takes it as the self: अणुः सूक्ष्मः एषः आत्माख्यः धर्मः — ‘this subtle thing called the self’. Madhva takes it as the Lord, the one who upholds all: धारकत्वात् धर्मो भगवान्. Raṅgarāmānuja: the truth of the self is not easy to know — आत्मतत्त्वं न सुज्ञानमिति सूक्ष्म एष धर्मः.

The witnesses differ here too: the base reads suvijñeyam, ‘easy to know well’; GRETIL and Madhva’s text read a shorter su-jñeya (see the variants on 1.1.21).

The offers

Direct Textual Meaning

Death offers, in rising order: शतायुषः पुत्रपौत्रान्वृणीष्व — ‘choose sons and grandsons who live a hundred years’ — herds, elephants, gold and horses, a great expanse of earth, and life itself: स्वयं च जीव शरदो यावदिच्छसि, ‘live yourself as many autumns as you wish’.

Then wealth and long life again, kingship over a great land, and the women of heaven with their chariots and music: इमा रामाः सरथाः सतूर्या — such as men cannot obtain. Only one thing is asked in return: मरणं मानुप्राक्षीः — ‘do not ask about death’.

The answers

Direct Textual Meaning

Naciketas answers with a few hard observations. These things last until tomorrow — श्वोभावा मर्त्यस्य — and wear out the vigour of every sense; even the longest life is short; keep your horses, your song and dance.

न वित्तेन तर्पणीयो मनुष्यो — ‘a man is not satisfied by wealth’. Having seen Death, he will have wealth enough, and will live as long as Death rules. वरस्तु मे वरणीयः स एव — ‘the boon I choose is that one alone’. And who, having come near those who do not age, would delight in a very long life, thinking about beauty and pleasure: अतिदीर्घे जीविते को रमेत.

The unshaken boy

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara describes Naciketas as answering ‘unshaken like a great lake’ — महाह्रदवदक्षोभ्य आह — and presses the point about long life: even Brahmā’s span is short — सर्वं यद्ब्रह्मणोऽपि जीवितम् आयुः अल्पमेव, ‘the whole life even of Brahmā is short’, how much more ours.

A word read two ways

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Mantra 1.1.28 calls the mortal क्वधःस्थः. Śaṅkara divides it as ku, ‘earth’, and adhaḥ-stha, ‘standing below’: one who stands on the earth below the higher worlds — कुः पृथिवी अधश्चासावन्तरिक्षादिलोकापेक्षया तस्यां तिष्ठतीति क्वधःस्थः. He also records another reading, ‘क्व तदास्थः’ इति वा पाठान्तरम् — ‘how could such a one set his heart on those things?’ — and explains both.

Raṅgarāmānuja comments on that other reading, kva tadāsthaḥ, as the text before him. Madhva’s text has a third spelling, kvādhasthaḥ (see the variants on 1.1.28).

The hidden boon

Traditional Interpretation

The vallī ends: योऽयं वरो गूढमनुप्रविष्टो नान्यं तस्मान्नचिकेता वृणीते — ‘the boon that has entered what is hidden — Naciketas chooses no other’. Both Śaṅkara and Raṅgarāmānuja note that this last line is the voice of the Veda itself, not of the boy: इति श्रुतेर्वचनम्.

Why Death hesitates: another reading

Comparative Vedānta

Raṅgarāmānuja gives Death a teacher’s caution: one who might fall midway in so deep a teaching should not be taught it — नोपदेष्टव्यम् इति मत्वाऽऽह. The test is therefore not of the boy’s worth alone but of whether he will see the teaching through.

Saying no to a good offer

Contemporary Reflection

Death’s offers are not evil things: children who live long, prosperity, a long life, music and beauty. What Naciketas refuses is the trade — taking them in place of the question. A modern reader may recognise the pattern in any life where comfort is offered in exchange for leaving a hard question unasked.

This is a reflection. In the Upaniṣad the refusal is what qualifies the boy to be taught; the teaching itself begins in the next vallī.

Open questions

  • Whether the ‘subtle dharma’ of 1.1.21 is the self (Śaṅkara) or the Lord (Madhva) — the word allows both and the context does not settle it.
  • Which reading of 1.1.28 is older: kvadhaḥsthaḥ (the base, with Śaṅkara), kva tadāsthaḥ (Raṅgarāmānuja; noted by Śaṅkara), or Madhva’s kvādhasthaḥ.
  • Whether Death’s reluctance tests the boy’s worth, his steadiness, or both: the text gives no reason of its own.
