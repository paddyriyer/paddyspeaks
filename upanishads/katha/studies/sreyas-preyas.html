Study IV · Kaṭha Upaniṣad

The good and the pleasant

Mantras 1.2.1–1.2.9: the two that bind a person, knowledge and ignorance, the blind leading the blind, and why this teaching cannot be reached by reasoning alone

Anuvākas 1.2.1, 1.2.2, 1.2.3, 1.2.4, 1.2.5, 1.2.6, 1.2.7, 1.2.8, 1.2.9

Having tested the boy, Death begins to teach. The good (śreyas) and the pleasant (preyas) both take hold of a person; the wise tell them apart and choose the good, the dull choose the pleasant for the sake of getting and keeping. Knowledge and ignorance lead far apart; those who live in ignorance while thinking themselves learned go round like the blind led by the blind. The self is rarely heard of, rarely taught, rarely understood; it is not well taught by an inferior man, and the understanding Naciketas has reached cannot be got by reasoning. The commentators agree on the contrast and differ sharply on who the ‘non-other’ teacher of 1.2.8 is.

Two things take hold of a person

Direct Textual Meaning

Death’s teaching opens with a contrast: अन्यच्छ्रेयोऽन्यदुतैव प्रेयस्ते उभे नानार्थे पुरुषं सिनीतः — ‘the good is one thing, the pleasant quite another; both, with different aims, bind a person’. Of the two, तयोः श्रेय आददानस्य साधु भवति — ‘it goes well with the one who takes the good’ — while हीयतेऽर्थाद्य उ प्रेयो वृणीते, ‘he who chooses the pleasant falls short of the goal’.

The next mantra says that the two do not come separately. श्रेयश्च प्रेयश्च मनुष्यमेतः — ‘the good and the pleasant both come to a man’ — and the wise one, going round them, tells them apart: तौ सम्परीत्य विविनक्ति धीरः. The wise choose the good over the pleasant; प्रेयो मन्दो योगक्षेमाद्वृणीते — ‘the dull choose the pleasant, for the sake of getting and keeping’.

Then Death turns to the boy: he has considered the pleasures offered him and let them go, and has not taken सृङ्कां वित्तमयीम्, the ‘chain’ or ‘road’ made of wealth, in which many men sink.

Telling them apart

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara explains why both ‘bind’: everyone is drawn to act by one or the other, by the wish for prosperity or for immortality — ताभ्यां विद्याविद्याभ्यामात्मकर्तव्यतया प्रयुज्यते सर्वः पुरुषः. Since the two are opposed, one person cannot pursue both together: अन्यतरापरित्यागेनैकेन पुरुषेण सहानुष्ठातुमशक्यत्वात्.

Why, then, do most people take the pleasant, if both are within their power? For the dull, Śaṅkara answers, the two are hard to tell apart and come to them as if mixed — मन्दबुद्धीनां दुर्विवेकरूपे सती व्यामिश्रीभूते इव. The wise one separates them as a swan separates milk from water: हंस इवाम्भसः पयः. ‘Getting and keeping’ means the growth and protection of the body: योगक्षेमनिमित्तं शरीराद्युपचयरक्षणनिमित्तमित्येतत्.

Raṅgarāmānuja reads the pair as two roads: अतिप्रशस्तं मोक्षवर्त्माऽपि अन्यत् — the most excellent road, to release, is one — and प्रियत्वास्पदं भोगवर्त्मापि अन्यत्, the road of enjoyment, the seat of what is dear, is another. He uses the same image of the swan — नीरक्षीरे हंस इव पृथक्करोति — glosses dhīra as ‘one who delights in understanding’ — धिया रमते इति धीरः — and defines the two words of the compound: शरीरस्योपचयो योगः, and keeping is protection, क्षेमः परिपालनम्.

Knowledge and ignorance lead far apart

Traditional Interpretation

दूरमेते विपरीते विषूची अविद्या या च विद्येति ज्ञाता — ‘far apart, opposed and going different ways, are these two, known as ignorance and knowledge’. Death counts Naciketas among those who seek knowledge, because the many desires did not tempt him: न त्वा कामा बहवोऽलोलुपन्त.

Śaṅkara ties the verse back to the good and the pleasant: ignorance has the pleasant as its object, knowledge the good — या च अविद्या प्रेयोविषया विद्येति च श्रेयोविषया. They are as different as darkness and light, तमःप्रकाशाविव, and lead to different ends, भिन्नफले संसारमोक्षहेतुत्वेनेत्येतत् — one to the round of rebirth, the other to release.

Raṅgarāmānuja calls ignorance ‘made of desire and action’, कामकर्मात्मिका, and knowledge ‘made of dispassion and knowledge of the truth’, वैराग्यतत्त्वज्ञानमयी. He also knows a different reading of the boy’s epithet, विद्याभीप्सितम्, ‘longed for by knowledge’, and explains its word order as a grammatical licence; it is the reading of Madhva’s text (see the variants on this vallī).

The blind led by the blind

Direct Textual Meaning

The other road is described without softening. अविद्यायामन्तरे वर्तमानाः स्वयं धीराः पण्डितम्मन्यमानाः — ‘living in the midst of ignorance, wise in their own eyes, thinking themselves learned’ — the deluded go round and round, अन्धेनैव नीयमाना यथान्धाः, ‘like the blind led by one who is blind’. Śaṅkara glosses their wandering as a crooked course of many forms, through old age, death and disease: दन्द्रम्यमाणाः अत्यर्थं कुटिलामनेकरूपां गतिं गच्छन्तः.

The same mantra stands in the Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad (1.2.8) with one different word: where the Kaṭha has dandramyamāṇāḥ, ‘wandering about’, the Muṇḍaka has जङ्घन्यमानाः, ‘struck again and again’.

For such a person the way to what lies beyond does not appear: न साम्परायः प्रतिभाति बालं. Śaṅkara takes sāmparāya as the next world and, by extension, the means of reaching it taught in scripture: सम्परेयत इति सम्परायः परलोकः. The one who thinks अयं लोको नास्ति पर इति मानी — ‘this world is, there is no other’ — falls again and again into Death’s power: पुनः पुनर्वशमापद्यते मे.

  • Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad 1.2.8: अविद्यायामन्तरे वर्तमानाः स्वयं धीराः पण्डितम्मन्यमानाः avidyāyāmantare vartamānāḥ svayaṃ dhīrāḥ paṇḍitammanyamānāḥ

Rare to hear, rare to teach, rare to know

Traditional Interpretation

श्रवणायापि बहुभिर्यो न लभ्यः — ‘that which many do not get even to hear of’ — and which many who hear do not know. आश्चर्यो वक्ता कुशलोऽस्य लब्धा — ‘a wonder is its teacher, skilled the one who obtains it’ — and आश्चर्यो ज्ञाता कुशलानुशिष्टः, ‘a wonder the one who knows it, taught by the skilled’.

Śaṅkara reads the verse as praise of Naciketas: among thousands only one, like him, comes to know the self — स सहस्रेषु कश्चिदेवात्मविद्भवति त्वद्विधः. Raṅgarāmānuja adds that even the chance to hear it is the fruit of great merit: श्रवणलाभोऽपि महासुकृतफलमिति भावः.

The Bhagavad Gītā (2.29) uses the same word for the same rarity, in its own wording: आश्चर्यवत्पश्यति कश्चिदेनमाश्चर्यवद्वदति तथैव चान्यः. The likeness is of theme and vocabulary, not a repeated verse.

  • Bhagavad Gītā 2.29: आश्चर्यवत्पश्यति कश्चिदेनमाश्चर्यवद्वदति तथैव चान्यः āścaryavatpaśyati kaścidenamāścaryavadvadati tathaiva cānyaḥ

Who is the ‘non-other’ teacher?

Comparative Vedānta

न नरेणावरेण प्रोक्त एष सुविज्ञेयो बहुधा चिन्त्यमानः — ‘taught by an inferior man, this is not well understood, being thought of in many ways’. Then the hardest half-line of the vallī: अनन्यप्रोक्ते गतिरत्र नास्ति — literally ‘when taught by a non-other, there is no going here’ — ‘for it is subtler than the subtle, beyond reasoning’.

Śaṅkara takes the ‘non-other’ to be a teacher who sees no difference, who has become the Brahman he teaches: अनन्येन अपृथग्दर्शिनाचार्येण प्रतिपाद्यब्रह्मात्मभूतेन प्रोक्ते. When such a one teaches, the many speculations — is it, is it not — have no place, or there is no further going, since nothing else remains to be known. Taught otherwise, the self is beyond reasoning, because reasoning never comes to rest: न हि तर्कस्य निष्ठा क्वचिद्विद्यते.

Raṅgarāmānuja takes the non-other to be one devoted to the self alone who has seen it directly — तदेकान्तिना ब्रह्मात्मसाक्षात्कारिणा — and records other readings, among them a text that reads anyaprokte, ‘taught by another’.

Madhva reverses the sense of the word. The ‘non-other’ is the one who does not know the difference — अन्यो भगवानन्योऽहमित्यजानन्ननन्यः, ‘not knowing “the Lord is one, I am another”’ — and when such a one teaches there is no knowledge: तेन प्रोक्ते गतिर्ज्ञानं नास्ति. He supports this from the next mantra, which says the understanding is ‘taught by another’, and cites a verse he attributes to the Brahmavaivarta: जीवानां चैव विष्णोश्च यो न वेत्ति भिदां पुमान् — the man who does not know the difference between the souls and Viṣṇu. His text also reads gatir asya, ‘no access to it’, for gatir atra (see the variants).

Not by reasoning

Traditional Interpretation

नैषा तर्केण मतिरापनेया प्रोक्तान्येनैव सुज्ञानाय प्रेष्ठ — ‘this understanding is not to be reached by reasoning; taught by another, dearest, it is easily known’. Death praises the boy as सत्यधृतिः, ‘steadfast in truth’, and wishes for more questioners like him.

Śaṅkara’s point is that the understanding comes from scripture, through a teacher who knows it; the reasoner who does not know scripture builds whatever his own mind imagines — तार्किको ह्यनागमज्ञः स्वबुद्धिपरिकल्पितं यत्किञ्चिदेव कल्पयति. Raṅgarāmānuja: the understanding becomes a means to release only when taught by a teacher other than oneself — स्वस्मादन्येनैव गुरुणा.

The Brahmasūtra states a related principle in its own terms: reasoning has no firm footing — तर्काप्रतिष्ठानादप्यन्यथानुमेयमिति चेदेवमप्यविमोक्षप्रसङ्गः (BS 2.1.11). Neither commentator cites the sūtra here; the comparison is this edition’s.

The pleasant is always nearer

Contemporary Reflection

A modern reflection. The Upaniṣad does not say the pleasant is evil; it says the pleasant and the good arrive together, looking alike, and that most choices are made ‘for getting and keeping’. That describes ordinary life well: the pleasant is usually nearer, more visible and easier to defend, and the good often shows its value only later.

The vallī also makes a claim about how some things are learnt. It does not deny that reasoning matters; it says that on this question reasoning alone does not settle anything, and that understanding comes through someone who has it. Whether one hears that as a statement about religious tradition or about any deep skill — a craft, a language, a discipline — is for the reader. This is a reflection, not the commentators’ teaching.

Open questions

  • Who the ‘non-other’ of 1.2.8 is: a teacher one with what he teaches (Śaṅkara), one devoted to it alone (Raṅgarāmānuja), or one who does not see the difference between the Lord and the soul, whose teaching gives no knowledge (Madhva). The compound allows all three.
  • Whether the boy is vidyābhīpsin, ‘longing for knowledge’ (the base and Śaṅkara), or vidyābhīpsita, ‘longed for by knowledge’ (Madhva’s text, and a reading Raṅgarāmānuja knew).
  • What exactly the ‘chain of wealth’ (sṛṅkā) of 1.2.3 is: Śaṅkara glosses it as a road (sṛti), Madhva as a chain (śṛṅkalā).
