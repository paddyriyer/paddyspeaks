Kaṭha Upaniṣad

Special philosophical studies

The central interpretive problems of the Upaniṣad, each with the Sanskrit, the commentators in their own words, and the open questions.

  1. I. Naciketas: the story and its frame

    Mantras 1.1.1–1.1.9: a sacrifice of everything, a gift of worn-out cows, a boy given to Death, and three nights without food at Death’s door

    The Kaṭha begins as a story. A father gives away everything at a sacrifice; his son sees that the cows are worthless and offers himself instead; the father, provoked, gives him to Death. The boy waits three nights at Death’s house without food, and Death, returning, offers three boons. This study sets out what the text says, how Śaṅkara, Raṅgarāmānuja and Madhva’s school read the frame, and where they differ.

  2. II. The three boons

    Mantras 1.1.10–1.1.20: peace with the father, the fire that leads to heaven and why it bears Naciketas’ name, and the question of what remains after death

    Naciketas asks first for his father’s good will, then for the fire that leads to heaven, and last for knowledge about what is left of a person after death. Death grants the first two at once, adds a fourth gift unasked, and hesitates over the third. The commentators agree about the first boon and divide over what ‘heaven’ means in the second and what exactly is asked in the third.

  3. III. The refusal: tests of the seeker

    Mantras 1.1.21–1.1.29: Death’s reluctance, his offers of sons, wealth, long life and pleasures, and the boy’s answers

    Death tries to withdraw the third boon: even the gods doubted this, it is subtle, choose something else. When the boy holds firm, Death offers long-lived sons, cattle and gold, a kingdom, life as long as he wishes, and the pleasures of heaven. Naciketas refuses each. The commentators agree that the passage is a test, and differ on what the subtle ‘dharma’ is and on why Death hesitates.

  4. IV. The good and the pleasant

    Mantras 1.2.1–1.2.9: the two that bind a person, knowledge and ignorance, the blind leading the blind, and why this teaching cannot be reached by reasoning alone

    Having tested the boy, Death begins to teach. The good (śreyas) and the pleasant (preyas) both take hold of a person; the wise tell them apart and choose the good, the dull choose the pleasant for the sake of getting and keeping. Knowledge and ignorance lead far apart; those who live in ignorance while thinking themselves learned go round like the blind led by the blind. The self is rarely heard of, rarely taught, rarely understood; it is not well taught by an inferior man, and the understanding Naciketas has reached cannot be got by reasoning. The commentators agree on the contrast and differ sharply on who the ‘non-other’ teacher of 1.2.8 is.

  5. V. The hidden one and the syllable Om

    Mantras 1.2.10–1.2.17: the Nāciketa fire and the permanent, what the boy let go, the god hard to see in the cave, the question beyond dharma and adharma, and the word all the Vedas proclaim

    Death admits that the permanent is not reached by things that pass, though he built the Nāciketa fire with them; he praises the boy for letting go even the highest rewards. Then he names what is to be known: the ancient god, hard to see, hidden in the cave of the heart, known by the yoga of the inner self. Naciketas asks for what is other than dharma and adharma, than what is done and undone, than past and future. Death answers with a single word, Om, the imperishable and the support. The commentators disagree on almost every key word: whose ‘permanent’, which ‘cave’, what ‘dharmya’ is, and whom Om names.

  6. VI. The unborn self and the one he chooses

    Mantras 1.2.18–1.2.25: the self that is not born and does not die, the Gītā’s version, smaller than the small, the calm of the faculties or the grace of the Creator, and ‘not by instruction… whom he chooses’

    Death now states what the self is: not born, not dying, not slain when the body is slain — two mantras the Bhagavad Gītā repeats in part. It is smaller than the small and greater than the great, set in the cave of the creature, seen by one free of desire through a ‘clarity’ the readings understand differently. It cannot be gained by instruction, intellect or much learning, only by the one whom it — or he — chooses, and not by one who has not turned from wrongdoing. The schools divide on who is described (the self that is Brahman, the individual self, or the Lord), on dhātuprasāda, and on who does the choosing.

  7. VII. Two in the cave

    Mantras 1.3.1–1.3.2: the two who drink the truth, shade and sunlight, and the bridge for those who would cross

    The third vallī opens with two who ‘drink the truth’ in the world of good deeds, both entered into the cave, the highest place — as unlike as shade and sunlight. The next mantra names a bridge for sacrificers and a fearless far shore for those who wish to cross. The commentators agree that two selves are meant, but not on which two, on whether both drink, or on what shade and sunlight mark: the self that wanders and the self that does not (Śaṅkara), the soul that knows little and the Lord who knows all (Raṅgarāmānuja), or one Lord in two forms (Madhva).

  8. VIII. The chariot

    Mantras 1.3.3–1.3.9: the self as the rider, the body as the chariot, the senses as horses, the one who enjoys, the trained and the untrained horses, and the end of the road

    Death gives the boy an image to think with. The self is the rider, the body the chariot, the intellect the charioteer, the mind the reins, the senses the horses, and the objects of sense their roads; the self joined to body, senses and mind is ‘the one who enjoys’. With an unskilled charioteer and slack reins the horses run wild and the rider does not reach the goal; with a skilled one they are like good horses, and he reaches ‘the end of the road, that highest place of Viṣṇu’. Śaṅkara and Raṅgarāmānuja agree on the image but not on whose the enjoying is; Madhva’s bhāṣya has no comment of its own on these mantras.

  9. IX. The ladder and the razor’s edge

    Mantras 1.3.10–1.3.17: from the senses to the person beyond which there is nothing, the ‘unmanifest’ and the Sāṅkhya question, the self hidden in all beings, restraint stage by stage, ‘arise, awake’, the soundless, and the close of the first adhyāya

    The chariot is now read upward: beyond the senses are their objects, beyond those the mind, the intellect, the great self, the unmanifest, and the person — ‘beyond the person there is nothing’. The self is hidden in all beings and is seen only by a sharpened, subtle intellect; the way to it is to draw speech into mind, mind into intellect, intellect into the great self, and that into the self at peace. ‘Arise, awake’, says the text: the path is a razor’s edge. The vallī closes with the soundless, touchless, deathless self and a promise to those who tell the story. The commentators differ on almost every rung — and on whether the ‘unmanifest’ is Sāṅkhya’s primal matter, which the Brahmasūtra denies.

  10. X. The openings turned outward

    Mantras 2.1.1–2.1.2: the self-born pierced the senses outward; one wise person turned the eye within, desiring immortality; the childish run after outward desires into the noose of death

    The second adhyāya opens with a diagnosis. The openings of the senses were made to face outward, so a person sees what is outside and not the self within. Yet someone wise, desiring immortality, turned the eye back and saw the inward self. The childish follow outward desires and walk into the wide-spread noose of death; the wise, knowing what is firm, ask for nothing among the things that are not. Śaṅkara and Raṅgarāmānuja agree on most of the image and differ on who the inward self is and whose noose it is; Madhva’s bhāṣya comments only on the verb.

  11. XI. This, truly, is that

    Mantras 2.1.3–2.1.9: the refrain ‘etad vai tat’; the knower of form and taste; dream and waking; the honey-eater near at hand; the first-born, Aditi and the fire in the fire-sticks; whence the sun rises

    Six of these seven mantras end with the same three words, etad vai tat — ‘this, truly, is that’. Each names something close at hand: the one by whom form and taste are known, the one who sees both dream and waking, the honey-eater, the first-born, Aditi, the fire hidden in the fire-sticks, that from which the sun rises. Each time the refrain says: this is what you asked about. The three commentators agree that ‘that’ is what Naciketas asked for, and disagree on what ‘is’ means: identity for Śaṅkara, ‘has that as its self’ for Raṅgarāmānuja, and for Madhva a series of statements about Viṣṇu.

  12. XII. What is here is there

    Mantras 2.1.10–2.1.15: from death to death goes the one who sees difference here; ‘there is no difference here at all’; the thumb-sized person; rain on the heights; pure water poured into pure water

    The vallī ends with its sharpest statements. What is here is there; whoever sees as if there were difference here goes from death to death. There is no difference here at all. A person the size of a thumb, lord of past and future, stands in the middle of the self. Water rained on the heights runs off down the slopes; pure water poured into pure water becomes just such. The three commentators read these words in three incompatible ways, each from his own commentary: Śaṅkara as the denial of any difference between the self and Brahman; Raṅgarāmānuja as one supreme self of all, with the released self becoming like it; Madhva as a denial of difference within the Lord, with the released self like him and never the same. This study sets the readings side by side and does not decide between them.

  13. XIII. The city of eleven gates

    Mantras 2.2.1–2.2.5: the body as the city of the unborn; the swan in the bright; the dwarf in the middle whom all the gods serve; what remains when the embodied one departs; not by breath does anyone live

    The second vallī of the second adhyāya begins with a city. The body is a city of eleven gates belonging to the unborn one; whoever attends to its lord does not grieve, and freed, is freed. A verse of many seats follows — the swan in the bright, the dweller in the air, the guest in the house — and then a figure in the middle, a dwarf whom all the gods serve, who leads the breaths up and down. When the embodied one slips out of the body, what is left here? Not by breath or by out-breath does anyone live, but by another, on whom those two rest. Śaṅkara reads the city as proof of a lord distinct from what serves him and of one self of all; Raṅgarāmānuja as the body seen apart from the self, and the Supreme on whom every life depends; Madhva as the city belonging to the Lord, and the breath as itself resting on him.

  14. XIV. What becomes of the self

    Mantras 2.2.6–2.2.8: the secret Death promises; some enter a womb, others the stationary, by their deeds and their learning; the person awake in those who sleep

    Death returns to the boy’s third question in three verses. He promises to tell the secret, ancient Brahman, and how the self fares on reaching death; some embodied ones enter a womb to take a body, others go to the stationary, according to their deeds and according to their learning; and the person who is awake in those who sleep, shaping desire after desire, is the bright, is Brahman, is called the immortal. The verses are read in three ways: Śaṅkara as the course of the unknowing self through births, set against the one Brahman that is its true self; Raṅgarāmānuja, taking ‘death’ as release, as the state of the released, with the births as the lot of those who turn away; Madhva as a teaching given to show the difference between the individual self and the Lord. This study reports what the text and each commentator say; it makes no claim about what happens at death.

  15. XV. The light by which all shines

    Mantras 2.2.9–2.2.15: one fire and one wind taking the form of each thing; the sun not soiled by the eye’s faults; the one controller; the eternal among the eternal; ‘there the sun does not shine’

    The vallī closes with images of one and many. As one fire entering the world takes the form of each thing, as one wind does, so the one inner self of all beings takes the form of each, and is outside as well. As the sun, the eye of the whole world, is not soiled by the eye’s faults, the inner self of all is not soiled by the world’s sorrow. The one controller, eternal among the eternal, conscious among the conscious, who grants the desires of many: those who see him standing in themselves have lasting happiness and peace. The pupil asks how that can be known — does it shine, or shine clearly? The answer is a verse the Muṇḍaka also has: there the sun does not shine, nor moon and stars, nor lightning, still less this fire; all shines after him as he shines. Śaṅkara reads these images as one self unaffected by what is superimposed on it; Raṅgarāmānuja as the Supreme indwelling each thing yet free of its faults, with a radiant form of his own; Madhva as the Lord, wholly different from the jīvas who are his reflections.

  16. XVI. The eternal aśvattha

    Mantras 2.3.1–2.3.3: the tree with its root above and branches below; all that is trembles in breath, a great fear, a raised thunderbolt; from fear of him the fire burns

    The last vallī opens with an image: the world is an aśvattha, a fig tree, with its root above and its branches below, and it is eternal. Its root is the bright, Brahman, the immortal; all the worlds rest in it and nothing goes beyond it. Whatever there is moves out of breath and trembles in it — a great fear, a raised thunderbolt — and from fear of him fire burns, the sun gives heat, and Indra, Wind and Death run their courses. The commentators agree that the root is the highest; they differ on what the tree is, what stands at its top, and what the fear is that keeps the world in order.

  17. XVII. The firm holding of the senses

    Mantras 2.3.4–2.3.11: to know before the body falls; as in a mirror; the senses apart from the self; the ladder once more; not in the field of sight; the five knowledges at rest — ‘this they call yoga’

    Death now presses the point of the whole teaching: one must come to know this here, before the body falls, or take a body again among the worlds. The self is seen here as in a mirror; elsewhere only as in a dream or in water. The wise one who knows that the senses are other than the self, with their own rising and setting, does not grieve. The ladder of the first adhyāya is climbed once more, from the senses to the person who is pervading and without a mark. That one is not in the field of sight; it is grasped by the heart, by insight, by the mind. When the five knowledges stand still with the mind and the intellect does not stir, that is the highest course — and that firm holding of the senses, the verse says, is what is called yoga. The commentators differ on how clear the mirror is, on what the ladder is for, and on what ‘yoga’ and its ‘arising and passing’ mean.

  18. XVIII. When the knots of the heart are cut

    Mantras 2.3.12–2.3.18: not by speech, mind or eye — ‘it is’; when every desire in the heart is let go; when the knots of the heart are cut, ‘this much is the teaching’; the hundred and one channels; drawing out the inner self like the reed from the grass; Naciketas reaches Brahman

    The Upaniṣad ends by saying how its subject is known and what knowing it does. It is not reached by speech, mind or eye; it is grasped as ‘it is’. When all the desires lodged in the heart are let go, and when all the knots of the heart are cut, the mortal becomes immortal — ‘this much is the teaching’. Of the heart’s hundred and one channels, one rises to the crown; going up by it one reaches immortality, the others lead every way. The thumb-sized person, the inner self, is to be drawn out from one’s body as a reed is drawn from its sheath of grass. Naciketas, having received this knowledge and the whole rule of yoga from Death, reached Brahman, free of stain and of death — and so may anyone who knows the self in this way. Here the three commentaries give three accounts of release: here and now, with no going; in two stages, with the going as the Lord’s work; or as knowledge of the Lord as other than the self.

  19. XIX. The Kaṭha as one teaching

    Mantras 1.1.1–2.3.18: the story that frames Death’s teaching, the two adhyāyas, and how Śaṅkara, Raṅgarāmānuja and Madhva read the whole

    The Kaṭha begins as a story — a father’s gift, a son sent to Death, three boons — and ends by returning to it: Naciketas, having received Death’s knowledge and the whole rule of yoga, reaches Brahman, and so does anyone who knows the self as he did. Between, the first adhyāya closes with a praise of the tale itself, and the second opens without it. The three commentators read the six vallīs as one work: for Śaṅkara the story praises the knowledge of the inner self; for Raṅgarāmānuja the whole Upaniṣad, as the Brahmasūtra decides, is about the Supreme; for Madhva it establishes that Viṣṇu is the highest.
