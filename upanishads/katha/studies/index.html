Kaṭha Upaniṣad

Special philosophical studies

The central interpretive problems of the Upaniṣad, each with the Sanskrit, the commentators in their own words, and the open questions.

  1. I. Naciketas: the story and its frame

    Mantras 1.1.1–1.1.9: a sacrifice of everything, a gift of worn-out cows, a boy given to Death, and three nights without food at Death’s door

    The Kaṭha begins as a story. A father gives away everything at a sacrifice; his son sees that the cows are worthless and offers himself instead; the father, provoked, gives him to Death. The boy waits three nights at Death’s house without food, and Death, returning, offers three boons. This study sets out what the text says, how Śaṅkara, Raṅgarāmānuja and Madhva’s school read the frame, and where they differ.

  2. II. The three boons

    Mantras 1.1.10–1.1.20: peace with the father, the fire that leads to heaven and why it bears Naciketas’ name, and the question of what remains after death

    Naciketas asks first for his father’s good will, then for the fire that leads to heaven, and last for knowledge about what is left of a person after death. Death grants the first two at once, adds a fourth gift unasked, and hesitates over the third. The commentators agree about the first boon and divide over what ‘heaven’ means in the second and what exactly is asked in the third.

  3. III. The refusal: tests of the seeker

    Mantras 1.1.21–1.1.29: Death’s reluctance, his offers of sons, wealth, long life and pleasures, and the boy’s answers

    Death tries to withdraw the third boon: even the gods doubted this, it is subtle, choose something else. When the boy holds firm, Death offers long-lived sons, cattle and gold, a kingdom, life as long as he wishes, and the pleasures of heaven. Naciketas refuses each. The commentators agree that the passage is a test, and differ on what the subtle ‘dharma’ is and on why Death hesitates.
