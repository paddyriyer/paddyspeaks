The city of eleven gates

Mantras 2.2.1–2.2.5: the body as the city of the unborn; the swan in the bright; the dwarf in the middle whom all the gods serve; what remains when the embodied one departs; not by breath does anyone live

Anuvākas 2.2.1, 2.2.2, 2.2.3, 2.2.4, 2.2.5

The second vallī of the second adhyāya begins with a city. The body is a city of eleven gates belonging to the unborn one; whoever attends to its lord does not grieve, and freed, is freed. A verse of many seats follows — the swan in the bright, the dweller in the air, the guest in the house — and then a figure in the middle, a dwarf whom all the gods serve, who leads the breaths up and down. When the embodied one slips out of the body, what is left here? Not by breath or by out-breath does anyone live, but by another, on whom those two rest. Śaṅkara reads the city as proof of a lord distinct from what serves him and of one self of all; Raṅgarāmānuja as the body seen apart from the self, and the Supreme on whom every life depends; Madhva as the city belonging to the Lord, and the breath as itself resting on him.

The words

Direct Textual Meaning

पुरमेकादशद्वारमजस्यावक्रचेतसः — ‘a city of eleven gates belongs to the unborn one, whose thought is not crooked’ — अनुष्ठाय न शोचति विमुक्तश्च विमुच्यते — ‘attending to it, one does not grieve; and freed, one is freed’. The refrain of the first vallī returns: एतद्वै तत्, ‘this, truly, is that’.

2.2.2 is a chain of seats and births: the swan seated in the bright (हंसः शुचिषत्), the good one in the mid-air, the priest at the altar, the guest in the house, the one seated in men, in the excellent, in truth, in the sky; born of the waters, of cattle, of truth, of the mountains — ‘truth, the great’ (ऋतं बृहत्). 2.2.3: ऊर्ध्वं प्राणमुन्नयति अपानं प्रत्यगस्यति — ‘he leads the breath upward and casts the out-breath down’ — मध्ये वामनमासीनं विश्वे देवा उपासते — ‘the dwarf seated in the middle, all the gods serve’.

2.2.4 asks: देहाद्विमुच्यमानस्य किमत्र परिशिष्यते — ‘when [the embodied one] is being freed from the body, what remains here?’ 2.2.5 answers by negation: न प्राणेन नापानेन मर्त्यो जीवति कश्चन — ‘not by breath, not by out-breath does any mortal live’ — इतरेण तु जीवन्ति यस्मिन्नेतावुपाश्रितौ — ‘but by another, in whom these two rest’.

A city needs a lord (Śaṅkara)

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara takes the image as an argument. A city, with its gates, gatekeepers and officers, exists for a master who is not part of it; the body, too, assembled from many parts, exists for a master not assembled with it: शरीरं पुरम्. He counts the gates — सप्त शीर्षण्यानि, नाभ्या सहार्वाञ्चि त्रीणि, शिरस्येकम् — seven in the head, three lower ones with the navel, one at the crown. Its owner is the unborn self, Brahman, whose thought is ever one like the sun’s light; ‘attending’ means meditating on him with right knowledge, and the one who is freed here from the bonds of ignorance पुनः शरीरं न गृह्णातीत्यर्थः — ‘does not take a body again’.

He reads the swan verse as the one self dwelling in every city, not one only, and allows that the verse may describe the sun without conflict, since the sun too has the self as its nature; its sense is सर्वथाप्येक एवात्मा जगतः, नात्मभेद इति मन्त्रार्थः. The ‘dwarf’ in 2.2.3 is वामनं वननीयं सम्भजनीयं, one to be resorted to, served by the senses as subjects serve a king.

2.2.4–5 complete the argument: when the self leaves, the whole assembly of body and senses at once falls powerless, as a city when its lord departs; so सोऽन्यः सिद्ध आत्मा, the self is established as other. Nothing assembled lives by itself; all live इतरेणैव संहतप्राणादिविलक्षणेन, by another, different from the assembled breaths.

The body seen apart, the Supreme on whom life rests (Raṅgarāmānuja)

Traditional Interpretation

For Raṅgarāmānuja the city belongs to the discerning self, unborn and straight of mind: the body, एकादशेन्द्रियलक्षणबहिर्निर्गमद्वारोपेतं शरीराख्यं पुरं भवति, is known as distinct from the self as a city from its master, while अविवेकिनस्तु देह आत्मैव भवतीति भावः — for the undiscerning the body is the self. ‘Freed, he is freed’ names two stages: free of sorrow and passion while living, and, when the remaining karma is used up, freed from all relation to matter — प्रारब्धकर्मावसाने अचिरादिना विरजां प्राप्य प्रकृतिसम्बन्धविमुक्तो भवतीत्यर्थः — which he supports from the Brahmasūtra (4.1.19).

The swan verse lists sun, wind, priest, guest and the rest, and says all of it is अपरिच्छिन्नसत्यरूपब्रह्मात्मकमित्यर्थः, having Brahman as its self. The dwarf is the Supreme in the heart, ‘to be served’, or small because bounded by the heart’s lotus: हृदयपुण्डरीकपरिमिततया ह्रस्वपरिमाणमित्यर्थः.

He reads 2.2.4 of the meditator, quoting the Chāndogya’s ‘his delay lasts only until he is released’: when such a one’s body slackens and he dies, nothing remains to be done — कृतकृत्यत्वात् कर्तव्यं किमपि न अविशिष्यते इति भावः. And 2.2.5 states the Supreme’s greatness: यदधीनं प्राणापानयोरपि जीवनम् तदधीनमेव सर्वेषां जीवनमिति भावः.

  • Brahmasūtra 4.1.19: भोगेन त्वितरे क्षपयित्वा सम्पद्यते bhogena tvitare kṣapayitvā sampadyate
  • Chāndogya Upaniṣad 6.14.2: तस्य तावदेव चिरं यावन्न विमोक्ष्ये tasya tāvadeva ciraṃ yāvanna vimokṣye

The Lord’s city (Madhva)

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva glosses ‘attending’ as knowing that the city is the Lord’s: इदं पुरं भगवदधीनमित्यनुष्ठाय. He adds a verse, introduced only by ‘इति च’ with no source named, that the knower is freed first through absence of self-conceit — विमुक्तो निरभीमानात् पूर्वमेवापरोक्षवित् — and afterwards freed in the chief sense, when even the appearance of sorrow is gone.

On 2.2.2 his comment is a series of verses, with no source named, that read each word as a name of Hari: the swan because he is ever free of every fault — नित्यं हीनोऽखिलैर्दोषैस्साररूपो यतो हरिः — and the rest in turn, ending बृहत्पूर्णगुणत्वाच्च स एव पुरुषोत्तमः.

One passage serves 2.2.3–2.2.5: breath is not the only support of the living; it supports them by resting on Viṣṇu — किन्तु विष्णुं समाश्रित्य प्राणो जीवान् बिभर्त्ययम् — so that अतो मुख्याश्रयो विष्णुश्चेतनानां स्वतन्त्रतः. The passage says nothing of its own about 2.2.4.

Swan, dwarf and city

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The swan verse is built of compounds in -sad, ‘seated in’, and -jā, ‘born of’, each an epithet of one subject. Śaṅkara derives haṃsa from ‘going’, हन्ति गच्छतीति; Raṅgarāmānuja takes it as the sun. Śaṅkara reads śuci as the sky, Raṅgarāmānuja as the hot season.

Vāmana is read two ways. Śaṅkara derives it from √van, ‘to resort to’: वननीयं सम्भजनीयं. Raṅgarāmānuja gives that reading and, as an alternative, the ordinary sense ‘small’, because the one in the heart is bounded by the heart’s measure.

Ekādaśadvāram is a bahuvrīhi, ‘having eleven gates’, describing puram. The commentators differ on what the eleven are: Śaṅkara counts bodily openings, Raṅgarāmānuja the outlets of the eleven senses.

What lives, and by what

Contemporary Reflection

A modern reflection. The section works by subtraction. A city is not its walls or gates but the one for whom they stand; breath is not what lives but what serves the living. The passage asks a reader to notice the difference between what keeps the body going and what the body is for.

The commentators agree that this ‘other’ is not the breath and disagree about who it is — the one self, the Supreme in whom the self rests, or the Lord on whom even breath depends. This is a reflection, not a claim of any commentator.

Open questions

  • Whether the swan verse of 2.2.2 describes the sun (as Raṅgarāmānuja takes haṃsa, and as Śaṅkara allows) or the self, and how the two readings are reconciled.
  • How the eleven gates are to be counted: bodily openings (Śaṅkara) or the outlets of the eleven senses (Raṅgarāmānuja).
  • The verses in Madhva’s comment on 2.2.1–2.2.2 name no source; whether they are his own or quoted needs a printed edition.
