The hidden one and the syllable Om

Mantras 1.2.10–1.2.17: the Nāciketa fire and the permanent, what the boy let go, the god hard to see in the cave, the question beyond dharma and adharma, and the word all the Vedas proclaim

Anuvākas 1.2.10, 1.2.11, 1.2.12, 1.2.13, 1.2.14, 1.2.15, 1.2.16, 1.2.17

Death admits that the permanent is not reached by things that pass, though he built the Nāciketa fire with them; he praises the boy for letting go even the highest rewards. Then he names what is to be known: the ancient god, hard to see, hidden in the cave of the heart, known by the yoga of the inner self. Naciketas asks for what is other than dharma and adharma, than what is done and undone, than past and future. Death answers with a single word, Om, the imperishable and the support. The commentators disagree on almost every key word: whose ‘permanent’, which ‘cave’, what ‘dharmya’ is, and whom Om names.

The permanent is not reached by what passes

Comparative Vedānta

Death speaks of himself: जानाम्यहं शेवधिरित्यनित्यं — ‘I know that what is called treasure is impermanent’ — न ह्यध्रुवैः प्राप्यते हि ध्रुवं तत्, ‘for the permanent is not reached by things that are not permanent’. Yet he goes on: ततो मया नाचिकेतश्चितोऽग्निरनित्यैर्द्रव्यैः प्राप्तवानस्मि नित्यम् — ‘so I built the Nāciketa fire with impermanent things, and have reached the permanent’. The two halves seem to contradict each other, and each commentator removes the contradiction differently.

Śaṅkara: the ‘permanent’ Death reached is only relatively so — his own station, called heaven: नित्यं याम्यं स्थानं स्वर्गाख्यं नित्यमापेक्षिकं प्राप्तवानस्मि. Raṅgarāmānuja: Death built the fire with the aim of the knowledge that leads to Brahman, and so obtained that knowledge, the means to the permanent fruit — नित्यफलसाधनं ज्ञानं प्राप्तवानस्मि — so that there is no conflict with the rule that Brahman is reached by knowledge alone: ब्रह्मप्राप्तेः ज्ञानैकसाध्यत्वस्य न विरोध.

Madhva takes the permanent to be the Lord — ध्रुवो भगवान् — and the impermanent to be those without devotion to him: अध्रुवैस्तद्भक्तिवर्जितैर्न प्राप्यते. His text reads naciketaḥ, a vocative, where the base has nāciketaḥ, ‘the Nāciketa [fire]’ (see the variants).

What the boy let go

Comparative Vedānta

Death lists what Naciketas has seen and released: कामस्याप्तिं जगतः प्रतिष्ठां — the fulfilment of desire, the foundation of the world — the endless fruit of rites, the far shore of fearlessness, the great praised wide-ranging foundation; and having seen it, दृष्ट्वा धृत्या धीरो नचिकेतोऽत्यस्राक्षीः, ‘wise Naciketas, you let it go with firmness’.

For Śaṅkara the list describes the highest state within the world, the station of Hiraṇyagarbha — क्रतोः उपासनायाः फलं हैरण्यगर्भं पदम् — and the boy’s letting even that go draws Death’s admiration: अहो बतानुत्तमगुणोऽसि. Raṅgarāmānuja reads the first phrases as the pleasures of every world up to Brahmā’s, and the rest as the nature of release; having seen both, the boy gave up the worldly desires — लौकिकान् कामान् प्रज्ञाशालित्वात् त्यक्तवानसि. He then offers a second reading in which every phrase describes the Supreme Self.

Madhva reads the epithets of the Lord alone, and draws a conclusion about the whole dialogue: since the object is called ‘wide-ranging’ (urugāya), उरुगायमित्युक्तत्वाच्च न जीवविषयोऽयं प्रश्नः — ‘the question is not about the soul’. He adds that one who had died and come to Yama could hardly doubt whether the dead exist: न च मृत्वा यमं प्राप्तस्य नचिकेतसो मृतोऽस्ति न वेति संशयो युज्यते.

The god hard to see, in the cave

Comparative Vedānta

Now Death names what is to be known: तं दुर्दर्शं गूढमनुप्रविष्टं गुहाहितं गह्वरेष्ठं पुराणम् — ‘him, hard to see, hidden, entered in, set in the cave, dwelling in the deep, ancient’ — and अध्यात्मयोगाधिगमेन देवं मत्वा धीरो हर्षशोकौ जहाति, ‘having known that god by the yoga of the inner self, the wise one leaves joy and sorrow behind’.

Śaṅkara: the god is the self; the cave is the intellect, where it is perceived — गुहायां बुद्धौ हितं निहितं — and the ‘deep’ is the tangle of troubles in which it dwells: गह्वरे विषमे अनेकानर्थसङ्कटे तिष्ठतीति गह्वरेष्ठम्. The yoga of the inner self is withdrawing the mind from objects and settling it in the self: विषयेभ्यः प्रतिसंहृत्य चेतस आत्मनि समाधानमध्यात्मयोगः.

Raṅgarāmānuja: the god is the Supreme Self, and ‘dwelling in the deep’ means the inner controller of the individual self — आत्मान्तर्यामिणम्. The yoga is knowledge of the individual self, which is a means to knowledge of the Supreme: जीवात्मज्ञानस्य परमात्मज्ञानहेतुत्वादिति भावः. He defends this against reading the god as the individual self, appealing to the Brahmasūtra on ‘the two who have entered the cave’ (BS 1.2.11) and to its commentary, which cites this mantra for the Supreme Self’s entering the cave.

Madhva gives the ‘deep’ a third sense: गह्वरे मुक्तजीवे स्थितम् — ‘dwelling in the released soul’.

Lifting out the subtle one

Comparative Vedānta

एतच्छ्रुत्वा सम्परिगृह्य मर्त्यः प्रवृह्य धर्म्यमणुमेतमाप्य — ‘having heard this and grasped it, a mortal, drawing out the dharmya, reaching this subtle one’ — rejoices, having found what is to be rejoiced in. Death ends: विवृतं सद्म नचिकेतसं मन्ये, ‘I think the house is open for Naciketas’.

The commentators divide the second phrase differently. For Śaṅkara dharmya describes the self, ‘not departing from dharma’ — धर्मादनपेतं धर्म्यं — and pravṛhya means lifting it apart from the body: प्रवृह्य उद्यम्य पृथक्कृत्य शरीरादेः. The open house means that Naciketas is fit for release: मोक्षार्हं त्वां मन्ये इत्यभिप्रायः. For Raṅgarāmānuja dharmya is what is drawn away — the body and the rest, produced by action, कर्मसाध्यं शरीरादि — and the subtle one reached is the Supreme Self. For Madhva what is drawn apart is the Lord, distinguished from the soul: प्रवृह्य जीवात् पृथक्कृत्य.

The verb returns at the very end of the Upaniṣad, where the person the size of a thumb is to be drawn out of one’s own body: तं स्वाच्छरीरात्प्रवृहेन्मुञ्जादिवेषीकां धैर्येण (2.3.17).

Other than dharma and adharma

Comparative Vedānta

अन्यत्र धर्मादन्यत्राधर्मादन्यत्रास्मात्कृताकृतात् — ‘other than dharma, other than adharma, other than this, done and undone’ — अन्यत्र भूताच्च भव्याच्च यत्तत्पश्यसि तद्वद, ‘other than past and future: what you see as that, tell it’. The mantra does not name its speaker.

Śaṅkara gives it to Naciketas, asking again — एतच्छ्रुत्वा नचिकेताः पुनराह — for what is outside the whole world of action and its fruit, cause and effect, and the three times: कालत्रयेण यन्न परिच्छिद्यत इत्यर्थः.

Raṅgarāmānuja discusses the mantra at length, with the Brahmasūtra’s statement that this Upaniṣad presents three topics and three questions: त्रयाणामेव चैवमुपन्यासः प्रश्नश्च (BS 1.4.6). On one of the readings he reports, ‘other than dharma and adharma’ asks about the means — a meditation unlike merit and sin — धर्मादधर्माच्च अन्यत्र इति उपासनप्रश्नः, and the rest asks about a goal not bounded by time: कालापरिच्छिन्नमुपेयं पृष्टम्. He weighs the objection that all four ‘other than’ phrases should describe Brahman, the goal, and rejects it: after the teaching that Brahman is reached by knowledge and not by rites, ‘other than dharma’ is best taken to ask about that knowledge — धर्मविलक्षणज्ञानरूपोपायपरत्वमेव युक्तम्.

  • Brahmasūtra 1.4.6: त्रयाणामेव चैवमुपन्यासः प्रश्नश्च trayāṇāmeva caivamupanyāsaḥ praśnaśca

The word all the Vedas proclaim

Comparative Vedānta

Death answers in one word: सर्वे वेदा यत्पदमामनन्ति — ‘the word that all the Vedas proclaim’, that all austerities declare, desiring which men live the life of a student — तत्ते पदं सङ्ग्रहेण ब्रवीम्योमित्येतत्, ‘that word I tell you in brief: it is Om’. Two short mantras follow: एतद्ध्येवाक्षरं ब्रह्म एतद्ध्येवाक्षरं परम् — ‘this syllable is Brahman, this syllable is the highest’ — and एतदालम्बनं श्रेष्ठमेतदालम्बनं परम्, ‘this support is the best, this support is the highest’.

The Gītā (8.11) has the same half-line, यदिच्छन्तो ब्रह्मचर्यं चरन्ति तत्ते पदं सङ्ग्रहेण प्रवक्ष्ये, with a different verb, and goes on (8.13) to the syllable Om.

Śaṅkara: Om is both the name and the symbol of what was asked — ओंशब्दवाच्यमोंशब्दप्रतीकं च — and of the two Brahmans, the higher and the lower: तयोर्हि प्रतीकमेतदक्षरम्. Whoever meditates on it gains whichever he seeks: the higher is to be known, the lower to be attained — परं चेत् ज्ञातव्यम् , अपरं चेत् प्राप्तव्यम्. The Praśna Upaniṣad (5.2) says the same of the two: एतद्वै सत्यकाम परं चापरं च ब्रह्म यदोङ्कारः.

Raṅgarāmānuja: Om is the word that denotes Brahman — प्रणवस्य ब्रह्मवाचकत्वात् — and the two mantras praise it as a word: एवं वाचकं प्रणवं द्वाभ्यां मन्त्राभ्यां स्तौति. It is called Brahman because it is the means of reaching Brahman — ब्रह्मप्राप्तिसाधनत्वात् ब्रह्म — and its letters a and m name the Supreme and the soul. Madhva cites, without naming its source, a verse that calls the syllable Brahman named Viṣṇu: एतद्ध्येवाक्षरं ब्रह्म विष्ण्वाख्यं परमव्ययम्.

  • Bhagavad Gītā 8.11: यदिच्छन्तो ब्रह्मचर्यं चरन्ति तत्ते पदं सङ्ग्रहेण प्रवक्ष्ये yadicchanto brahmacaryaṃ caranti tatte padaṃ saṅgraheṇa pravakṣye
  • Praśna Upaniṣad 5.2: एतद्वै सत्यकाम परं चापरं च ब्रह्म यदोङ्कारः etadvai satyakāma paraṃ cāparaṃ ca brahma yadoṅkāraḥ

A word as a support

Contemporary Reflection

A modern reflection. The vallī moves from the hardest thing to see — ‘hidden in the cave’, ‘beyond past and future’ — to the simplest thing to say: one syllable. The word the text uses for it is ālambana, a support, something to lean on or hold. Whatever one believes about what the syllable names, the structure is familiar: what cannot be grasped directly is approached through something that can be held.

The commentators do not agree on what Om names, or on whether the support is to be crossed or kept. The text gives the support and leaves the leaning to the reader. This is a reflection, not a claim of any commentator.

Open questions

  • How Death can say both that the permanent is not reached by impermanent things and that he reached it by a fire built of them: a relative permanence (Śaṅkara), the knowledge that leads to the permanent (Raṅgarāmānuja), or the Lord, reached by devotion (Madhva).
  • Who speaks 1.2.14 and what it asks: Naciketas asking again (Śaṅkara), or a question that first asks about the means of release, knowledge unlike rites (Raṅgarāmānuja). The mantra names no speaker.
  • Whether dharmya in 1.2.13 describes the self (Śaṅkara) or names the body that is drawn away from it (Raṅgarāmānuja).
