Study X · Kaṭha Upaniṣad

The openings turned outward

Mantras 2.1.1–2.1.2: the self-born pierced the senses outward; one wise person turned the eye within, desiring immortality; the childish run after outward desires into the noose of death

Anuvākas 2.1.1, 2.1.2

The second adhyāya opens with a diagnosis. The openings of the senses were made to face outward, so a person sees what is outside and not the self within. Yet someone wise, desiring immortality, turned the eye back and saw the inward self. The childish follow outward desires and walk into the wide-spread noose of death; the wise, knowing what is firm, ask for nothing among the things that are not. Śaṅkara and Raṅgarāmānuja agree on most of the image and differ on who the inward self is and whose noose it is; Madhva’s bhāṣya comments only on the verb.

Two verses, one turn

Direct Textual Meaning

पराञ्चि खानि व्यतृणत्स्वयम्भूस्तस्मात्पराङ् पश्यति नान्तरात्मन् — ‘the self-born pierced the openings outward; therefore one sees outward, not within the self’. कश्चिद्धीरः प्रत्यगात्मानमैक्षदावृत्तचक्षुरमृतत्वमिच्छन् — ‘a certain wise one, desiring immortality, with eye turned back, saw the inward self’.

The second verse divides people by where they go. पराचः कामाननुयन्ति बालास्ते मृत्योर्यन्ति विततस्य पाशम् — ‘the childish follow outward desires; they go into the noose of wide-spread death’. अथ धीरा अमृतत्वं विदित्वा ध्रुवमध्रुवेष्विह न प्रार्थयन्ते — ‘but the wise, having known immortality as the firm thing, ask for nothing here among the unfirm’.

The verbs carry the contrast: the openings were pierced outward, the many follow outward, the one turned back. What the first verse calls ‘the inward self’ it does not define; the commentators do.

Why the vallī begins here

Traditional Interpretation

The first adhyāya ended with a call to rise and wake. Śaṅkara, in the words printed at the head of this vallī, links it to 1.3.12, where the self is said to be seen by a subtle intellect, and asks what stands in that intellect’s way: the vallī begins, he says, to show the cause of not seeing, since an obstacle can be removed only once it is known.

Raṅgarāmānuja makes the same link from the other side. Death, having urged ‘arise, awake’, sees people still turned away from the self and grieves: इति प्रोत्साहनेऽपि आत्मस्वरूपविमुखान् पश्यन् शोचति. The verse is a lament before it is a teaching.

Who pierced the openings, and how

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara takes the self-born to be the highest Lord, called so because he is always independent: स्वयम्भूः परमेश्वरः स्वयमेव स्वतन्त्रो भवति सर्वदा न परतन्त्र इति. The verb means he struck or wounded them: व्यतृणत् हिंसितवान् हननं कृतवानित्यर्थः. The senses are named ‘openings’ after the openings of the body through which they work, and they go outward to show sound and the rest.

Raṅgarāmānuja also reads ‘wounded’ — स्वतन्त्र ईश्वरः इमानि खानि हिंसितवान् — and offers a second reading: since roots have many senses, the verb may mean that he made the senses to show other things: यद्वा धातूनाम् अनेकार्थत्वात् परार्थप्रकाशकानि इन्द्रियाणि सृष्टवान् इत्यर्थः.

Madhva’s only comment on this mantra names the root of the verb: तृणु कात्करणे इति धातुः. The meaning given for the root in the digital text is not clear, and this edition does not guess at it.

The inward self and the turned eye

Comparative Vedānta

For Śaṅkara the inward self is the self that is inward and is the self: प्रत्यक् चासावात्मा चेति प्रत्यगात्मा. The ‘eye’ stands for all the senses, turned back from every object: आवृत्तं व्यावृत्तं चक्षुः श्रोत्रादिकमिन्द्रियजातमशेषविषयात् यस्य स आवृत्तचक्षुः. The two directions exclude each other: न हि बाह्यविषयालोचनपरत्वं प्रत्यगात्मेक्षणं चैकस्य सम्भवति. The wise one is like a river made to run against its current: नद्याः प्रतिस्रोतःप्रवर्तनमिव.

Raṅgarāmānuja uses the same image — नद्याः प्रतिस्रोतः प्रवृत्त इव — and the same reading of the eye: चक्षुश्शब्दः इन्द्रियमात्रपरः. The wise one is a seeker of release whose senses have each turned from their objects: स्वस्वविषयव्यावृत्तेन्द्रियो मुमुक्षुः इत्यर्थः. But he adds that the supreme self too may be called inward, because it is the principal referent of ‘I’ in every self: परमात्मनः सर्वजीवगताहन्तास्पदत्वेन मुख्याहमर्थत्वात्, प्रत्यक्त्वमस्तीति द्रष्टव्यम्. For Śaṅkara the inward self seen is one’s own nature; for Raṅgarāmānuja the inward turn leads to a self within which the supreme self dwells as its self.

Both note the grammar. ‘Saw’ (aikṣat) is a past form read as present, as the Veda allows: Śaṅkara, ऐक्षत् अपश्यत् पश्यतीत्यर्थः, छन्दसि कालानियमात्; Raṅgarāmānuja also calls the active ending Vedic: छान्दसं परस्मैपदम्.

The noose of death and the firm thing

Comparative Vedānta

Śaṅkara names two obstacles: ignorance, which sees only outward, and craving for the pleasures it shows. The death whose noose the childish enter is not one event but the whole bundle of ignorance, desire and action: मृत्योः अविद्याकामकर्मसमुदायस्य. The noose is the joining to and parting from body and senses: देहेन्द्रियादिसंयोगवियोगलक्षणम्. The immortality of the gods is not firm — देवाद्यमृतत्वं ह्यध्रुवम् — and the wise therefore ask for nothing in saṃsāra; they rise above the desires for sons, wealth and worlds: पुत्रवित्तलोकैषणाभ्यो व्युत्तिष्ठन्त्येवेत्यभिप्रायः.

Raṅgarāmānuja gives a second reading in which the speaker names himself: ‘wide-spread’ means one whose command is nowhere obstructed, and the noose is Death’s own — विततस्य - सर्वत्र अप्रतिहताज्ञस्य मृत्योः मम पाशं यान्ति इत्यर्थः. For him the wise find the firm immortality in the inward self — धीमन्तः प्रत्यगात्मन्येव ध्रुवम् - अमृतत्वं विदित्वा — and for the one who knows that principle, everything else is worth asking about: प्रत्यक्तत्त्वज्ञस्य सर्वं जिज्ञासितव्यम् इति भावः.

The direction of attention

Contemporary Reflection

A modern reflection. The verse does not blame the senses for facing outward; it says they were made so. What it praises is rare and deliberate: one person who, wanting something that does not pass, turned round. Read today, it is a description of how attention usually runs, and of what it would take to reverse it.

The commentators mean something more exact — the self that Śaṅkara calls one’s own nature, the self in which Raṅgarāmānuja finds the Lord — and they differ on it. This is a reflection, not a claim of any commentator, and not advice about any practice.

Open questions

  • Madhva’s gloss of the root of vyatṛṇat (‘तृणु कात्करणे’) is unclear in the digital text; a printed edition is needed to read it.
  • In Madhva’s text a passage that glosses ‘इदं गुह्यं ब्रह्म प्रवक्ष्यामि … यथा मरणं प्राप्य’ — words of 2.2.6 — stands after 2.1.3; whether it belongs there or has been displaced needs a printed edition.
  • Whether ‘the noose of wide-spread death’ is Death speaking of himself, as Raṅgarāmānuja’s second reading has it, or death as the bundle of ignorance, desire and action, as Śaṅkara reads it.
