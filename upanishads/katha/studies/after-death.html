Study XIV · Kaṭha Upaniṣad

What becomes of the self

Mantras 2.2.6–2.2.8: the secret Death promises; some enter a womb, others the stationary, by their deeds and their learning; the person awake in those who sleep

Anuvākas 2.2.6, 2.2.7, 2.2.8

Death returns to the boy’s third question in three verses. He promises to tell the secret, ancient Brahman, and how the self fares on reaching death; some embodied ones enter a womb to take a body, others go to the stationary, according to their deeds and according to their learning; and the person who is awake in those who sleep, shaping desire after desire, is the bright, is Brahman, is called the immortal. The verses are read in three ways: Śaṅkara as the course of the unknowing self through births, set against the one Brahman that is its true self; Raṅgarāmānuja, taking ‘death’ as release, as the state of the released, with the births as the lot of those who turn away; Madhva as a teaching given to show the difference between the individual self and the Lord. This study reports what the text and each commentator say; it makes no claim about what happens at death.

The words

Direct Textual Meaning

हन्त त इदं प्रवक्ष्यामि गुह्यं ब्रह्म सनातनम् — ‘now I shall tell you this secret, ancient Brahman’ — यथा च मरणं प्राप्य आत्मा भवति गौतम — ‘and how, on reaching death, the self fares, Gautama’.

योनिमन्ये प्रपद्यन्ते शरीरत्वाय देहिनः — ‘some embodied ones enter a womb, to take a body’ — स्थाणुमन्येऽनुसंयन्ति यथाकर्म यथाश्रुतम् — ‘others go to the stationary, according to their deeds, according to their learning’.

य एष सुप्तेषु जागर्ति कामं कामं पुरुषो निर्मिमाणः — ‘the person who is awake in those who sleep, shaping desire after desire’ — तदेव शुक्रं तद्ब्रह्म तदेवामृतमुच्यते — ‘that is the bright, that is Brahman, that alone is called the immortal’. All worlds rest on it; no one passes beyond it; and again, ‘this, truly, is that’.

The course of the unknowing, and the one Brahman (Śaṅkara)

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara takes Death to promise two things: the Brahman by knowing which all saṃsāra ceases, and, for the one who does not know it, how the self wanders after death — यथा आत्मा संसरति तथा शृणु हे गौतम. In 2.2.7 the unknowing enter a womb to take a body, and the lowest go to the condition of trees and other stationary things, स्थाणुं वृक्षादिस्थावरभावम्; each according to the deeds done in this life — यथाकर्म यद्यस्य कर्म — and according to the knowledge gained: यादृशं च विज्ञानमुपार्जितं तदनुरूपमेव शरीरं प्रतिपद्यन्त इत्यर्थः. He supports this with a line he cites from the Aitareya Āraṇyaka, ‘births are according to understanding’.

2.2.8 then delivers what was promised: यत्प्रतिज्ञातं गुह्यं ब्रह्म प्रवक्ष्यामीति तदाह. The one awake while the breaths and senses sleep, fashioning what is wished for, is the pure, is Brahman — तदेव शुक्रं शुभ्रं शुद्धं तद्ब्रह्म नान्यद्गुह्यं ब्रह्मास्ति — and on it all the worlds rest, since it is the cause of all.

‘Death’ as release (Raṅgarāmānuja)

Traditional Interpretation

Raṅgarāmānuja reads ‘death’ in 2.2.6 as release: the self, on reaching death — that is, मोक्षं प्राप्य — becomes of a certain kind, and Death will tell this again to a pupil fit to be taught. ‘Hanta’ he takes as an exclamation of wonder.

2.2.7 then describes the others, those who turn from hearing the truth of the Supreme and are unlike Naciketas — परमात्मतत्त्वश्रवणविमुखाः त्वद्विसदृशाः — who enter a brāhmaṇa’s or another womb, or the stationary state, without overstepping the rites and meditations they performed. He supports this with Chāndogya and Bṛhadāraṇyaka phrases he cites.

In 2.2.8 ‘desire after desire’ is a gerund, ‘willing again and again’ — संकल्प्य संकल्प्य इत्यर्थः — not the sons and pleasures offered to Naciketas in the first vallī; he refers the point to the Bhāṣya on the Brahmasūtra’s sandhya-adhikaraṇa and its Śrutaprakāśikā, as cited by him. The immortal named here is the unconditioned immortal, so the verse gives no ground for identity of the released self and the Supreme: एतेन अमृतान्तरनिषेधात् मुक्तपरमात्मनोः अभेदप्रत्याशा प्रत्युक्ता.

To show the difference (Madhva)

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva’s gloss on these verses does not stand under them in the digital text used here: a passage explaining the words of 2.2.6 and 2.2.8 stands after 2.1.3, and this edition files it under 2.1.2–2.1.3; it is cited here by that key. His entry under 2.2.6–2.2.9 concerns the fire image of 2.2.9.

In that passage Death tells how the jīva fares after death in order to show the difference between the jīva and the Lord — यथा मरणं प्राप्य जीवो भवति तच्च प्रवक्ष्यामि जीवेश्वरभेदज्ञापनाय. The one who enjoys the fruit of action and sleeps is the jīva; the one awake when the jīvas sleep in dissolution is Viṣṇu: यः प्रलयादिषु जीवेषु सुप्तेषु जागर्ति स विष्णुः परं ब्रह्मेत्यर्थः. Going to a womb is not Brahman — न हि जीवस्य योन्यादिगमनं ब्रह्म — and Brahman is rightly known only when known as different from the jīva.

Three readings of one promise

Comparative Vedānta

The three commentators read ‘how the self fares on reaching death’ differently. For Śaṅkara it is the wandering of the self that does not know Brahman — यथा आत्मा संसरति — answered by the one Brahman that is its self. For Raṅgarāmānuja ‘death’ means release, and the births of 2.2.7 belong to others. For Madhva the verses are told so that the jīva, who goes to wombs, is known as different from Viṣṇu, who wakes when the jīvas sleep.

The Bṛhadāraṇyaka (4.4.6) also ties what a person becomes to deeds and attachment: तदेव सक्तः सह कर्मणैति. The Kaṭha adds ‘according to learning’, yathāśrutam, beside ‘according to deeds’; all three commentators keep both.

None of the readings is given here as the meaning of the text, and none is a statement of fact about death.

  • Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.4.6: तदेव सक्तः सह कर्मणैति tadeva saktaḥ saha karmaṇaiti

Awake in those who sleep

Contemporary Reflection

A modern reflection. The answer to a question about death turns out to be about sleep. In each night something keeps on — dreams are shaped, desires take form — while the body and senses rest. The verse points to that wakefulness and calls it the immortal.

Whether that wakeful one is the reader’s own self, the Supreme within the self, or the Lord distinct from it is where the commentators part. This is a reflection, not a claim of any commentator, and it makes no claim about what happens at death.

Open questions

  • Whether maraṇa in 2.2.6 means death followed by further birth (Śaṅkara, Madhva) or release (Raṅgarāmānuja).
  • Whether the births of 2.2.7 describe all who die without knowledge (Śaṅkara) or those who turn away from the teaching (Raṅgarāmānuja).
  • Madhva’s gloss on 2.2.6 and 2.2.8 stands after 2.1.3 in the digital text used here; whether it belongs there needs a printed edition.
  • The line Śaṅkara cites from the Aitareya Āraṇyaka, and the Bhāṣya and Śrutaprakāśikā Raṅgarāmānuja refers to, are reported as cited by them; this edition has not verified them in those works.
