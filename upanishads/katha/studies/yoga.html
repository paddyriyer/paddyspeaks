Study XVII · Kaṭha Upaniṣad

The firm holding of the senses

Mantras 2.3.4–2.3.11: to know before the body falls; as in a mirror; the senses apart from the self; the ladder once more; not in the field of sight; the five knowledges at rest — ‘this they call yoga’

Anuvākas 2.3.4, 2.3.5, 2.3.6, 2.3.7, 2.3.8, 2.3.9, 2.3.10, 2.3.11

Death now presses the point of the whole teaching: one must come to know this here, before the body falls, or take a body again among the worlds. The self is seen here as in a mirror; elsewhere only as in a dream or in water. The wise one who knows that the senses are other than the self, with their own rising and setting, does not grieve. The ladder of the first adhyāya is climbed once more, from the senses to the person who is pervading and without a mark. That one is not in the field of sight; it is grasped by the heart, by insight, by the mind. When the five knowledges stand still with the mind and the intellect does not stir, that is the highest course — and that firm holding of the senses, the verse says, is what is called yoga. The commentators differ on how clear the mirror is, on what the ladder is for, and on what ‘yoga’ and its ‘arising and passing’ mean.

The words

Direct Textual Meaning

2.3.4: इह चेदशकद्बोद्धुं प्राक्शरीरस्य विस्रसः — ‘if one has been able to know it here, before the body falls apart’ — and if not, one becomes fit for embodiment in the created worlds. 2.3.5 gives the degrees of seeing: यथादर्शे तथात्मनि यथा स्वप्ने तथा पितृलोके — ‘as in a mirror, so in oneself; as in a dream, so in the world of the fathers’ — as in water, so in the world of the gandharvas, and च्छायातपयोरिव ब्रह्मलोके, ‘as shade and sunlight, in the world of Brahmā’.

2.3.6: knowing the separateness of the senses and their rising and setting, मत्वा धीरो न शोचति, ‘the wise one does not grieve’. 2.3.7–8 repeat the ladder of 1.3.10–11 with a change of wording: इन्द्रियेभ्यः परं मनो मनसः सत्त्वमुत्तमम् — ‘beyond the senses is mind, beyond mind the highest, sattva’ — up to अव्यक्तात्तु परः पुरुषो व्यापकोऽलिङ्ग एव च, ‘beyond the unmanifest the person, pervading and without a mark’.

2.3.9: न सन्दृशे तिष्ठति रूपमस्य न चक्षुषा पश्यति कश्चनैनम् — ‘his form does not stand within sight; no one sees him with the eye’ — he is framed हृदा मनीषा मनसाभिक्लृप्तो, ‘by the heart, by insight, by the mind’. 2.3.10–11: यदा पञ्चावतिष्ठन्ते ज्ञानानि मनसा सह — ‘when the five knowledges stand still together with the mind’ — and the intellect does not stir, that is the highest course; तां योगमिति मन्यन्ते स्थिरामिन्द्रियधारणाम् — ‘that firm holding of the senses they consider yoga’ — अप्रमत्तस्तदा भवति योगो हि प्रभवाप्ययौ, ‘then one becomes heedful, for yoga is arising and passing away’.

Here, in a clear mirror; yoga as disjunction (Śaṅkara)

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara draws the practical conclusion of 2.3.4: effort for knowing the self must be made before the body falls, तस्माच्छरीरविस्रंसनात्प्रागात्मावबोधाय यत्न आस्थेयः. In 2.3.5 the mirror is the clearest of the images: as one sees one’s reflection in a mirror quite distinctly, यथा आदर्शे प्रतिबिम्बभूतमात्मानं पश्यति लोकः अत्यन्तविविक्तम्, so here, in one’s own intellect made clear like a mirror, the self is seen distinctly. In the world of the fathers and of the gandharvas the seeing is blurred, as in a dream or in water; only in the world of Brahmā is it as distinct as shade and sunlight, and that world is hard to reach — so the effort belongs here.

On 2.3.6 the senses arise from their own causes, and their rising and setting in waking and dream are theirs, not the self’s: उदयास्तमयौ च उत्पत्तिप्रलयौ जाग्रत्स्वप्नावस्थाप्रतिपत्त्या नात्मन इति मत्वा. In 2.3.7 sattva means the intellect, सत्त्वशब्दाद्बुद्धिरिहोच्यते, and the objects are included under the senses. The person ‘without a mark’ of 2.3.8 is free of every quality of transmigration: सर्वसंसारधर्मवर्जित इत्येतत्.

On 2.3.11 he turns the word: they call this state yoga, though it is really disjunction — ताम् ईदृशीं तदवस्थां योगमिति मन्यन्ते वियोगमेव सन्तम् — the state of the yogin is separation from every connection with harm. ‘Arising and passing’ means that yoga comes and goes, उपजनापायधर्मक इत्यर्थः, and so heedfulness is enjoined to keep it from passing: अतः अपायपरिहारायाप्रमादः कर्तव्य इत्यभिप्रायः.

A dim mirror; refuge as the means (Raṅgarāmānuja)

Traditional Interpretation

Raṅgarāmānuja agrees on 2.3.4 — strive for knowledge of the self before the body falls, तस्मात् शरीरपातात्प्रागेव आत्मज्ञानाय यतेत इति भावः — but reads 2.3.5 the other way. The verse shows how hard the self is to know, आत्मनो दुर्बोधत्वमेवाह: what is seen in a mirror is not seen as the thing itself is, and the images that follow — dream, water, shade mixed with sunlight — are all of unclear seeing; even in the world of Brahmā there is no full perception, एवं ब्रह्मलोकेऽपि न सम्यक् उपलम्भः. In an alternative reading he grants that the self can be discerned there, but says it is not easy for those who live here.

On 2.3.6 rising, setting and difference belong to the senses and the body, and are not in the self whose one form is knowledge: ज्ञानैकाकारे आत्मनि न सन्ति इति ज्ञात्वा न शोचति इत्यर्थः. He takes 2.3.7–8 together and gives the ladder a purpose of its own: even with knowledge of the self as distinct from the body, refuge in the Lord is the means — देहविविक्तप्रत्यगात्मयाथात्म्यज्ञानेऽपि भगवच्छरणागतिरेव उपायः — for the highest is to be won over, and the winning over of the Supreme is surrender alone: परस्य च वशीकरणं शरणागतिरेव.

On 2.3.9–11 he draws on Vyāsārya, whom he cites: the ‘knowledges’ are the senses, and the ‘highest course’ is the going for release, leaving off the movement within the body, शरीरान्तस्सञ्चरणं विहाय मोक्षार्थगमनं परमा गतिः. Heedfulness is needed because yoga can slip at any moment, योगस्य प्रतिक्षणापायशालितया अवधानम् अपेक्षितमिति भावः — or, in a second reading, because yoga is the means to every human end.

The Lord seen by the eye of knowledge (Madhva)

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva’s comment on this passage is a few verses closing इति च, with no source named. On 2.3.5 it is the Lord dwelling in the jīva who is seen, by those whose sight is knowledge, as a face in a mirror: जीवे स्थितस्तु भगवान् दृश्यते ज्ञानदृष्टिभिः, आदर्शे मुखवत् सम्यङ् न तथा पितृलोकगः — not so in the world of the fathers, somewhat more clearly among the gandharvas, and clearly in the world of Brahmā: स्पष्टं तथा ब्रह्मलोके दृश्यते पुरुषोत्तमः.

The ladder is repeated, he says, to show that the great purport of every passage is the Lord’s supremacy, known through the gradation of the deities: तारतम्यपरिज्ञानपूर्वकं सर्वतो हरेः. On 2.3.9 no one sees Viṣṇu with the senses apart from his manifestations, and the yogin is released only when he sees even those with the eye of knowledge: तदैव मुच्यते योगी न दुष्टैरिन्द्रियैः क्वचित्. On 2.3.11 he reads ‘arising and passing’ as coming from the Lord: भगवतस्सकाशात् प्रभवाप्ययौ.

In the digital text used here his bhāṣya has no comment of its own on 2.3.4, 2.3.6 or 2.3.10: the passages filed under them concern 2.3.5, the ladder of 2.3.7–8 (marked ॥ ६-७ ॥, one off this edition’s numbering), and 2.3.11.

The ladder once more, and the tortoise

Comparative Vedānta

The first adhyāya’s ladder began इन्द्रियेभ्यः परा ह्यर्था अर्थेभ्यश्च परं मनः, with the objects between the senses and the mind, and named the intellect buddhi. Here the objects are left out and the intellect is called sattva. Śaṅkara says the objects are counted with the senses because they are of one kind with them, अर्थानामिहेन्द्रियसमानजातीयत्वादिन्द्रियग्रहणेनैव ग्रहणम्; Raṅgarāmānuja, too, reads the two ladders as saying the same thing.

The Gītā’s image of the steady sage, यदा संहरते चायं कूर्मोऽङ्गानीव सर्वशः — ‘when he draws in his senses from their objects on every side, as a tortoise its limbs’ — stands close to the Kaṭha’s ‘firm holding of the senses’. Neither text cites the other; the likeness is of thought, not of words.

  • Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.3.10: इन्द्रियेभ्यः परा ह्यर्था अर्थेभ्यश्च परं मनः indriyebhyaḥ parā hyarthā arthebhyaśca paraṃ manaḥ
  • Bhagavad Gītā 2.58: यदा संहरते चायं कूर्मोऽङ्गानीव सर्वशः yadā saṃharate cāyaṃ kūrmo'ṅgānīva sarvaśaḥ

‘Yoga’ and ‘arising and passing’

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Yoga, from the root yuj, ‘join, yoke’, is defined here not by what is joined but by what is held: indriya-dhāraṇā, the holding of the senses, firm (sthirā). Śaṅkara plays on the word — yoga that is really viyoga, separation — while Raṅgarāmānuja, with Vyāsārya, simply identifies yoga with the highest course of the previous verse.

Prabhavāpyayau is a dual: ‘arising and passing away’, and the clause ‘for yoga is arising and passing’ is elliptical. Śaṅkara and Raṅgarāmānuja take it of yoga itself — it can come and go, so heedfulness is needed; Raṅgarāmānuja adds a reading in which yoga brings about what is wished for and the end of what is not. Madhva takes it of the Lord, from whom arising and passing come.

The aorist aśakat in 2.3.4 is, for Raṅgarāmānuja, a Vedic exchange of the present-stem suffix, विकरणव्यत्ययः छान्दसः. In 2.3.10 the text printed with him reads बुद्धिश्च न विचेष्टेत, an optative, where the base and the other witnesses have विचेष्टति; it is not a collated witness, and the reading is recorded here only as his.

Holding still

Contemporary Reflection

A modern reflection. The definition is sober: yoga here is not a feat but a holding — the senses kept still, the mind with them, the intellect not stirring — and it is said at once that this can be lost, so one must stay heedful. The text does not promise an experience; it names a discipline and warns that it does not keep itself.

The commentators set it in different frames: the self seen at last without its coverings, the self known and then the Lord sought in refuge, the Lord seen by the eye of knowledge. The edition makes no claim about what such practice does; it reports what the verses and the commentaries say. This paragraph is a reflection, not a reading of the text.

Open questions

  • Whether the mirror of 2.3.5 is the clearest seeing, available here (Śaṅkara, and Madhva of the Lord in the jīva), or an image of unclear seeing that shows how hard the self is to know (Raṅgarāmānuja).
  • Whether ‘yoga is arising and passing away’ says that yoga can be lost (Śaṅkara, Raṅgarāmānuja) or that arising and passing come from the Lord (Madhva).
  • The text printed with Raṅgarāmānuja reads बुद्धिश्च न विचेष्टेत in 2.3.10; its basis needs a printed edition.
  • Madhva’s bhāṣya, in the digital text used here, has no comment of its own on 2.3.4, 2.3.6 or 2.3.10.
