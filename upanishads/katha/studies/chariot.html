Study VIII · Kaṭha Upaniṣad

The chariot

Mantras 1.3.3–1.3.9: the self as the rider, the body as the chariot, the senses as horses, the one who enjoys, the trained and the untrained horses, and the end of the road

Anuvākas 1.3.3, 1.3.4, 1.3.5, 1.3.6, 1.3.7, 1.3.8, 1.3.9

Death gives the boy an image to think with. The self is the rider, the body the chariot, the intellect the charioteer, the mind the reins, the senses the horses, and the objects of sense their roads; the self joined to body, senses and mind is ‘the one who enjoys’. With an unskilled charioteer and slack reins the horses run wild and the rider does not reach the goal; with a skilled one they are like good horses, and he reaches ‘the end of the road, that highest place of Viṣṇu’. Śaṅkara and Raṅgarāmānuja agree on the image but not on whose the enjoying is; Madhva’s bhāṣya has no comment of its own on these mantras.

The image, part by part

Direct Textual Meaning

आत्मानं रथिनं विद्धि शरीरं रथमेव तु — ‘know the self as the rider, and the body as the chariot’; बुद्धिं तु सारथिं विद्धि मनः प्रग्रहमेव च — ‘know the intellect as the charioteer, and the mind as the reins’. इन्द्रियाणि हयानाहुर्विषयांस्तेषु गोचरान् — ‘the senses, they say, are the horses, and the objects of sense the ground they range over’.

Then a definition: आत्मेन्द्रियमनोयुक्तं भोक्तेत्याहुर्मनीषिणः — ‘the self joined with the senses and the mind the wise call the enjoyer’. Six parts are named — rider, chariot, charioteer, reins, horses, roads — and one more term, the enjoyer, for the whole when it is yoked together.

Why a chariot

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara says the image makes the teaching easier to grasp — तथा च प्रतिपत्तिसौकर्यम् — and the rider is the self as it wanders, shaped by its adjuncts: तत्र यः उपाधिकृतः संसारी. Each part is chosen for a reason. The body is the chariot because the senses, like horses yoked to it, pull it along: रथबद्धहयस्थानीयैरिन्द्रियैराकृष्यमाणत्वाच्छरीरस्य. The intellect is the charioteer because nearly everything the body does is the intellect’s work: सर्वं हि देहगतं कार्यं बुद्धिकर्तव्यमेव प्रायेण. The mind is the reins because the senses act when the mind takes hold of them, as horses move by the reins: मनसा हि प्रगृहीतानि श्रोत्रादीनि करणानि प्रवर्तन्ते रशनयेवाश्वाः.

Raṅgarāmānuja reads the whole passage up to 1.3.9 as teaching the equipment for reaching the highest place of Viṣṇu, which lies at the far end of the road of saṃsāra: संसाराध्वपारभूतवैष्णवपरमपदप्राप्तौ परिकरम् उपदिशन्. The rider is the one who presides over the body — शरीराधिष्ठातारं रथिनं विद्धि — and the intellect is the charioteer because the body moves by the intellect’s decisions.

Who is the enjoyer?

Comparative Vedānta

Both commentators deny that the self on its own is the enjoyer, and mean different things by it. Śaṅkara: न हि केवलस्यात्मनो भोक्तृत्वमस्ति ; बुद्ध्याद्युपाधिकृतमेव तस्य भोक्तृत्वम् — enjoying belongs to the self only through its adjuncts, the intellect and the rest. He cites another Upaniṣad that shows the self alone does not enjoy — it ‘seems to think, seems to move’, ध्यायतीव लेलायतीव — and says that only so can the highest place of Viṣṇu, still to be described, be understood as one’s own self: वैष्णवस्य पदस्यात्मतया प्रतिपत्तिरुपपद्यते.

Raṅgarāmānuja takes ātman in this line to mean the body — आत्मशब्दो देहपरः — and the enjoyer to be one who both acts and enjoys: कर्तृत्वभोक्तृत्ववान् इत्यर्थः. Without body, senses, mind and intellect the rider would be idle and could do nothing, worldly or ritual: रथित्वेन रूपितस्य उदासीनस्य आत्मनो गमनरूपलौकिकवैदिकक्रियाकर्तृत्वमेव नास्ति. So, he concludes, न हि केवलस्यात्मनः कर्तृत्वं भोक्तृत्वं वा अस्तीति भावः.

The sentences sound alike. For Śaṅkara the enjoying is borrowed from the adjuncts and is not the self’s own; for Raṅgarāmānuja the self really acts and enjoys, but only with its instruments.

Wild horses and good horses

Traditional Interpretation

Four mantras set two charioteers side by side. For one who lacks understanding, with the mind never held, the senses are out of control, दुष्टाश्वा इव सारथेः — ‘like the bad horses of a charioteer’; for one who understands, with the mind always held, they obey, सदश्वा इव सारथेः — ‘like good horses’. The first न स तत्पदमाप्नोति संसारं चाधिगच्छति — ‘does not reach that place, and goes on into saṃsāra’; the second reaches it, यस्माद्भूयो न जायते, ‘from which he is not born again’.

Śaṅkara makes the charioteer the intellect: an unskilled, undiscriminating one — अनिपुणोऽविवेकी — cannot hold the horses back, अशक्यनिवारणानि; a skilled one can start them or stop them at will, प्रवर्तयितुं निवर्तयितुं वा शक्यानि. The one who fails does not merely miss the goal; he goes on into birth and death: संसारं च जन्ममरणलक्षणम् अधिगच्छति.

Raṅgarāmānuja comments on the mantras in pairs. In the world, horses obey a good charioteer with good reins; so when understanding and mind are sound, the senses pictured as horses obey: अश्वत्वेन रूपितानि इन्द्रियाणि वश्यानि भवन्ति. The one without a held mind is unclean because his thinking always runs the wrong way — सर्वदा विपरीतचिन्ताप्रवणत्वात् — and the failure does more than leave the goal unreached: it leads him into the dense forest of saṃsāra, गहनं संसारकान्तारमेव प्रापयति.

The end of the road

Comparative Vedānta

सोऽध्वनः पारमाप्नोति तद्विष्णोः परमं पदम् — ‘he reaches the end of the road, that highest place of Viṣṇu’. Śaṅkara glosses the end of the road as the end of the journey of saṃsāra — संसारगतेः पारं — and Viṣṇu as the all-pervading Brahman, the supreme self called Vāsudeva: व्यापनशीलस्य ब्रह्मणः परमात्मनो वासुदेवाख्यस्य. The ‘place’ is its true nature: सतत्त्वमित्येतत्.

If it is a place one goes to, why does one not come back from it? Śaṅkara answers a little later: since it is everyone’s inner self, knowing it is only spoken of as going — सर्वस्य प्रत्यगात्मत्वादवगतिरेव गतिरित्युपचर्यते. Raṅgarāmānuja: the one with sound understanding and mind reaches the nature of the supreme self, which lies at the end of the road: संसाराध्वपारभूतं परमात्मस्वरूपं प्राप्नोति इत्यर्थः.

Madhva’s bhāṣya has no comment of its own on the chariot mantras; his next comment, on the series that follows, names the person at its top as Viṣṇu: तस्यास्तु पुरुषो विष्णुः. The next study takes up that series.

Reins, not a ban on horses

Contemporary Reflection

A modern reflection. In this image the horses are not the enemy. A chariot without horses goes nowhere, and the good charioteer is not the one who has got rid of them but the one whose reins hold. The verses praise skill and steadiness, not the absence of the senses.

The commentators draw more from the image than this — a doctrine of the self, of the enjoyer, of the goal — and differ on each. This is a reflection, not a claim of any commentator, and not advice about any practice.

Open questions

  • Whether ātman in ‘ātmendriyamanoyukta’ (1.3.4) is the self (Śaṅkara) or the body (Raṅgarāmānuja), and whether the enjoying is the self’s own or borrowed from its adjuncts.
  • How a ‘place’ that is reached can be one from which there is no return: Śaṅkara’s answer is that the going is figurative; the other readings take the reaching more literally.
  • Madhva’s bhāṣya, in the digital text used here, has no comment on 1.3.3–1.3.9; his comment that follows concerns 1.3.10–1.3.12.
