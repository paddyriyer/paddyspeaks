Study VI · Kaṭha Upaniṣad

The unborn self and the one he chooses

Mantras 1.2.18–1.2.25: the self that is not born and does not die, the Gītā’s version, smaller than the small, the calm of the faculties or the grace of the Creator, and ‘not by instruction… whom he chooses’

Anuvākas 1.2.18, 1.2.19, 1.2.20, 1.2.21, 1.2.22, 1.2.23, 1.2.24, 1.2.25

Death now states what the self is: not born, not dying, not slain when the body is slain — two mantras the Bhagavad Gītā repeats in part. It is smaller than the small and greater than the great, set in the cave of the creature, seen by one free of desire through a ‘clarity’ the readings understand differently. It cannot be gained by instruction, intellect or much learning, only by the one whom it — or he — chooses, and not by one who has not turned from wrongdoing. The schools divide on who is described (the self that is Brahman, the individual self, or the Lord), on dhātuprasāda, and on who does the choosing.

Not born, does not die

Comparative Vedānta

न जायते म्रियते वा विपश्चिन्नायं कुतश्चिन्न बभूव कश्चित् — ‘the knower is not born and does not die; it has not come from anywhere, nor has it become anyone’ — अजो नित्यः शाश्वतोऽयं पुराणो न हन्यते हन्यमाने शरीरे, ‘unborn, constant, everlasting, ancient, it is not slain when the body is slain’. And: if the slayer thinks he slays and the slain thinks he is slain, उभौ तौ न विजानीतो नायं हन्ति न हन्यते — ‘neither of them understands: this one does not slay and is not slain’.

The Bhagavad Gītā has both verses in its second chapter. Its 2.20 shares the second half of 1.2.18 word for word, but its first half reads न जायते म्रियते वा कदाचिन्नायं भूत्वाभविता वा न भूयः — ‘it is never born and never dies; having been, it will not again cease to be’ — where the Kaṭha has vipaścit, ‘the knower’, and ‘not from anywhere’. Its 2.19 shares the second half of 1.2.19, but its first half reads य एनं वेत्ति हन्तारं यश्चैनं मन्यते हतम् — ‘he who thinks it a slayer, and he who thinks it slain’.

Shared wording shows that the two texts are related; it does not show which drew on which, and this edition does not decide it.

  • Bhagavad Gītā 2.20: अजो नित्यः शाश्वतोऽयं पुराणो न हन्यते हन्यमाने शरीरे ajo nityaḥ śāśvato'yaṃ purāṇo na hanyate hanyamāne śarīre
  • Bhagavad Gītā 2.19: उभौ तौ न विजानीतो नायं हन्ति न हन्यते ubhau tau na vijānīto nāyaṃ hanti na hanyate

Which self is described?

Comparative Vedānta

Śaṅkara: having given Om as the support, Death now states the nature of the self it points to — तस्योङ्कारालम्बनस्यात्मनः साक्षात्स्वरूपनिर्दिधारयिषयेदमुच्यते. The body may be struck, but the self in it is untouched, like space — तत्स्थोऽप्याकाशवदेव — and so the round of merit and demerit belongs only to one who does not know the self: अतोऽनात्मज्ञविषय एव धर्माधर्मादिलक्षणः संसारः नात्मज्ञस्य.

Raṅgarāmānuja: these two mantras describe the individual self first — प्रथमं तावत् प्रत्यगात्मस्वरूपमाह — and he reports the view of an earlier teacher that both are about the soul: मन्त्रद्वयमपि जीवविषयकमेव. Vipaścit, for him, means ‘fit to be a knower’: विपश्चित्वार्होऽयम्. The Supreme Self comes in the next mantra.

Madhva reads the first line through the Lord: because the Lord did not come into being from anything, the one who knows him is not born and does not die — अयं भगवान् कुतोऽपि न बभूव यस्मादतस्तद्वेत्तापि विपश्चिन्न जायते न म्रियते च. The soul itself was never born either; it is born through connection with a body — देहसम्बन्धाद्धि जायते. On 1.2.19 he adds that even the soul has no death of its own: जीवस्यापि स्वतो मरणाभावादुभौ तौ न विजानीतः.

Smaller than the small, greater than the great

Comparative Vedānta

अणोरणीयान्महतो महीयानात्मास्य जन्तोर्निहितो गुहायाम् — ‘smaller than the small, greater than the great, the self is set in the cave of this creature’. The Chāndogya (3.14.3) makes the same double claim of ‘my self within the heart’: smaller than a grain of rice or barley — एष म आत्मान्तर्हृदयेऽणीयान्व्रीहेर्वा यवाद्वा — and greater than the earth, the sky and all the worlds.

Śaṅkara: whatever is small or great in the world is so only through the self; without it, it would not be — अणु महद्वा यदस्ति लोके वस्तु, तत्तेनैवात्मना नित्येनात्मवत्सम्भवति. The ‘creature’ is every living being, from Brahmā to a blade of grass.

Raṅgarāmānuja sees a change of subject here: having described the individual self in two mantras, Death now describes the Supreme Self that is its self — तदात्मभूतपरमात्मस्वरूपमाह — ‘the self of this creature’ meaning the one who enters it and governs it from within: अन्तःप्रविश्य नियन्ता इत्यर्थः. Madhva cites a verse that explains the Lord’s ‘greatness’ as his exceeding the soul in qualities and measure: जीवाद् गुणपरीमाणं यस्माद्विष्णोर्महत्तरम्.

  • Chāndogya Upaniṣad 3.14.3: एष म आत्मान्तर्हृदयेऽणीयान्व्रीहेर्वा यवाद्वा eṣa ma ātmāntarhṛdaye'ṇīyānvrīhervā yavādvā

By the clarity of the faculties, or by the grace of the Creator?

Comparative Vedānta

The second line is the most disputed of the vallī. The base reads तमक्रतुः पश्यति वीतशोको धातुप्रसादान्महिमानमात्मनः — ‘the one without desire sees him, free from sorrow, through dhātuprasāda, the greatness of the self’. Madhva’s text divides the compound: धातुः प्रसादान्महिमानमात्मनः, ‘through the grace of the Creator (dhātṛ)’.

Śaṅkara keeps the compound and takes dhātu in its sense ‘supporter’: the mind and the other faculties are called dhātus because they support the body, and when they are clear the self’s greatness is seen — मनआदीनि करणानि धातवः शरीरस्य धारणात्प्रसीदन्तीत्येषां धातूनां प्रसादादात्मनो महिमानं. The one ‘without desire’ is अकामः.

Raṅgarāmānuja takes the same word as the supporter of all, the Supreme Self — धारकस्य परमात्मनः — and its prasāda as grace; he also allows the construction ‘free from sorrow through dhātuprasāda’. On the next mantra he names it outright: one without the Lord’s favour, called dhātuprasāda, cannot reach this truth — धातुप्रसादशब्दितभगवदनुग्रहशून्यस्य परमात्मतत्त्वम्. He knows too a reading with akratum and īśam (‘sees the Lord… by the grace of the Creator’), in which akratu describes the Lord, untouched by the rise and fall that action brings, and dhātuḥ means the Lord’s.

Madhva’s comment does not gloss dhātuḥ; it explains akratu unusually, as ‘one whose resolve (kratu) is on a, that is Viṣṇu’ — ए विष्णौ क्रतुर्यस्य सोऽक्रतुस्तन्निश्चयः — and ‘the greatness of the self’ as the one greater than the soul: आत्मनः सकाशान्महिमानं महामानम्. The two readings, one letter apart, carry the difference between a self seen in a calm mind and a Lord who shows himself by grace.

Sitting, it travels far

Comparative Vedānta

आसीनो दूरं व्रजति शयानो याति सर्वतः — ‘sitting, it travels far; lying down, it goes everywhere’ — कस्तं मदामदं देवं मदन्यो ज्ञातुमर्हति, ‘who but I can know that god who rejoices and does not rejoice?’ Then: the bodiless among bodies, the stable among the unstable — अशरीरं शरीरेषु अनवस्थेष्ववस्थितम् — great and all-pervading; knowing it, the wise one does not grieve.

Śaṅkara: the self appears to have contradictory qualities through its adjuncts — विरुद्धधर्मवान् — so that, like a wishing-jewel, it appears to some as if it had every form: विश्वरूप इव चिन्तामणिवत्कस्यचिदवभासते. Bodiless, it is like space: स्वेन रूपेणाकाशकल्प आत्मा; for one who knows it so, grief has no ground — न ह्येवंविधस्वात्मविदः शोकोपपत्तिः.

Madhva cites a verse: the Lord reconciles contradictions by his lordship — ऐश्वर्याद् भगवान् विष्णुर्विरुद्धं घटयत्यसौ. Raṅgarāmānuja: the qualities that look contradictory belong to the Supreme through the souls he is the self of, and ‘who but I’ means: who but one like me, favoured by the Supreme — प्रसादानुगृहीतमादृशजनादन्यः को वा ज्ञातेत्यर्थः.

‘Whom he chooses’

Comparative Vedānta

नायमात्मा प्रवचनेन लभ्यो न मेधया न बहुना श्रुतेन — ‘this self is not gained by instruction, nor by intellect, nor by much learning’ — यमेवैष वृणुते तेन लभ्यस्तस्यैष आत्मा विवृणुते तनूंस्वाम्, ‘by the one whom he chooses it is gained; to him this self reveals its own form’. The Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad (3.2.3) has the same mantra.

The grammar leaves open who ‘he’ is. Śaṅkara makes the seeker the chooser and the self the chosen: the seeker longs for his own self, and by that self alone the self is gained — यमेव स्वमात्मानम् एष साधको वृणुते प्रार्थयते, तेनैवात्मना वरित्रा स्वयमात्मा लभ्यः — that is, आत्मनैवात्मा लभ्यत इत्यर्थः. Instruction (pravacana) he takes as taking up many Vedas: अनेकवेदस्वीकरणेन.

Raṅgarāmānuja makes the Supreme Self the chooser: ‘he’ is the Supreme, who chooses a seeker — साधकं प्रार्थयते — and the one he chooses is the one dearest to him. The worshipper’s love of the Lord awakens the Lord’s love for the worshipper, and that is the cause of attaining him: भगवतः उपासके प्रीतिमुत्पाद्य तत्प्राप्तिहेतुः भवतीत्यर्थः. Pravacana, he says, means reflection: प्रवचनं मननम्; and the self ‘reveals its form’ means that the Supreme gives himself: स्वात्मानं प्रयच्छति इत्यर्थः.

Madhva’s bhāṣya, in the text this edition uses, gives the mūla of 1.2.22–1.2.25 without a comment on them; this edition does not supply a Dvaita reading of the mantra that he did not write.

  • Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad 3.2.3: नायमात्मा प्रवचनेन लभ्यो न मेधया न बहुना श्रुतेन nāyamātmā pravacanena labhyo na medhayā na bahunā śrutena

Who cannot attain it

Traditional Interpretation

Whatever the choosing means, the next mantra sets conditions: नाविरतो दुश्चरितान्नाशान्तो नासमाहितः — not one who has not ceased from wrongdoing, not one who is not calm, not one who is not collected, nor one whose mind is not at peace — can reach it by knowledge: प्रज्ञानेनैनमाप्नुयात्. Śaṅkara’s positive form: one who has turned from wrongdoing and from the restlessness of the senses, whose mind is collected and at peace even about the fruit of that, and who has a teacher — समाधानफलादप्युपशान्तमानसश्चाचार्यवान्. Raṅgarāmānuja calls these the ancillaries of the meditation that leads to the Supreme: परमात्मप्राप्तिहेतुभूतोपासनाङ्गतया कांश्चिद्धर्मान् उपदिशति.

The vallī closes with an image: यस्य ब्रह्म च क्षत्रं च उभे भवत ओदनः — ‘for whom priesthood and kingship are both food’ — मृत्युर्यस्योपसेचनं क इत्था वेद यत्र सः, ‘for whom Death is the sauce: who knows thus where he is?’ Śaṅkara: even Death, who takes everything, is only the sauce on his rice — सर्वहरोऽपि मृत्युः यस्य उपसेचनमिवौदनस्य. Raṅgarāmānuja takes the two classes as standing for all that moves and does not move, and the eater as the Supreme, the withdrawer of all — निखिलचराचरसंहर्ता परमात्मा — citing the Brahmasūtra अत्ता चराचरग्रहणात्.

Chosen and choosing

Contemporary Reflection

A modern reflection. The vallī ends with a sentence that can be read in two directions. On one reading, what is sought is gained only by the one who truly seeks it, for itself; on the other, it is given to the one it chooses. Many people recognise both experiences in learning anything deep: it comes when you want it for its own sake, and it also seems to come rather than be taken.

The next mantra keeps the reading honest: no account of choosing excuses the absence of the ordinary disciplines — ceasing from harm, calm, attention. The commentators disagree on the first sentence and agree on the second. This is a reflection, not the commentators’ teaching.

Open questions

  • Who chooses in 1.2.23: the seeker, choosing the self (Śaṅkara), or the Supreme, choosing the seeker (Raṅgarāmānuja). The pronoun eṣa allows both; Madhva’s bhāṣya here gives no comment.
  • Whether the line of 1.2.20 read dhātuprasādāt, ‘through the clarity of the faculties’ (the base, GRETIL and Śaṅkara), or dhātuḥ prasādāt, ‘through the grace of the Creator’ (Madhva’s text) — and whether Raṅgarāmānuja’s ‘grace of the supporter’ reads the compound or the divided form.
  • Whether 1.2.18–1.2.19 describe the self that is Brahman (Śaṅkara), the individual self (Raṅgarāmānuja), or the knower of the unborn Lord (Madhva), and whether 1.2.20 changes the subject.
  • The relation between these mantras and Bhagavad Gītā 2.19–2.20: the shared half-verses do not show which text is earlier.
