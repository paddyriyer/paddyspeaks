Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 2 · Vallī 3 · Mantra 10 of 18

When the five knowings stand still

When the five knowings stand still together with the mind, and the intellect does not stir, that, they say, is the highest course.

5 Faithful English translation

When the five knowings stand still together with the mind, and the intellect does not stir — that they call the highest course.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

When the five senses are at rest together with the mind, and the intellect itself does not stir, that, they say, is the highest state.

Key concepts इन्द्रिय indriya, a sense मनस् mind बुद्धि buddhi, the intellect अध्यात्मयोग inward yoga

7 Explain it to a beginner

The last vallī now turns from what the self is to how it is reached. This verse describes a state of deep stillness. Normally our five senses are busy taking in the world, the mind is busy arranging what they bring, and the intellect is busy deciding what to do about it. Here all of that comes to rest: the senses stop reaching outward, the mind goes quiet with them, and even the deciding part of us stops moving. The text calls this ‘the highest course’. Śaṅkara says this is the yoga by which the self is reached, and explains that the senses are called ‘knowings’ because they exist for knowing. Raṅgarāmānuja says the intellect here is simply the mind in its deciding mode, and that the highest course is the journey towards release rather than the restless round within the body. The next verse gives this stillness its name.

An analogy When the wind drops and a pond stops rippling, the water does not change, but now you can see to the bottom.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse say?

When the five senses rest together with the mind and the intellect does not stir, that is called the highest course.

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara: the yoga that reaches the self

The verse answers how the self of 2.3.9 is reached: by yoga, तदर्थो योग उच्यते. The senses are turned back from their objects and rest in the self, स्वविषयेभ्यो निवर्तितानि आत्मन्येव, together with the mind they follow; the intellect, whose mark is deciding, does not busy itself with its own work, बुद्धिश्च अध्यवसायलक्षणा न विचेष्टति स्वव्यापारेषु न विचेष्टते न व्याप्रियते.

Traditional Interpretation

Raṅgarāmānuja: the senses, the mind and the course to release

The ‘knowings’ are the senses, ज्ञायते अनेन इति व्युत्पत्त्या, ज्ञानानि इन्द्रियाणि इत्यर्थः, as Vyāsārya explains under the sūtra ‘sapta gateḥ’ (as cited by him). The intellect is the mind with its deciding, and he quotes the Bhāṣya: अध्यवसायाभिमानचिन्तावृत्तिभेदात् मन एव बुद्ध्यहंकारचित्तशब्दैः व्यपदिश्यते. The highest course is the going for release in place of moving about within the body, शरीरान्तस्सञ्चरणं विहाय मोक्षार्थगमनं परमा गतिः.

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva

Madhva’s bhāṣya has no comment on this mantra: the line filed under 2.3.9–2.3.11 in the file used here glosses yogo hi prabhavāpyayau of 2.3.11.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The witnesses

The base reads the active viceṣṭati; the vishvAsa mūla has the middle viceṣṭate, and Madhva’s text tam āhuḥ for tām āhuḥ (corrections.json). Raṅgarāmānuja, quoting the line in his comment on 2.3.12, has the optative, बुद्धिश्च न विचेष्टेत.

The highest course is the course towards release, leaving behind movement within the body: शरीरान्तस्सञ्चरणं विहाय मोक्षार्थगमनं परमा गतिः.

Dvaita

No position is given for this school here: the edition does not force one.

Where they disagree

Both read the verse as yoga, the senses and the mind brought to rest. Śaṅkara makes the intellect a separate deciding faculty; Raṅgarāmānuja, with the Bhāṣya he quotes, makes it a function of the mind, and reads the ‘highest course’ as the way to release. Madhva’s bhāṣya does not comment on this mantra.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • पञ्चावतिष्ठन्ते pañcāvatiṣṭhante → पञ्च + अवतिष्ठन्ते

    a + a

    Two like vowels merge into the long vowel, P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः).

  • बुद्धिश्च buddhiśca → बुद्धिः + च

    visarga before c

    Visarga before c becomes s, P. 8.3.34 (विसर्जनीयस्य सः), and s before c becomes ś, P. 8.4.40 (स्तोः श्चुना श्चुः).

  • तामाहुः tāmāhuḥ → ताम् + आहुः

    m before a vowel

    Final m stays before a vowel and is joined to it.

  • परमां गतिम् paramāṃ gatim → परमाम् + गतिम्

    m before a consonant

    Final m → anusvāra before a consonant, P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः).

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this mantra.

Morphology

अवतिष्ठन्ते
ava + √sthā in the middle voice, P. 1.3.22 (समवप्रविभ्यः स्थः): with ava the root takes the middle endings. Stated as a recurring state: whenever the senses rest, then.
विचेष्टति
vi + √ceṣṭ, here with active endings; the root is usually middle (ceṣṭate). The vishvAsa mūla reads viceṣṭate (corrections.json), and Śaṅkara glosses the active with the middle, न विचेष्टते न व्याप्रियते. Raṅgarāmānuja, quoting the verse in his comment on 2.3.12, has the optative बुद्धिश्च न विचेष्टेत.
आहुः
A perfect of √ah used with present sense, ‘they say’; with two accusatives, ‘they call that the highest course’.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

A temporal clause in two parts, yadā … avatiṣṭhante and buddhiś ca na viceṣṭati, taken up by the pronoun tām in the main clause tām āhuḥ paramāṃ gatim, where āhuḥ takes two accusatives (call that the highest course). tām is feminine by attraction to gatim.

Ambiguities

What are the ‘five knowings’?
Both commentators take them as the five senses. Śaṅkara: they are called knowings because they serve knowing, ज्ञानार्थत्वाच्छ्रोत्रादीनीन्द्रियाणि ज्ञानान्युच्यन्ते. Raṅgarāmānuja derives the word, ‘that by which one knows’, ज्ञायते अनेन इति व्युत्पत्त्या, ज्ञानानि इन्द्रियाणि इत्यर्थः.
Is the intellect a faculty apart from the mind?
Śaṅkara takes buddhi as the deciding faculty, अध्यवसायलक्षणा. Raṅgarāmānuja, with the Bhāṣya he quotes, takes it as the mind itself in its function of deciding: अध्यवसायाभिमानचिन्तावृत्तिभेदात् मन एव बुद्ध्यहंकारचित्तशब्दैः व्यपदिश्यते (quoted by him).
The ‘highest course’
Śaṅkara introduces the verse as the yoga by which the self, grasped by heart and mind (2.3.9), is reached, सा हृन्मनीट् कथं प्राप्यत इति तदर्थो योग उच्यते. Raṅgarāmānuja takes the highest course as going towards release instead of wandering within the body, शरीरान्तस्सञ्चरणं विहाय मोक्षार्थगमनं परमा गतिः, which he says is made clear in the passage he cites.

Metre

anuṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 16 + 16

Regular: 16 and 16 syllables.

Textual variations between witnesses

  • Variant reading The vishvAsa mūla: न विचेष्टते तामाहुः परमां — The vishvAsa mūla reads viceṣṭate, middle, for viceṣṭati, active (‘[the intellect] does not stir’); Śaṅkara comments on viceṣṭati and paraphrases it with viceṣṭate, so the sense is the same.
  • Variant reading Madhva’s mūla: न विचेष्टति तमाहुः — Madhva’s text reads tam āhuḥ, masculine, for tām āhuḥ, feminine, agreeing with gatim.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Attention

Much of ordinary life is spent with the senses pulled outward and the mind busy. The verse describes the opposite pole, not as something to be forced but as a state that comes when all of it settles. This is a reflection, not a claim made by the text.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin When were your senses, your thoughts and your decisions last all quiet at the same time?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Is the intellect a faculty beside the mind, or the mind in one of its modes? Does the verse need either answer?

A contemplation exercise For a few minutes, notice the senses reaching out, and let each one come back.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
