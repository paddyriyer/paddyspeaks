Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 1 · Vallī 2 · Mantra 24 of 25

Not one who has not turned from wrong

Not one who has not ceased from bad conduct, nor one who is not calm, not collected, nor one whose mind is not at peace, can reach him by knowledge.

5 Faithful English translation

‘Not one who has not ceased from bad conduct, not one not calm, not one not collected, nor even one whose mind is not at peace, would attain him by knowledge.’

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

‘No one who has not turned away from wrongdoing, who is restless, who is scattered, or whose mind is not at peace, can reach the self through knowledge.’

Key concepts धर्म dharma ज्ञान knowledge शान्ति peace मनस् mind

7 Explain it to a beginner

After saying that cleverness and learning are not enough, the Upaniṣad says what is needed. A person who has not stopped doing wrong cannot reach the self, nor can someone whose senses are restless, whose attention is scattered, or whose mind is not at peace. Knowledge alone, without this, does not get there. Śaṅkara explains each condition: give up forbidden actions, quiet the craving of the senses, gather the mind to one point, and stop worrying even about the results of that concentration. He adds that the person who does all this, with a teacher, reaches the self. Raṅgarāmānuja treats these virtues as part of the practice of worship: someone who ignores ordinary morality cannot make that practice whole.

An analogy Like trying to see the bottom of a pond: no instrument helps while the water is still being stirred.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse say?

That no one who has not ceased from wrong conduct, who is not calm, not collected, or whose mind is not at peace, can reach the self by knowledge.

Traditional Interpretation

What does Śaṅkara add?

A positive restatement: the one who has turned from wrong conduct and from the restlessness of the senses, whose mind is collected and at peace even about the fruit of concentration, and who has a teacher, reaches the self, समाहितचित्तः समाधानफलादप्युपशान्तमानसश्चाचार्यवान्. Prajñāna is knowledge of Brahman, ब्रह्मविज्ञानेन.

Comparative Vedānta

Why are moral rules made part of meditation?

Raṅgarāmānuja: the verse teaches certain virtues as auxiliaries of the worship that leads to the Supreme, परमात्मप्राप्तिहेतुभूतोपासनाङ्गतया कांश्चिद्धर्मान् उपदिशति. Just as the general rule against lying is also enjoined within a particular rite, abstaining from wrong conduct is enjoined as part of worship; whoever ignores it, its defect leaves the worship incomplete.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

And Madhva?

Madhva gives the verse without comment in our source; his text reads aśāntamanaso for aśāntamānaso and prajñānenaivam for prajñānenainam (corrections.json).

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 1.2.24

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Knowledge of Brahman reaches the self only in one prepared by restraint, concentration and a teacher: समाहितचित्तः समाधानफलादप्युपशान्तमानसश्चाचार्यवान्.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, Kaṭhopaniṣat-prakāśikā 1.2.24

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Moral restraint is an auxiliary of the worship that attains the Supreme: परमात्मप्राप्तिहेतुभूतोपासनाङ्गतया कांश्चिद्धर्मान् उपदिशति.

Dvaita

No position is given for this school here: the edition does not force one.

Where they disagree

Both make ethical and mental preparation a condition of knowing; Śaṅkara treats it as preparing the mind for knowledge of Brahman, Raṅgarāmānuja as a component of the worship that wins the Lord. Madhva does not comment here in our source.

Morphology

आप्नुयात्
Optative 3 sg. of āp ‘reach, obtain’: ‘would attain’, a statement of possibility; the four negated subjects each take it.
एनम्
Enclitic pronoun ‘him’, referring back to the self of the preceding verses.
अशान्तमानसः
mānasa ‘mind’ (a derivative of manas); Madhva’s text has manas itself, aśāntamanasaḥ, with the same sense.

Anvaya

दुश्चरितात् अविरतः न, अशान्तः न, असमाहितः न, अशान्तमानसः वा अपि न प्रज्ञानेन एनम् आप्नुयात् ॥

duścaritāt avirataḥ na, aśāntaḥ na, asamāhitaḥ na, aśāntamānasaḥ vā api na prajñānena enam āpnuyāt ||

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Four negated subjects, each introduced by na (nāvirato …, nāśānto, nāsamāhitaḥ, nāśāntamānaso), share one verb, āpnuyāt, with prajñānena as instrument and enam as object. Śaṅkara and Raṅgarāmānuja both restate it positively: the one who has ceased, is calm, collected and at peace does reach him.

Ambiguities

How do aśānta and aśāntamānasa differ?
Śaṅkara: aśānta is one not restrained from the restlessness of the senses, इन्द्रियलौल्यात् अशान्तः; aśāntamānasa one who, though collected, is still anxious for the fruit of concentration, समाहितचित्तोऽपि सन्समाधानफलार्थित्वात् नापि अशान्तमानसः. Raṅgarāmānuja: one whose surges of desire and anger are not quieted, अनुपशान्तकामक्रोधवेगः, and one whose mind is not restrained.
enam or evam
Madhva’s text reads prajñānenaivam āpnuyāt, ‘would attain [it] so’, for prajñānenainam, ‘would attain him’ (corrections.json, variant). Śaṅkara’s paraphrase of the positive sense also says ‘thus’, प्रज्ञानेन एवं यथोक्तमात्मानं प्राप्नोतीत्यर्थः, after glossing enam in the lemma.

Metre

anuṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 16 + 16

Regular: 16 and 16 syllables.

Textual variations between witnesses

  • Variant reading Madhva’s mūla: नाशान्तमनसो वापि — Madhva’s text reads nāśāntamanaso, ‘one whose mind is not at peace’ (manas), for nāśāntamānaso (mānasa, ‘mind’); the same sense.
  • Variant reading Madhva’s mūla: प्रज्ञानेनैवमाप्नुयात् — Madhva’s text reads prajñānenaivam āpnuyāt, ‘would attain [it] so’, for prajñānenainam āpnuyāt, ‘would attain him’.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Ethics and insight

It is tempting to treat understanding as a matter of information. The verse says that how one lives — whether one has stopped harming, whether the mind can settle — decides what one is able to see. (A modern reflection.)

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Have you noticed that you think less clearly when you are doing something you know is wrong?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Why might Śaṅkara add that the seeker must be calm even about the fruit of concentration?

A contemplation exercise For one day, notice each time the mind is pulled by a sense craving, and simply let it pass.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
