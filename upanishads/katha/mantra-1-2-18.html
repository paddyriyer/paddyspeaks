Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 1 · Vallī 2 · Mantra 18 of 25

Not born, and never dying

Yama begins to describe the self: the knower is not born and does not die; it came from nothing and nothing came from it; unborn, eternal, everlasting, ancient, it is not slain when the body is slain.

5 Faithful English translation

‘The knower is not born or dies; this one came from nowhere; [nor] did anyone come to be [from it]. Unborn, eternal, everlasting [is] this ancient one; it is not slain when the body is slain.’

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

‘The knowing self is never born and never dies. It did not come from anything, and nothing came from it. Unborn, eternal, lasting, ancient, it is not killed when the body is killed.’

Key concepts विपश्चित् the wise, the knower आत्मन् self, Self जीव individual self मृत्यु death ब्रह्मन् Brahman

7 Explain it to a beginner

Here Yama finally starts to answer the boy's question about what survives death. The real self, he says, is never born and never dies. It did not come from anything, and nothing is produced out of it. It is unborn, eternal, lasting and ancient, yet always new, as Śaṅkara puts it. When the body is struck and killed, the self is not killed. Śaṅkara compares it to space, which is never wounded by what happens to the things it fills. The great commentators disagree about whose self this is: the self that is one with the absolute, the individual soul, or the soul that knows God. But they all read the verse as a denial that death touches what we most truly are. The Bhagavad Gītā repeats almost the same words.

An analogy Like the space inside a jar: smash the jar and the space is not broken — it was never made of clay.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse say?

That the knowing self is neither born nor dies, has no source and produces nothing, is unborn, eternal, lasting and ancient, and is not slain when the body is slain.

Traditional Interpretation

Why name birth and death first?

Śaṅkara: of all the changes a produced thing undergoes, birth and death are the first and the last, so denying them denies every change: तासामाद्यन्ते जन्मविनाशलक्षणे विक्रिये इहात्मनि प्रतिषिध्येते. Purāṇa means it is ‘new even of old’, पुरापि नव एवेति, since it does not grow by parts; and it abides in the body unharmed, like space, तत्स्थोऽप्याकाशवदेव.

Comparative Vedānta

Why does Raṅgarāmānuja take it of the individual self?

Because the next verse speaks of a slayer and a slain, and nobody thinks of the Supreme that way: न हि परमात्मनि वध्यधातुकभावभ्रान्तिः कस्याप्यस्ति. Each adjective then gives a reason for a denial: unborn for ‘not born’, eternal for ‘does not die’, and so on. The Supreme is introduced only at 1.2.20.

Traditional Interpretation

And Madhva?

The verse first concerns the Lord, who came from nowhere, and then the wise who know him: birth and death are the body’s, not theirs. यतः कश्चिज्जीवः स्वतो न बभूव । देहसम्बन्धाद्धि जायते — no individual self came into being of itself; it is born through connection with a body. He supports it with verses he cites without naming a source.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

How does the line stand in Madhva’s text?

Madhva’s mūla reads hanyamāne ’pi dehe for hanyamāne śarīre (corrections.json). The Gītā’s form of the verse (2.20) has śarīre, as the base does.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 1.2.18

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

This is the self beyond all change, the same as Brahman, which Om pointed to: अथेदानीं तस्योङ्कारालम्बनस्यात्मनः साक्षात्स्वरूपनिर्दिधारयिषयेदमुच्यते. Since it has no birth or decay, it is not harmed by what happens to the body.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, following Vyāsārya, Kaṭhopaniṣat-prakāśikā 1.2.18

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The verse and the next describe the individual self, which is eternal and unborn: मन्त्रद्वयमपि जीवविषयकमेव. The Supreme, whose body it is, is taught next, from 1.2.20.

Dvaita · Madhva, Kāṭhakopaniṣad-bhāṣya 1.2.18

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Because the Lord came from nowhere, his knower is not born and does not die: अयं भगवान् कुतोऽपि न बभूव यस्मादतस्तद्वेत्तापि विपश्चिन्न जायते न म्रियते च. The individual self is born only through a body.

Where they disagree

Śaṅkara reads one self, identical with Brahman; Raṅgarāmānuja reads the individual self, distinct from the Supreme of 1.2.20; Madhva reads the Lord and the soul who knows him. All three deny that death touches the self.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • विपश्चिन्नायं vipaścinnāyaṃ → विपश्चित् + न + अयम्

    consonant and vowel sandhi

  • कुतश्चिन्न kutaścinna → कुतश्चित् + न

    t before a nasal

    A final t before n becomes n, P. 8.4.45 (यरोऽनुनासिकेऽनुनासिको वा).

  • अजो नित्यः ajo nityaḥ → अजः + नित्यः

    visarga sandhi

    aḥ before a voiced consonant becomes o: P. 6.1.114 (हशि च) with P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः).

  • शाश्वतोऽयं śāśvato'yaṃ → शाश्वतः + अयम्

    visarga and vowel sandhi

    aḥ before a becomes o and the a is elided (written with avagraha): P. 6.1.114 (हशि च) by extension and P. 6.1.109 (एङः पदान्तादति).

  • पुराणो न purāṇo na → पुराणः + न

    visarga sandhi

    As above: aḥ → o before n, P. 6.1.114 (हशि च).

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this mantra.

Morphology

जायते
Present middle of jan, a ya-stem (class 4): ‘is born’. म्रियते, from mṛ ‘die’, is formed the same way and shares the negation.
बभूव
Perfect 3 sg. of bhū ‘become, come to be’: ‘came into being’ — or, with कश्चित् as predicate, ‘became anyone’ (see Ambiguities).
हन्यमाने
Present passive participle of han ‘strike, slay’, in the locative with शरीरे: a locative absolute, ‘when the body is being slain’.
विपश्चित्
An old Vedic word for the inspired, the wise; here the self as knower. Śaṅkara explains why: its consciousness is never lost.

Anvaya

विपश्चित् न जायते म्रियते वा । अयम् कुतश्चित् न बभूव । कश्चित् न बभूव । अयम् अजः नित्यः शाश्वतः पुराणः । शरीरे हन्यमाने न हन्यते ॥

vipaścit na jāyate mriyate vā | ayam kutaścit na babhūva | kaścit na babhūva | ayam ajaḥ nityaḥ śāśvataḥ purāṇaḥ | śarīre hanyamāne na hanyate ||

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Five short statements with one subject, vipaścit / ayam. The first two lines deny birth and death and any origin (na jāyate mriyate vā; nāyaṃ kutaścit na babhūva kaścit); four predicate adjectives follow (ajo nityaḥ śāśvato … purāṇaḥ); the verse ends with a locative absolute (hanyamāne śarīre) set against the main verb na hanyate.

Ambiguities

Whose self is this — the self that is Brahman, the individual self, or the knower of the Lord?
Śaṅkara: the self asked about in 1.2.14 and pointed to by Om, now described directly: अथेदानीं तस्योङ्कारालम्बनस्यात्मनः साक्षात्स्वरूपनिर्दिधारयिषयेदमुच्यते. Raṅgarāmānuja, following Vyāsārya: this verse and the next concern the individual self, मन्त्रद्वयमपि जीवविषयकमेव, because no one imagines the Supreme as slayer or slain. Madhva: the Lord came from nowhere, and so his knower, the vipaścit, is not born and does not die: अयं भगवान् कुतोऽपि न बभूव यस्मादतस्तद्वेत्तापि विपश्चिन्न जायते न म्रियते च.
na babhūva kaścit
Śaṅkara reads it as a separate statement: nothing other came from this self, अस्माच्चात्मनो न बभूव कश्चिदर्थान्तरभूतः. Raṅgarāmānuja: it did not earlier become anyone, as a man or another being, पूर्वमपि मनुष्यादिरूपेण जननशून्यः. Madhva: no individual self came to be of itself; it is ‘born’ through connection with a body, देहसम्बन्धाद्धि जायते.
śarīre or dehe
Madhva’s text ends the line hanyamāne ’pi dehe, ‘even when the body is slain’ (corrections.json, variant): another word for body, with api, the same count of syllables.

Metre

triṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 22 + 22

Regular: 22 and 22 syllables, four pādas of eleven.

Textual variations between witnesses

  • Variant reading Madhva’s mūla: पुराणो न हन्यते हन्यमानेऽपि — Madhva’s text ends the line hanyamāne ’pi dehe, ‘even when the body is slain’, for hanyamāne śarīre, ‘when the body is slain’.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Death and meaning

Most people meet death as the end of a story. The verse asks the reader to separate two questions that usually travel together: what happens to the body, and what happens to the one who knows. Whatever one believes, it is a useful distinction to hold at a bedside. (A modern reflection.)

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin When you say ‘I’, do you mean your body, your memories, or the one who is aware of both?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Why does Śaṅkara think that denying birth and death denies every other change?

A contemplation exercise Notice one change in your body today, and ask what in you noticed it without changing.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
