Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 2 · Vallī 2 · Mantra 10 of 15

The one wind in every form

As one wind, entering the world, takes the shape of every form, so the one inner self of all beings takes the shape of every form, and is outside as well.

5 Faithful English translation

As wind, one, having entered the world, became corresponding in form to each and every form, so the one inner self of all beings [is] corresponding in form to each and every form, and [is] outside.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

As the one wind, entering the world, takes the shape of everything it fills, so the one self within all beings takes the shape of each form, and yet remains outside them.

Key concepts अन्तरात्मन् the inner self वायु breath, air प्राण breath, life-force आत्मन् self, Self एकत्व oneness जीव individual self

7 Explain it to a beginner

This verse repeats the one before it almost word for word, with wind in place of fire. The wind is one, yet it fills every space it enters and takes its shape: the inside of a flute, a room, a pair of lungs. So too, Death says, the one self lives inside every being and takes the shape of each body, while also being outside them all. The commentators do not say much more here than ‘another example, the same meaning’. Śaṅkara adds that the wind enters bodies as breath. Repeating a teaching with a second picture is a teacher’s way of helping a hard idea sink in, and the Upaniṣad does it here on purpose. For Madhva, writing just after this verse, the shapes the wind takes are the Lord’s counterparts, and living souls are his reflections, never the Lord himself.

An analogy The same air is the shape of a balloon, a bottle and a breath; it is still one air.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse say?

The image of 2.2.9 again, with wind: as one wind entering the world takes the shape of every form, so does the one inner self of all beings, and it is outside as well.

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara and Raṅgarāmānuja: another example

Śaṅkara: तथान्यो दृष्टान्तः; the wind enters bodies as breath, प्राणात्मना देहेष्वनुप्रविष्टः, and the rest is as before, रूपं रूपं प्रतिरूपो बभूवेत्यादि समानम्. Raṅgarāmānuja: उदाहरणान्तरमाह, and the meaning is as before, पूर्ववत्.

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva: the wind and the reflections

In the source his comment follows the mūla of this mantra (madhva.json files it under 2.2.9): the god Vāyu too is one supporter, there is no other, एवं देवो वायुरपि धारकोऽन्यो न विद्यते. On the shared second line: Janārdana is one and independent, dwelling within all souls, एकस्स्वतन्त्रो नान्योऽस्ति सर्वजीवान्तरस्थितः; the conscious souls in every form are his reflections, रूपं रूपं प्रति ह्यस्य प्रतिबिम्बाश्च चेतनाः, and they are outside him, never his own nature, बाह्याश्च ते ततो नास्य स्वरूपं ते कथञ्चन.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 2.2.10

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

A second illustration of the one self appearing many, the wind entering bodies as breath, प्राणात्मना देहेष्वनुप्रविष्टः; the meaning is that of 2.2.9.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, Kaṭhopaniṣat-prakāśikā 2.2.10

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Another example of the same teaching, उदाहरणान्तरमाह, read as in 2.2.9: the inner ruler present in each thing.

Dvaita · Madhva, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 2.2.10 (filed under 2.2.9)

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Souls are the Lord’s reflections, outside him and never his nature: बाह्याश्च ते ततो नास्य स्वरूपं ते कथञ्चन.

Where they disagree

As in 2.2.9: one self appearing as many (Śaṅkara), one inner ruler in each (Raṅgarāmānuja), one independent Lord whose reflections, the souls, remain other than him (Madhva).

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • वायुर्यथैको vāyuryathaiko → वायुः + यथा + एकः

    visarga as r

    After i or u the visarga becomes r before a voiced sound: the s becomes ru, P. 8.2.66 (ससजुषो रुँः).

  • यथैको yathaiko → यथा + एकः

    ā + e; aḥ before a voiced consonant

    ā + e give ai, P. 6.1.88 (वृद्धिरेचि); -aḥ becomes -o before bh, P. 6.1.114 (हशि च).

  • प्रविष्टो रूपं praviṣṭo rūpaṃ → प्रविष्टः + रूपम्

    aḥ before a voiced consonant

    -aḥ becomes -o before a voiced consonant, P. 6.1.114 (हशि च).

  • प्रतिरूपो बभूव pratirūpo babhūva → प्रतिरूपः + बभूव

    aḥ before a voiced consonant

    -aḥ becomes -o before a voiced consonant, P. 6.1.114 (हशि च).

  • एकस्तथा ekastathā → एकः + तथा

    visarga before t

    Visarga before t becomes s, P. 8.3.34 (विसर्जनीयस्य सः).

  • बहिश्च bahiśca → बहिः + च

    visarga before c

    Visarga before c becomes s, P. 8.3.34 (विसर्जनीयस्य सः), and s before c becomes ś, P. 8.4.40 (स्तोः श्चुना श्चुः).

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • सर्वभूतान्तरात्मा sarvabhūtāntarātmā

    ṣaṣṭhī-tatpuruṣa (with a karmadhāraya in each member) · सर्व + भूत + अन्तर + आत्मन्

    Vigraha सर्वाणि भूतानि सर्वभूतानि, अन्तरः आत्मा अन्तरात्मा, सर्वभूतानाम् अन्तरात्मा

    the inner self of all beings

Morphology

रूपम्
A distributive repetition (vīpsā), P. 8.1.4 (नित्यवीप्सयोः), as in 2.2.9: ‘each and every form’.
वायुः
vāyu, ‘wind’, from √vā, ‘blow’; in the body it is breath (prāṇa), as Śaṅkara’s gloss says.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

The same yathā … tathā comparison as 2.2.9, word for word, with vāyuḥ in place of agniḥ as the subject of the first line.

Ambiguities

Why a second image?
Śaṅkara calls it simply another illustration, तथान्यो दृष्टान्तः, and says the rest is the same as before, रूपं रूपं प्रतिरूपो बभूवेत्यादि समानम्; Raṅgarāmānuja likewise, उदाहरणान्तरमाह, and पूर्ववत्. Neither says what the wind adds to the fire. In Śaṅkara’s gloss the wind enters bodies as breath, so the image moves from things that burn to living bodies.
What are the ‘counterpart forms’?
As in 2.2.9: appearances of the one self (Śaṅkara), the inner ruler in each thing (Raṅgarāmānuja); for Madhva the wind in each thing is the Lord’s counterpart, and conscious souls are his reflections, outside him.

Metre

triṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 22 + 22

Two lines of 22 syllables (11 + 11), as in 2.2.9.

Textual variations between witnesses

The witnesses differ here only in spelling (listed in section 14).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Education and learning

Good teachers give a second example when the first has not landed, and the Upaniṣad does the same here. Repetition with variation is how difficult ideas are learned. This is a reflection, not a claim made by the text.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Why might a teacher repeat a lesson with a new example?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Fire shows itself by burning, wind by moving and filling. What does each picture let you see about the self that the other does not?

A contemplation exercise Notice your breath for a few rounds: the same air, taking the shape of your body as it enters and leaves.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
