Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 1 · Vallī 1 · Mantra 7 of 29

A guest enters like fire

At Yama’s house, after three nights without welcome, the household tells Yama: a brāhmaṇa guest is fire itself; bring water.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

‘A brāhmaṇa who comes as a guest enters a house like fire itself; people calm him with this welcome. Bring water, son of Vivasvat!’

Key concepts अतिथि guest अग्नि Agni, fire मृत्यु death धर्म dharma

7 Explain it to a beginner

The scene jumps. The boy has gone to the house of Death, but Death was away. For three nights he waited without food. When Death returns, his household warns him: a brāhmaṇa guest who enters a house is like fire. If he is not welcomed, he burns the house; welcomed, he is calmed. So bring water to wash his feet, quickly! In ancient India the guest was sacred, almost a god. Leaving a guest unfed was a serious wrong, even for Death himself. The verse turns the situation around: the boy who was given away to Death has become Death’s honoured guest, and Death is now in his debt.

An analogy Like a powerful official who returns home to find an important visitor has been kept waiting in the hall for days, and whose staff whisper urgently: attend to him at once.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the mantra say?

A brāhmaṇa guest enters a house as fire; people appease him with this welcome; bring water, son of Vivasvat.

Traditional Interpretation

Why is the guest called fire?

Śaṅkara: the guest is fire itself, entering as if to burn — वैश्वानरः अग्निरेव साक्षात् प्रविशति अतिथिः सन् ब्राह्मणः गृहान् दहन्निव — and the welcome quiets that burning. Raṅgarāmānuja gives the household’s fear in their own words: तदपचारेण दग्धा मा भूम, let us not be burned by slighting him.

Traditional Interpretation

What does the Dvaita tradition add?

Madhva cites a verse that fills in the story: स जगाम यमं बालो ब्रह्मचारी यमस्य तु, the boy went to Yama, and though honoured by Yama’s wife did not accept her welcome; when Yama came she said आगते तु यमे प्राह यमं सोदकमाहर. A later Dvaita commentator on Madhva, Vāmanapaṇḍita, takes vaiśvānara as Viṣṇu dwelling in all men (not quoted here).

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 1.1.7

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The guest is fire; the welcome is a ‘quieting’ of fire: वैश्वानरः अग्निरेव साक्षात्.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, Prakāśikā 1.1.7

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The elders warn Yama, for fear of being burned by the slight: तदपचारेण दग्धा मा भूम.

Dvaita · Madhva (a verse he cites), Kāṭhakopaniṣad-bhāṣya 1.1.5–7

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Yama’s wife speaks, the boy having refused her welcome: आगते तु यमे प्राह यमं सोदकमाहर. (Vāmanapaṇḍita, commenting on Madhva, reads vaiśvānara as Viṣṇu in all men.)

Where they disagree

The schools differ on who speaks (ministers or wife, elders, the wife) and Vāmanapaṇḍita’s reading of vaiśvānara as Viṣṇu goes beyond the others’ ‘fire’.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

Morphology

वैश्वानरः
Vṛddhi derivative of viśvānara, ‘belonging to all men’: the fire, especially as the sacrificial and digestive fire.
गृहान्
gṛha is often plural in the sense ‘a house, a household’.

Anvaya

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

A general statement (a guest enters as fire), a second about custom (people appease him), and an imperative addressed to Yama. The speakers are not named in the verse.

Ambiguities

Who speaks?
Śaṅkara: Yama’s ministers or wife — अमात्या भार्या वा ऊचुर्बोधयन्तः. Raṅgarāmānuja: the elders at the door. Madhva’s cited verse and his sub-commentators: Yama’s wife, who had offered the welcome that the boy would not accept while Yama was away.
What happened in the three nights?
The verse does not narrate it; Śaṅkara supplies it: the boy went to Yama’s house and stayed three nights while Yama was away — स च यमभवनं गत्वा तिस्रो रात्रीरुवास यमे प्रोषिते. Madhva’s verse adds that he refused the wife’s hospitality.

Metre

anuṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 16 + 16

A regular anuṣṭubh, 16 + 16 syllables as written (with the unjoined hiatus).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Ethical leadership

Even the most powerful owe basic courtesy to whoever comes to their door. The story makes Death himself the host who failed and must make amends. This is a modern reflection, not the text’s claim.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin How do you treat someone who arrives when it is inconvenient?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Why might a tradition treat hospitality as something close to sacred?

A contemplation exercise Welcome the next person who interrupts you as if their visit mattered.
