Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 2 · Vallī 3 · Mantra 12 of 18

Except for one who says ‘it is’

It cannot be reached by speech, by mind or by the eye. How can it be grasped, except by one who says ‘it is’?

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

It cannot be reached by words, nor by the mind, nor by the eye. How could it be known at all, except by one who says, ‘It is’?

Key concepts ब्रह्मन् Brahman इन्द्रिय indriya, a sense श्रद्धा faith, trust उपनिषद् Upaniṣad

7 Explain it to a beginner

The previous verses described stilling the senses and the mind. That raises a worry: if what we seek cannot be seen, said or even thought, how do we know it is there at all? The verse admits the first part: it cannot be reached by speech, by the mind or by the eyes. Then it asks: how could it be known except by one who says, ‘It is’? Śaṅkara explains that it is known as the root of everything, the way every clay pot leads back to clay, and that it is found by the one who, trusting scripture, affirms that it exists, never by the one who denies it. Raṅgarāmānuja reads ‘the one who says it is’ as scripture itself, since the Upaniṣads alone make this known. He also spends much of his comment on a side question: how many senses there are.

An analogy You cannot see the root of a tree, but the trunk, the leaves and the fruit all say that it is there.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse say?

It cannot be reached by speech, mind or eye; how could it be grasped, except by one who says ‘it is’?

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara: it is, as the root of the world

He states the objection: what the faculties do not reach is held not to exist, so Brahman would not be, and yoga would be pointless. The answer: true, it is not reached by speech, mind, eye or any sense, नैव वाचा न मनसा न चक्षुषा नान्यैरपीन्द्रियैः प्राप्तुं शक्यते इत्यर्थः; yet it is, known as the root of the world, तथापि सर्वविशेषरहितोऽपि जगतो मूलमित्यवगतत्वादस्त्येव, as a pot and the like lead back to clay. For the one who denies it, it is not grasped in any way, न कथञ्चनोपलभ्यत इत्यर्थः.

Traditional Interpretation

Raṅgarāmānuja: the senses, and scripture as the one who says ‘it is’

The first line is plain, स्पष्टोऽर्थः. He then takes up the Brahmasūtra’s discussion of whether the senses are seven or eleven, which cites 2.3.10, and concludes with the sūtra हस्तादयस्तु स्थितेऽतो नैवम् and the Gītā’s इन्द्रियाणि दशैकं च (as quoted by him) that they are eleven. The ‘one who says it is’ is scripture, अस्तीति ब्रुवत: शब्दात् अन्यत्र इत्यर्थः, since the self is known from the Upaniṣads alone, तस्य उपनिषदेकगम्यत्वात् इति भावः.

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva: ‘asti’ as a name

In verses he cites (इति च) on 2.3.12–13, Keśava, greater than all that is, is named ‘asti’ and is to be known so, अस्तीति नामकस्तस्माज्ज्ञातव्यस्स तथैव च; how could he be attained by those who do not know his greatness, अनाधिक्यं जानतां तु कथं स उपलभ्यते.

Comparative Vedānta

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Beyond every faculty, the self still is, known as the root of the world, जगतो मूलमित्यवगतत्वादस्त्येव; it is found by the one who affirms it with faith.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, Kaṭhopaniṣat-prakāśikā 2.3.12

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The self is known through scripture alone, तस्य उपनिषदेकगम्यत्वात् इति भावः; scripture is ‘the one who says it is’.

Dvaita · Madhva, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 2.3.12–13

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

In verses he cites, the Lord is named ‘asti’ and is attained only by those who know his supremacy, अनाधिक्यं जानतां तु कथं स उपलभ्यते.

Where they disagree

Śaṅkara reads ‘one who says it is’ as the person who affirms it with faith; Raṅgarāmānuja as scripture itself; Madhva, in verses he cites, makes ‘asti’ a name of the Lord and the condition of attaining him knowledge of his greatness.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • नैव naiva → न + एव

    a + e

    a + e → ai, P. 6.1.88 (वृद्धिरेचि).

  • प्राप्तुं शक्यो न prāptuṃ śakyo na → प्राप्तुम् + शक्यः + न

    m before a consonant; aḥ before a voiced consonant

    Final m → anusvāra before a consonant, P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः). -aḥ becomes -o before a voiced consonant, P. 6.1.114 (हशि च).

  • अस्तीति astīti → अस्ति + इति

    i + i

    Two like vowels merge into the long vowel, P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः).

  • ब्रुवतोऽन्यत्र bruvato'nyatra → ब्रुवतः + अन्यत्र

    aḥ before a

    The visarga of -aḥ becomes u before a, giving o, P. 6.1.114 (हशि च) with P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः), and the following a is merged, P. 6.1.109 (एङः पदान्तादति); the avagraha marks it.

  • कथं तदुपलभ्यते kathaṃ tadupalabhyate → कथम् + तत् + उपलभ्यते

    m before a consonant; t before a vowel

    Final m → anusvāra before a consonant, P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः). A final t becomes d before a voiced sound, P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते).

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this mantra.

Morphology

ब्रुवतः
Present participle of √brū, ablative singular, governed by anyatra, ‘apart from one who says’. Śaṅkara reads it of the one who affirms existence, following scripture, with faith; Raṅgarāmānuja of scripture itself (see the commentary).
शक्यः
śakya, gerundive of √śak, ‘able to be’, with the infinitive prāptum: ‘able to be reached’. Masculine, agreeing with the self (ātmā) of the preceding verses.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

The first line is one clause, the negation repeated with each instrument (vācā, manasā, cakṣuṣā), prāptuṃ śakyaḥ as predicate and the self understood as subject. The second line is a rhetorical question: kathaṃ tad upalabhyate, with anyatra governing the ablative participle bruvataḥ, whose object is the quoted asti iti.

Ambiguities

Who is ‘one who says it is’?
Śaṅkara: the one who affirms existence, following scripture, with faith, अस्तित्ववादिन आगमार्थानुसारिणः; for one who denies it, Brahman is not apprehended in any way, न कथञ्चनोपलभ्यत इत्यर्थः. Raṅgarāmānuja: the ‘one who says it is’ is scripture, अस्तीति ब्रुवत: शब्दात् अन्यत्र इत्यर्थः, because the self is known from the Upaniṣads alone, तस्य उपनिषदेकगम्यत्वात् इति भावः.
If no faculty reaches it, is it there at all?
Śaṅkara raises the objection that what no faculty reaches does not exist, and answers that it is known as the root of the world, तथापि सर्वविशेषरहितोऽपि जगतो मूलमित्यवगतत्वादस्त्येव: every effect traced back ends in being, as a pot leads back to clay.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Philosophy of mind

Some things are known not by being perceived but by being inferred or testified to. The verse grants that its subject is not an object of the senses and still asks the reader not to conclude that it is nothing. This is a reflection, not a claim made by the text.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin What do you believe exists though you have never seen it?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Is ‘one who says it is’ a person of faith, or the text itself? What changes between the two readings?

A contemplation exercise Notice one thing today you know only because someone told you.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
