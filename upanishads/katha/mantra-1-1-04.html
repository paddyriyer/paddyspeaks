Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 1 · Vallī 1 · Mantra 4 of 29

‘To whom will you give me?’

The boy asks his father three times to whom he will be given; the angry father answers: to Death.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

He said to his father, ‘Father, to whom will you give me?’ He asked a second time, and a third. His father said to him, ‘I give you to Death!’

Key concepts मृत्यु death सत्य truth, the real कर्मन् action, ritual work

7 Explain it to a beginner

The boy goes to his father with a question that is really a protest: ‘To whom will you give me?’ If the ritual requires giving everything, then he too belongs to his father; let him be given rather than worthless cows. The father ignores him. He asks again, and a third time. Finally the father, angry at being pressed in public, snaps: ‘I give you to Death!’ The words are spoken in irritation, but in the world of the Upaniṣad words spoken in a sacrifice cannot simply be taken back. The rest of the story follows from this outburst. The boy’s persistence and the father’s anger set him on the road to the house of Death.

An analogy Like a child who keeps asking an uncomfortable question at the dinner table until a parent loses patience and says something that cannot be unsaid.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the mantra say?

The boy asks his father to whom he will give him, three times; the father replies, ‘I give you to Death’.

Traditional Interpretation

What is the boy trying to do?

Śaṅkara: to prevent the bad result that would come to his father from a defective rite, by completing the gift with himself — तदेवं क्रत्वसम्पत्तिनिमित्तं पितुरनिष्टं फलं पुत्रेण सता निवारणीयं. Raṅgarāmānuja: स्वात्मदानेनापि पितुः क्रतुसाद्गुण्यमिच्छन्, wishing the rite complete even by giving himself.

Traditional Interpretation

Why does the father answer as he does?

Śaṅkara: thinking ‘this is not how a child behaves’, the father grew angry — नायं कुमारस्वभाव इति क्रुद्धः सन् पिता. Madhva cites a verse that reads the reply as a curse: मां दत्वापि न ते गावो दातव्या ईदृशा इति, ‘even giving me, you should not give cows like these’, and then तं शशाप पिता स्वयम्, the father himself cursed him.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

tata or tāta, dadāmi or dadāni?

The base, GRETIL and the vishvAsa mūla read tata; Madhva’s text reads tāta, the later form, which is also Śaṅkara’s gloss (हे तत तात). Madhva’s text reads dadāni, ‘let me give’, for dadāmi, ‘I give’ (corrections.json).

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 1.1.4

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The boy acts to save his father from the fruit of a defective rite, by offering himself; the father speaks in anger: नायं कुमारस्वभाव इति क्रुद्धः सन् पिता.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, Prakāśikā 1.1.4

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The boy, ‘foremost of believers’, wants his father’s rite complete even at the price of himself: स्वात्मदानेनापि पितुः क्रतुसाद्गुण्यमिच्छन्.

Dvaita · Madhva (a verse he cites), Kāṭhakopaniṣad-bhāṣya 1.1.4

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The reply is a curse: उवाच पुत्रस्तं बालस्तं शशाप पिता स्वयम्. Vedeśatīrtha, commenting on Madhva, quotes a Kāṭhaka prose version in which the father adds instructions for the boy’s stay in Death’s house (reported as cited by him).

Where they disagree

The schools agree on the boy’s motive; they differ on the force of the father’s words — an outburst of anger (Śaṅkara, Raṅgarāmānuja) or a formal curse (Madhva’s cited verse).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

  • स होवाच sa hovāca → सः + ह + उवाच

    saḥ before a consonant; a + u

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

Morphology

तत
Vocative of tata, a Vedic word for father (tāta in later Sanskrit). Śaṅkara glosses हे तत तात; Madhva’s text reads tāta (corrections.json).
ददामि
Present ‘I give’ with future sense, an angry declaration. Madhva’s text reads dadāni, first-person imperative, ‘let me give’ (corrections.json).
द्वितीयम्
Neuter accusatives used as adverbs: ‘a second time, a third time’ (he asked).

Anvaya

सः ह पितरम् उवाच — ‘तत, माम् कस्मै दास्यसि’ इति । द्वितीयम् तृतीयम् [उवाच] । तम् ह [पिता] उवाच — ‘त्वा मृत्यवे ददामि’ इति ॥

saḥ ha pitaram uvāca — ‘tata, mām kasmai dāsyasi’ iti | dvitīyam tṛtīyam uvāca | tam ha pitā uvāca — ‘tvā mṛtyave dadāmi’ iti ||

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Two quoted speeches framed by uvāca. The adverbs dvitīyam tṛtīyam stand alone: ‘[he said it] a second, a third time’. The father’s reply has no named subject; the context supplies it.

Ambiguities

Why does the boy offer himself?
Śaṅkara: the boy wanted to avert the bad result of a defective rite by completing it even at the cost of giving himself — आत्मप्रदानेनापि क्रतुसम्पत्तिं. Raṅgarāmānuja says the same: स्वात्मदानेनापि पितुः क्रतुसाद्गुण्यमिच्छन्. Madhva’s cited verse makes the point directly: give me rather than such cows.
Anger or curse?
Śaṅkara and Raṅgarāmānuja: the father, angry at the boy’s insistence, says it. Madhva’s cited verse calls it a curse: तं शशाप पिता स्वयम्.

Metre

prose · syllables counted as written: 34

Prose, 34 syllables as written; a dialogue in narrative prose.

Textual variations between witnesses

  • Variant reading Madhva’s mūla: पितरं तात कस्मै — Madhva’s text reads tāta, the usual vocative ‘father’, for the Vedic tata of the base, GRETIL and the vishvAsa mūla; Śaṅkara glosses tata as tāta.
  • Variant reading Madhva’s mūla: त्वा ददानीति — Madhva’s text reads dadānīti, ‘[I] shall give’ (first person imperative), for dadāmīti, ‘I give’.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Ego and self-image

Pressed in front of others, the father answers to save face, and the answer cannot be withdrawn. Many irreversible decisions are made this way. This is a modern reflection, not the text’s claim.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Have you ever said something in anger that you could not take back?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The boy asks three times. When is persistence a duty, and when is it provocation?

A contemplation exercise The next time you feel pressed into a quick answer, pause for one breath before replying.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
