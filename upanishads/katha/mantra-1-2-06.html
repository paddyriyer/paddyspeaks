Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 1 · Vallī 2 · Mantra 6 of 25

This world and no other

The hereafter never dawns on the heedless, dazed by wealth; thinking ‘this world, no other’, he falls into Death’s power again and again.

5 Faithful English translation

‘The hereafter does not shine upon the child, heedless, deluded by the delusion of wealth. “This [is the] world; there is no other”: thinking so, he comes again and again into my power.’

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

‘What lies beyond never dawns on the childish, careless and dazed by wealth. “This world is all there is,” he thinks, “there is no other” — and so he falls into my hands again and again.’

Key concepts साम्पराय sāmparāya, the passing-on मृत्यु death लोक world, realm मोह delusion

7 Explain it to a beginner

Having described those who think they are wise, Death describes where their thinking leads. To a childish person, careless and dazzled by money, the question of what lies beyond death never even comes up. Such a person decides that this world is all there is and that there is nothing after it. Death then says, quietly, that this person comes into his power again and again. In the Upaniṣad’s picture, refusing to think about death does not protect anyone from it; it only means meeting it over and over, in birth after birth, without ever understanding it. The verse is the opposite of Naciketas, a boy who walked into Death’s house to ask exactly the question that others avoid.

An analogy Like a student who decides there will be no examination and so never studies, and keeps sitting the same examination again.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does Death say?

That the hereafter does not occur to the heedless, the person dazed by wealth, who thinks ‘this world is all; there is no other’; and that such a person comes into Death’s power again and again.

Traditional Interpretation

What is ‘coming into my power’?

Śaṅkara: being born again and again and falling under the control of Death: पुनः पुनः जनित्वा वशम् अधीनताम् आपद्यते मे मृत्योर्मम, that is, riding a chain of sorrows marked by birth and death, जननमरणादिलक्षणदुःखप्रबन्धारूढ एव भवतीत्यर्थः. Raṅgarāmānuja: he becomes the object of the torments Death inflicts, मत्क्रियमाणयातना विषयो भवति इत्यर्थः.

Comparative Vedānta

How is the verse used elsewhere?

Śaṅkara quotes it in full in his Brahmasūtra-bhāṣya on 3.1.13, the sūtra on the fate of evildoers, as a scripture in the words of Yama himself that shows them falling under his control: अनिष्टादिकारिणां यमवश्यतां दर्शयति.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What is the force of mānī?

An agent noun from man ‘think’: ‘one who [habitually] thinks so’ — Śaṅkara: इत्येवं मननशीलः मानी. Since mānin also means ‘proud’, Raṅgarāmānuja reads ‘wrongly proud’: दुर्मानी.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 1.2.6

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The careless person sees only this visible world, स्त्र्यन्नपानादिविशिष्टः, denies the unseen one, and so remains in the round of birth and death under Death’s control.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, Kaṭhopaniṣat-prakāśikā 1.2.6

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Thinking that only this world exists and the other does not, अयमेव लोकोऽस्ति; परलोको नास्तीति मन्यमानः, he becomes subject to the torments Death inflicts.

Dvaita

No position is given for this school here: the edition does not force one.

Where they disagree

Śaṅkara stresses repeated birth and death; Raṅgarāmānuja the torments in Yama’s realm, the reading the Brahmasūtra (3.1.13) also gives. Madhva comments on the passage 1.2.4–8 only at 1.2.8.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • बालं प्रमाद्यन्तं वित्तमोहेन bālaṃ pramādyantaṃ vittamohena → बालम् + प्रमाद्यन्तम् + वित्तमोहेन

    final m before a consonant

    P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः).

  • अयं लोको नास्ति ayaṃ loko nāsti → अयम् + लोकः + न + अस्ति

    final m before a consonant; aḥ before a voiced consonant; savarṇa-dīrgha

    ayam before l: P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः). lokaḥ before n: P. 6.1.114 (हशि च), P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः). na + asti: a + a give ā, P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः).

  • पर इति para iti → परः + इति

    aḥ before a vowel other than a

    The visarga becomes y, P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि), and is dropped, P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य); the hiatus stays.

  • पुनर्वशमापद्यते punarvaśamāpadyate → पुनः + वशम् + आपद्यते

    r before a voiced consonant; final m before a vowel

    punar ends in r, which is kept before the voiced v (it becomes visarga only before a pause or a voiceless sound, P. 8.3.15 (खरवसानयोर्विसर्जनीयः)). vaśam keeps its m before ā.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • वित्तमोहेन vittamohena

    tatpuruṣa · वित्त + मोह

    Vigraha वित्तनिमित्तः मोहः

    delusion caused by wealth; Śaṅkara: वित्तनिमित्तेनाविवेकेन

Morphology

साम्परायः
A derivative (with vṛddhi of the first syllable) of samparāya ‘the passing-on, the hereafter’: ‘what concerns the hereafter’. Śaṅkara explains both: सम्परेयत इति सम्परायः परलोकः, and the derivative as the scriptural means of reaching it.
प्रमाद्यन्तम्
Present participle of pra-mad, fourth class (the stem vowel lengthened: mādya-): ‘being careless, distracted’.
मानी
mānin, an agent noun of man ‘think’: one who habitually thinks so. It can also mean ‘proud, conceited’, which is how Raṅgarāmānuja takes it: ‘मानी’ इत्यस्य दुर्मानीत्यर्थः.

Anvaya

प्रमाद्यन्तम् वित्तमोहेन मूढम् बालम् साम्परायः न प्रतिभाति । अयम् लोकः [अस्ति], परः न अस्ति इति मानी पुनः पुनः मे वशम् आपद्यते ।

pramādyantam vittamohena mūḍham bālam sāmparāyaḥ na pratibhāti | ayam lokaḥ asti, paraḥ na asti iti mānī punaḥ punaḥ me vaśam āpadyate |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Two sentences. In the first, sāmparāyaḥ is the subject of na pratibhāti, and the accusative bālam, with two participles and an instrumental of cause, names the person to whom it does not appear. In the second, a quoted thought closed by iti — ‘this [is the] world; there is no other’ — is held by mānī, the subject of āpadyate; me, ‘my’, is Death’s.

Ambiguities

What is sāmparāya?
Śaṅkara takes it as the scriptural means of reaching the next world, derived from samparāya, the next world itself: सम्परेयत इति सम्परायः परलोकः, तत्प्राप्तिप्रयोजनः साधनविशेषः शास्त्रीयः साम्परायः. Raṅgarāmānuja: the activity that secures the next world, परलोकसाधनव्यापारः. At 1.1.29 the same word named the passing-on itself.
‘This world; there is no other’ — or ‘neither this nor the other’
Both commentators read: only this world exists, there is no other. Raṅgarāmānuja reports that on the sūtra BS 3.1.13 the verse was read अयं लोको नास्ति पर उत मानी and explained as denying both worlds, अयं च लोकः परश्च लोको नास्तीत्यर्थो वर्णितः; he adds that the reading is not accepted by the learned, शिष्टपरिग्रहाभावात्.

Metre

triṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 22 + 23

The halves count 22 and 23. The third pāda, ayaṃ loko nāsti para iti mānī, has 12, with the hiatus para iti counted as written; the other pādas have 11.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Aging and mortality

It is possible to live for decades without once asking seriously what a life is for or what death means, especially when there is money to spend and much to do. The verse does not threaten such a person with punishment so much as describe a pattern: the question avoided keeps returning, at every loss and every illness, until it is faced. This is a reflection, not part of the commentaries.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin When did the question of death last come to your mind, and what did you do with it?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Is ‘this world is all there is’ a conclusion people reach, or a habit of not looking?

A contemplation exercise Spend five quiet minutes with the question Naciketas asked, without trying to answer it.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
