Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 1 · Vallī 2 · Mantra 4 of 25

Far apart: knowledge and ignorance

Ignorance and knowledge lie far apart and lead opposite ways; Death judges Naciketas a seeker of knowledge, since no desire tore him away.

5 Faithful English translation

‘Far apart [are] these two, opposed, going different ways: what is known as ignorance and [what is known] as knowledge. I consider Naciketas one who longs for knowledge; desires, many [as they were], did not tear you away.’

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

‘Ignorance and what is called knowledge are far apart; they are opposites and lead in opposite directions. I see that Naciketas longs for knowledge: all those desires could not pull you away.’

Key concepts विद्या vidyā अविद्या avidyā मृत्यु death वर boon

7 Explain it to a beginner

Death now says something about two ways of living, which he calls ignorance and knowledge. They are not just a little different; they lie far apart and lead in opposite directions, like light and darkness, as one commentator puts it. Ignorance here is the life that follows the pleasant; knowledge is the life that follows the good. Then Death turns to the boy and gives his judgement: Naciketas wants knowledge. Death knows this because he tested him. He offered many desirable things, and none of them could pull the boy away from what he had asked. A person’s real wish is shown by what he refuses, not only by what he says he wants. The test is over, and the teaching can begin.

An analogy Like two roads that leave the same crossroads in opposite directions: a few steps apart at first, they end very far from each other.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does Death say?

That ignorance and what is called knowledge are far apart, opposed and divergent; and that he judges Naciketas to be one who longs for knowledge, because many desires could not tear him away.

Traditional Interpretation

Why are they far apart?

Śaṅkara: because one is discrimination and the other its absence, like darkness and light, विवेकाविवेकात्मकत्वात् तमःप्रकाशाविव; they lead to different results, worldly existence and release, विषूच्यौ नानागती भिन्नफले संसारमोक्षहेतुत्वेनेत्येतत्. Ignorance concerns the pleasant, knowledge the good: या च अविद्या प्रेयोविषया विद्येति च श्रेयोविषया.

Traditional Interpretation

What are knowledge and ignorance for Raṅgarāmānuja?

Ignorance is made of desire and action, कामकर्मात्मिका; knowledge consists of dispassion and knowledge of the truth, वैराग्यतत्त्वज्ञानमयी. The desires did not break him from the path of the good: श्रेयोमार्गाद्विच्छेदं न कृतवन्तः.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

How is alolupanta formed?

An intensive of lup ‘break, tear away’. Raṅgarāmānuja cites P. 3.1.24 (लुपसदचरजपजभदहदशगॄभ्यो भावगर्हायाम्) for the intensive and notes the Vedic irregularities: छान्दसो यलोपः, or a Vedic middle and no augment, अडभावश्च. Śaṅkara glosses the sense: the desires did not cut him off, न विच्छेदं कृतवन्तः.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 1.2.4

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Knowledge and ignorance are as opposed as light and darkness and cause release and worldly existence respectively; Naciketas, unmoved by desire, is a fit vessel of the good: अतो विद्यार्थिनं श्रेयोभाजनं मन्ये इत्यभिप्रायः.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, Kaṭhopaniṣat-prakāśikā 1.2.4

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Ignorance is desire and action, knowledge is dispassion and knowledge of the truth; the boy is not under the sway of objects: विषयवशगो न भवसि इत्यर्थः.

Dvaita

No position is given for this school here: the edition does not force one.

Where they disagree

Śaṅkara and Raṅgarāmānuja agree on the verse. Śaṅkara defines the pair by discrimination (viveka), Raṅgarāmānuja by its content (desire and action against dispassion and knowledge of the truth). Madhva comments on the passage 1.2.4–8 only at 1.2.8; his text has two variants here.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • दूरमेते dūramete → दूरम् + एते

    final m before a vowel

    The m stays before a vowel.

  • विषूची अविद्या viṣūcī avidyā → विषूची + अविद्या

    pragṛhya dual

    viṣūcī is a dual ending in ī and so pragṛhya, P. 1.1.11 (ईदूदेद्द्विवचनं प्रगृह्यम्); it does not combine with the following a, P. 6.1.125 (प्लुतप्रगृह्या अचि नित्यम्).

  • विद्येति vidyeti → विद्या + इति

    guṇa

    ā + i give e, P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः).

  • विद्याभीप्सिनं नचिकेतसं मन्ये vidyābhīpsinaṃ naciketasaṃ manye → विद्याभीप्सिनम् + नचिकेतसम् + मन्ये

    final m before a consonant

    P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः).

  • कामा बहवोऽलोलुपन्त kāmā bahavo'lolupanta → कामाः + बहवः + अलोलुपन्त

    āḥ before a voiced consonant; aḥ before a

    kāmāḥ before b: the visarga becomes y, P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि), which is dropped before a consonant, P. 8.3.22 (हलि सर्वेषाम्). bahavaḥ before a: P. 6.1.113 (अतो रोरप्लुतादप्लुते), P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः), P. 6.1.109 (एङः पदान्तादति).

Junctions outside the general rules

  • विषूची · अविद्या — pragṛhya: viṣūcī avidyā: the dual viṣūcī is pragṛhya, P. 1.1.11 (ईदूदेद्द्विवचनं प्रगृह्यम्), and does not combine with the following vowel, P. 6.1.125 (प्लुतप्रगृह्या अचि नित्यम्).

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • विद्याभीप्सिनम् vidyābhīpsinam

    upapada-tatpuruṣa · विद्या + अभीप्सिन्

    Vigraha विद्याम् अभीप्सति इति

    one who longs for knowledge; Śaṅkara and Raṅgarāmānuja: विद्यार्थिनं

Morphology

विषूची
Feminine dual of viṣvañc ‘turned in different directions’, in the Vedic form in -ī; the classical form is viṣūcyau, which Śaṅkara gives as his gloss: विषूची विषूच्यौ.
अलोलुपन्त
Raṅgarāmānuja analyses it as the imperfect of the intensive of lup, citing P. 3.1.24 (लुपसदचरजपजभदहदशगॄभ्यो भावगर्हायाम्), with a Vedic loss of y (lolupya- > lolup-): छान्दसो यलोपः; or as the intensive without its suffix, with a Vedic middle ending and no augment: यङ्लुगन्ताद्वा छान्दसमात्मनेपदम्, अडभावश्च. In the text the augment a- is hidden by the sandhi bahavo ’lolupanta.
विद्याभीप्सिनम्
abhīpsin ‘longing for’: abhi + īps, the desiderative of āp ‘obtain’, with the agent suffix -in.
त्वा
The enclitic accusative of tvam.

Anvaya

या अविद्या, या च विद्या इति ज्ञाता, एते दूरम् विपरीते विषूची [च] । नचिकेतसम् विद्याभीप्सिनम् मन्ये । बहवः कामाः त्वा न अलोलुपन्त ।

yā avidyā, yā ca vidyā iti jñātā, ete dūram viparīte viṣūcī ca | naciketasam vidyābhīpsinam manye | bahavaḥ kāmāḥ tvā na alolupanta |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Three clauses. A nominal sentence with the dual subject ete and two dual predicates (viparīte, viṣūcī), explained by the relative clause avidyā yā ca vidyeti jñātā, where yā serves both nouns. Then Death’s judgement, with a double accusative (naciketasaṃ vidyābhīpsinaṃ manye), and its reason, the many desires did not tear you away; Death passes from the third person (Naciketas) to the second (tvā).

Ambiguities

vidyābhīpsinam or vidyābhīpsitam
The base and the other witnesses read vidyābhīpsinam, ‘longing for knowledge’. Madhva’s text reads vidyābhīpsitam (corrections.json). Raṅgarāmānuja knows that reading and explains it as a compound with the participle placed last, as in āhitāgni: ‘विद्याभीप्सितम्’ इति पाठे आहिताग्नयादित्वात् निष्ठान्तस्य परनिपातः, so that it means the same.
alolupanta or lolupantaḥ
Madhva’s text reads bahavo lolupantaḥ, a participle, ‘[you, whom] many greedy desires [did not move]’ (corrections.json). The base reading is a finite verb with a hidden augment.

Metre

triṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 21 + 23

The halves count 21 and 23: the pādas have 11, 10, 12 and 11 syllables. avidyā yā ca vidyeti jñātā has 10; vidyābhīpsinaṃ naciketasaṃ manye has 12. The hiatus viṣūcī avidyā is counted as written.

Textual variations between witnesses

  • Variant reading Madhva’s mūla: विद्याभीप्सितं नचिकेतसं — Madhva’s text reads vidyābhīpsitaṃ, ‘longed for by knowledge’, for vidyābhīpsinaṃ, ‘longing for knowledge’.
  • Variant reading Madhva’s mūla: बहवो लोलुपन्तः — Madhva’s text reads bahavo lolupantaḥ, a participle (‘many [desires], greedy’), for bahavo ’lolupanta, ‘many [desires] did not tempt [you]’.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Learning and apprenticeship

Teachers often learn more about a student’s seriousness from what the student turns down than from what the student says. Death’s judgement of Naciketas rests on exactly that: the offers refused. The verse also insists that the two directions are not a little apart but far apart — a reminder that small daily preferences add up to very different lives. This is a reflection, not part of the commentaries.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin What have you turned down recently, and what did that show about what you want?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Why would the Upaniṣad call the life of the pleasant ‘ignorance’ rather than ‘wrongdoing’?

A contemplation exercise Notice one small preference today and ask which of the two directions it leans.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
