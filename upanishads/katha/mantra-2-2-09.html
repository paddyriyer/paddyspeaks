Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 2 · Vallī 2 · Mantra 9 of 15

The one fire in every form

As one fire, entering the world, takes the shape of every form, so the one inner self of all beings takes the shape of every form, and is outside as well.

5 Faithful English translation

As fire, one, having entered the world, became corresponding in form to each and every form, so the one inner self of all beings [is] corresponding in form to each and every form, and [is] outside.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

As the one fire, entering the world, takes the shape of whatever it burns, so the one self within all beings takes the shape of each form, and yet remains outside them.

Key concepts अन्तरात्मन् the inner self अग्नि Agni, fire आत्मन् self, Self एकत्व oneness जीव individual self ब्रह्मन् Brahman

7 Explain it to a beginner

Think of a fire. It is one fire, yet when it burns a log it looks like a log on fire, when it burns a straw it looks like a burning straw, and so on: it takes the shape of whatever it enters. Death tells Naciketas that the self within all beings is like that. There is one inner self in every creature, and it takes the shape of each body, yet it is also outside all of them. Śaṅkara says the one self only seems many because of the bodies it appears in, while in itself it stays unchanged, like open space. Raṅgarāmānuja says the supreme Self is present inside each thing as its inner ruler and also reaches beyond it. Madhva reads the ‘many shapes’ as other beings that depend on God and reflect him, never as God himself.

An analogy One flame lit under many differently shaped lamps shows as many flames of different shapes; the fire itself is one.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse say?

A comparison: as one fire, entering the world, takes the shape of every form it meets, so the one self within all beings takes the shape of every form, and is outside as well.

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara: why the teaching is repeated

He says the scripture repeats the teaching of the self’s oneness again and again out of care, because minds unsettled by bad reasoning do not take it in even when it is stated many times: तत्प्रतिपादने आदरवती पुनः पुनराह श्रुतिः. The fire, one and of the nature of light, enters the world and becomes manifold according to what it burns, दाह्यभेदेन बहुविधो बभूव; so the self, being very subtle, enters every body, and yet is outside in its own unchanged form, बहिश्च स्वेनाविकृतेन रूपेण आकाशवत्.

Traditional Interpretation

Raṅgarāmānuja: the inner ruler in each thing

He too says the point is repeated because it is hard to grasp, इत्यस्य अर्थस्य दुर्बोधत्वात् तदृढीकरणाय पुनरपि उपदिशति. The fire-element, spread through the world, has its form laid into each material thing; so the one supreme Self, his form as inner ruler entering each thing, also pervades outside, प्रतिवस्तु प्रतिसंक्रान्तान्तर्यामिविग्रहः, बहिश्च व्याप्नोति इत्यर्थः.

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva: the counterparts are not the Lord

His comment, in verse, after this mantra: as the one fire that cooks is in the worlds and there is no other, अग्निर्यथैको लोकेषु प्रविष्टोऽन्यो न विद्यते, so the insentient fires in every form are that god’s counterparts, रूपं रूपं प्रति ह्येते सन्त्यचेतनवह्नयः. The ‘many forms’ are things distinct from him that bear his likeness.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

A word on the image

rūpaṃ rūpam is a distributive repetition, ‘each and every form’; the same pair, and the whole second line, return in 2.2.10 with wind in place of fire.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 2.2.9

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The one self appears manifold through the bodies it enters, as fire through what it burns, and remains unchanged outside them, बहिश्च स्वेनाविकृतेन रूपेण आकाशवत्.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, Kaṭhopaniṣat-prakāśikā 2.2.9

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The supreme Self is in each thing as its inner ruler and pervades beyond it: प्रतिवस्तु प्रतिसंक्रान्तान्तर्यामिविग्रहः, बहिश्च व्याप्नोति इत्यर्थः.

Dvaita · Madhva, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 2.2.9

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The fires in every form are insentient counterparts of the one god, रूपं रूपं प्रति ह्येते सन्त्यचेतनवह्नयः, not the god himself.

Where they disagree

All three read one in many. For Śaṅkara the many is appearance through adjuncts; for Raṅgarāmānuja the one is truly present in each as its inner ruler; for Madhva the many are distinct counterparts that bear his likeness. The verse states only the comparison.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • अग्निर्यथैको agniryathaiko → अग्निः + यथा + एकः

    visarga as r

    After i or u the visarga becomes r before a voiced sound: the s becomes ru, P. 8.2.66 (ससजुषो रुँः).

  • यथैको yathaiko → यथा + एकः

    Visarga before c becomes s, P. 8.3.34 (विसर्जनीयस्य सः), and s before c becomes ś, P. 8.4.40 (स्तोः श्चुना श्चुः).

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • सर्वभूतान्तरात्मा sarvabhūtāntarātmā

    ṣaṣṭhī-tatpuruṣa (with a karmadhāraya in each member) · सर्व + भूत + अन्तर + आत्मन्

    Vigraha सर्वाणि भूतानि सर्वभूतानि, अन्तरः आत्मा अन्तरात्मा, सर्वभूतानाम् अन्तरात्मा

    the inner self of all beings

  • प्रतिरूपः pratirūpaḥ

    bahuvrīhi · प्रति + रूप

    Vigraha प्रत्युप्तं रूपं यस्य सः

    one whose form is laid into each thing (Raṅgarāmānuja’s analysis)

Morphology

रूपम्
rūpaṃ rūpam is a repetition for distribution (vīpsā), P. 8.1.4 (नित्यवीप्सयोः): ‘every single form’. Raṅgarāmānuja: भौतिकव्यक्तिषु वीप्सायां द्विर्वचनम्. Śaṅkara takes it with prati, रूपं रूपं प्रति: towards each form.
बभूव
Perfect of √bhū, ‘became’: the fire, one, came to have many shapes.
भुवनम्
From √bhū, ‘that in which beings come to be’; Śaṅkara’s gloss gives this derivation.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

A yathā … tathā comparison over two lines. In the first, agniḥ is the subject of babhūva, with ekaḥ and praviṣṭaḥ as predicates and rūpaṃ rūpam pratirūpaḥ saying what it became. In the second, the subject sarvabhūtāntarātmā repeats the same predicates; babhūva is understood, and bahiś ca adds what fire does not illustrate: the self is also outside.

Ambiguities

What does pratirūpaḥ mean?
Śaṅkara: having a counterpart form in each thing, तत्र तत्र प्रतिरूपवान्, many through the difference of what it burns, दाह्यभेदेन बहुविधो बभूव. Raṅgarāmānuja: one whose form is laid into each thing, प्रत्युप्तं रूपं यस्य स तथोक्तः. Madhva (on 2.2.9–10) takes the ‘counterparts’ to be other things than the Lord: the insentient fires are his प्रतिरूपकाः, and, for the second line, conscious souls are his reflections.
What is ‘outside’ (bahiḥ)?
Śaṅkara: outside the forms in its own unchanged nature, like space. Raṅgarāmānuja: the supreme Self pervades outside as well, बहिश्च व्याप्नोति इत्यर्थः. Madhva (his comment after 2.2.10): it is the souls, the reflections, that are ‘outside’ the Lord and never his own nature, बाह्याश्च ते ततो नास्य स्वरूपं ते कथञ्चन.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Philosophy of mind

Whether there is one awareness appearing in many minds, or many minds each with its own awareness, is still argued over. The verse offers an image rather than an argument: one fire, many shapes. This is a reflection, not a claim made by the text.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin What stays the same in you while your shape, mood and role keep changing?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The fire image fits the first line; why does the verse need ‘and outside’ (bahiś ca) for the self?

A contemplation exercise Look at a flame for a minute: notice that its shape is the wick’s and the draught’s, while the fire is the same.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
