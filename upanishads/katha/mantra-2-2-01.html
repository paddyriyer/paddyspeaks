Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 2 · Vallī 2 · Mantra 1 of 15

The city of eleven gates

The body is a city with eleven gates belonging to the unborn one; one who attends to him does not grieve and, freed, is freed. This is that.

5 Faithful English translation

Having attended to the eleven-gated city of the unborn one, whose thought is not crooked, one does not grieve; and, freed, one is freed. This truly is that.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

The body is a city of eleven gates belonging to the unborn one, whose awareness never bends. Whoever attends to him grieves no more; already free, he is set free. This is that.

Key concepts पुर pura, the city (of the body) आत्मन् self, Self मोक्ष liberation शोक grief, sorrow इन्द्रिय indriya, a sense ईश्वर the Lord

7 Explain it to a beginner

The second part of Death’s teaching opens with a picture. The body is like a walled city with eleven gates. Śaṅkara counts them as the body’s openings: seven in the head, the navel and two below it, and one at the crown. A city does not exist for its own sake; its gates, guards and streets serve a ruler who lives inside but is not one of its buildings. So the body serves an owner, the unborn one whose awareness never bends. One who keeps attention on that owner stops grieving and is freed. Raṅgarāmānuja reads the gates as the senses and the owner as the individual self that knows itself as distinct from the body; Madhva says the city belongs to the Lord.

An analogy Like a walled town whose gates, guards and markets all work for a ruler who lives within but is not one of its houses.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse say?

The body is a city of eleven gates whose owner is unborn and straight in thought. One who attends to him does not grieve; freed, he is freed. This is that.

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara: a new approach to the hard-to-know

He takes the verse as a fresh attempt at the same truth, because Brahman is hard to know, पुनरपि प्रकारान्तरेण ब्रह्मतत्त्वनिर्धारणार्थोऽयमारम्भः, दुर्विज्ञेयत्वाद्ब्रह्मणः. The body is called a city because, like a city, it is an assembly of parts that serves a master who is not part of the assembly, स्वात्मना असंहतराजस्थानीयस्वाम्यर्थं भवितुमर्हति. Attending means meditation grounded in right knowledge, ध्यानं हि तस्यानुष्ठानं सम्यग्विज्ञानपूर्वकम्.

Traditional Interpretation

Raṅgarāmānuja: the discerning self and its city

The city belongs to the discerning self, free of birth and change, जननादिविक्रियारहितस्य ऋजुबुद्धेः विवेकिन आत्मनः; as a city stands apart from its lord, the body is known as apart from the self. For him the release is in two stages: free of sorrow and passion while living, जीवद्दशायाम् आध्यात्मिकादिदुःख रागद्वेषादिविमुक्त एव सन्, and free of matter at the end, citing the Brahmasūtra 'भोगेन तु इतरे क्षपयित्वाऽथ सम्पद्यते'. The refrain means that even the freed self has the supreme Self as its self, एतन्मन्त्रप्रतिपाद्यमुक्तस्वरूपमपि, परमात्मात्मकमेव इत्यर्थः.

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva: the city is the Lord’s

Attending is settling the conviction that the city belongs to the Lord, इदं पुरं भगवदधीनमित्यनुष्ठाय । अजस्येदं पुरमिति स्थितिं कृत्वा. For the twofold release he cites a verse, naming no source (इति च): one is freed first, through having no self-conceit, विमुक्तो निरभीमानात् पूर्वमेवापरोक्षवित्, and chiefly freed afterwards, मुख्यतो मुच्यते पश्चात् दुःखाद्याभासहानतः.

Comparative Vedānta

Three owners of one city

The same genitive ajasya names the supreme self within (Śaṅkara), the individual self that discerns itself from the body (Raṅgarāmānuja) and the Lord to whom the body belongs (Madhva). All three agree that the owner is not the city; they differ on who the owner is.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Freed, and freed

vimuktaḥ is a past participle and vimucyate a present passive of the same root. Read together they mark a release already true and one still taking place, which each commentator places differently in time.

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The owner of the city is the supreme Lord, meditated on as one in all beings, तं सर्वैषणाविनिर्मुक्तः सन्समं सर्वभूतस्थं ध्यात्वा न शोचति; freed here, one takes no body again.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, Kaṭhopaniṣat-prakāśikā 2.2.1

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The discerning self knows the body as distinct, विविच्य जानन्, is freed from sorrow while living and from matter at the end; even then it has the supreme Self as its self.

Dvaita · Madhva, Kāṭhakopaniṣad-bhāṣya 2.2.1

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The city belongs to the Lord, इदं पुरं भगवदधीनमित्यनुष्ठाय; release comes first through the loss of self-conceit and fully afterwards.

Where they disagree

All three read the body as a city with an owner other than itself. Śaṅkara and Madhva make the owner the supreme Lord; Raṅgarāmānuja makes him the discerning individual self, whose own self is the supreme. They agree that release has two moments, now and later.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • अजस्यावक्रचेतसः ajasyāvakracetasaḥ → अजस्य + अवक्रचेतसः

    a + a

    Two like vowels merge into the long vowel, P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः).

  • विमुक्तश्च vimuktaśca → विमुक्तः + च

    visarga before c

    The visarga becomes s before a voiceless consonant, P. 8.3.34 (विसर्जनीयस्य सः), and s becomes ś before c, P. 8.4.40 (स्तोः श्चुना श्चुः).

  • एतद्वै etadvai → एतत् + वै

    t before a voiced consonant

    A final t becomes d before a voiced sound, P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते).

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • एकादशद्वारम् ekādaśadvāram

    bahuvrīhi · एकादशन् + द्वार

    Vigraha एकादश द्वाराणि यस्य तत्

    that which has eleven gates: the city of the body

  • अवक्रचेतसः avakracetasaḥ

    bahuvrīhi (its first member a nañ-tatpuruṣa, न वक्रम्) · अवक्र + चेतस्

    Vigraha अवक्रं चेतः यस्य सः

    one whose thought is not crooked

Morphology

अनुष्ठाय
anu-√sthā with the absolutive suffix -ya, used after a preverb: ‘having stood by, having attended to’. The s of sthā is retroflex after anu. What the attending is, the commentators decide differently (see the word table).
विमुच्यते
The present passive of vi-√muc. With the participle vimuktaḥ of the same root, the line says that one already freed is freed: two moments of one release.

Anvaya

अजस्य अवक्रचेतसः एकादशद्वारम् पुरम् अनुष्ठाय न शोचति । विमुक्तः च विमुच्यते । एतत् वै तत् ।

ajasya avakracetasaḥ ekādaśadvāram puram anuṣṭhāya na śocati | vimuktaḥ ca vimucyate | etat vai tat |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

The absolutive anuṣṭhāya, with its object puram and the genitives ajasya avakracetasaḥ naming the city’s owner, comes before na śocati; the one who attends is not named. The second clause sets the participle vimuktaḥ beside the finite vimucyate of the same root. The refrain etad vai tat closes the verse, as in vallī 2.1.

Ambiguities

Whose city is it?
Śaṅkara: the unborn, changeless self, the king of the city, whom one meditates on as its lord, तं परमेश्वरं पुरस्वामिनम् अनुष्ठाय ध्यात्वा. Raṅgarāmānuja: the discerning individual self, who knows the body as distinct from himself, अविवेकिनस्तु देह आत्मैव भवतीति भावः. Madhva: the city belongs to the Lord, इदं पुरं भगवदधीनमित्यनुष्ठाय.
The eleven gates
Śaṅkara counts openings of the body: seven in the head, three below with the navel, one at the crown. Raṅgarāmānuja takes the gates as the eleven senses through which one goes outward.
vimuktaś ca vimucyate
Śaṅkara: freed here from the bonds of ignorance, desire and action, and then not taking a body again. Raṅgarāmānuja: freed while living from sorrow and passion, and at the end of the karma already begun, freed from matter after the path of light, प्रारब्धकर्मावसाने अचिरादिना विरजां प्राप्य प्रकृतिसम्बन्धविमुक्तो भवतीत्यर्थः. Madhva cites a verse: freed first by giving up self-conceit, and chiefly freed afterwards.

Metre

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Ego and self-image

We easily speak of the body as ‘me’. The image of a city with an owner asks whether the one who notices hunger, sight and sound is the same as the streets those things travel along. This is a reflection, not a claim made by the text.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin If your body is a city, who lives in it?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Compare Śaṅkara’s count of the eleven gates with Raṅgarāmānuja’s. What changes in the teaching when the gates are senses rather than openings?

A contemplation exercise For a few minutes, notice sights and sounds arriving as if through gates, and notice the one who receives them.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
