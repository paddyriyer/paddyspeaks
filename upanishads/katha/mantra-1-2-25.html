Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 1 · Vallī 2 · Mantra 25 of 25

For whom priest and ruler are food

He for whom the priesthood and the warrior class are both rice, and death the sauce poured on it — who knows thus where he is?

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

‘He for whom priests and kings alike are only rice, and death itself the sauce poured over it — who can truly know where he is?’

Key concepts आत्मन् self, Self मृत्यु death ब्रह्मन् Brahman ईश्वर the Lord

7 Explain it to a beginner

The second chapter of the Kaṭha ends with a striking picture. Imagine a being for whom the priests and the kings, the two most powerful groups in ancient society, are just a bowl of rice, and death itself is only the sauce poured over it. Who can really know where such a being is? Śaṅkara says the verse describes the self, which is greater than everything that seems great; even death, which takes all, is merely seasoning to it. Someone who has not prepared in the ways the last verse described cannot know it. Raṅgarāmānuja says priests and kings stand for the whole world, and the image shows the supreme Lord who, at the end of time, takes everything back into himself. The tone is meant to humble the reader.

An analogy Like a meal seen from the diner’s side: what seemed a whole world to the grains of rice is one bowl to the one who eats.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the verse say?

That there is one for whom priest and warrior together are food and death the sauce, and it asks who can truly know where he is.

Traditional Interpretation

Why food and sauce?

Śaṅkara: even the two classes that uphold everything are only food to the self, and death, which takes all, is like the sauce on rice — not even enough to be a meal: सर्वहरोऽपि मृत्युः यस्य उपसेचनमिवौदनस्य, अशनत्वेऽप्यपर्याप्तः.

Comparative Vedānta

How does Raṅgarāmānuja argue it?

Odana cannot literally be said of two classes, so a quality must be meant; since death, the sauce, is itself eaten while causing others to be eaten, the meal is what is destroyed, यस्य विनाश्यो भवतीत्यर्थः, and the one described is the Supreme who withdraws the whole world. He cites the Brahmasūtra अत्ता चराचरग्रहणात्.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Does Madhva comment?

Not in our source: his bhāṣya gives the verse and the vallī’s colophon without comment. His text reads ca cobhe (corrections.json).

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 1.2.25

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The self is so great that death is only sauce to it; the unprepared cannot know it: इत्थमेवं यथोक्तसाधनवानिवेत्यर्थः.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, Kaṭhopaniṣat-prakāśikā 1.2.25

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The verse describes the Supreme who withdraws the whole world into himself, निखिलचराचरसंहर्ता परमात्मा, as the Brahmasūtra on the ‘eater’ concludes.

Dvaita

No position is given for this school here: the edition does not force one.

Where they disagree

Śaṅkara reads the eater as the self, whose greatness dwarfs even death; Raṅgarāmānuja as the Supreme who destroys the world at its end. Madhva does not comment here in our source.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • क्षत्रं च kṣatraṃ ca → क्षत्रम् + च

    anusvāra

    Final m → anusvāra before a consonant, P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः).

  • भवत ओदनः bhavata odanaḥ → भवतः + ओदनः

    visarga before a vowel

    The visarga of -aḥ before a vowel other than a is lost and the hiatus stays: P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि) with P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य).

  • मृत्युर्यस्योपसेचनं mṛtyuryasyopasecanaṃ → मृत्युः + यस्य + उपसेचनम्

    visarga; vowel sandhi

    After u the visarga becomes r before a voiced sound, P. 8.2.66 (ससजुषो रुँः); a + u → o, P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः); final m → anusvāra before a consonant, P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः).

  • क इत्था ka itthā → कः + इत्था

    visarga before a vowel

    As above: the visarga of -aḥ before i is lost and the hiatus stays, P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि) with P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य).

Junctions outside the general rules

  • च · उभे — vedic hiatus: ca ubhe: a before u is not joined (regular sandhi would give cobhe). The hiatus keeps the second pāda at eight syllables; Madhva’s text reads ca cobhe, adding a second ca, which joins the vowels and keeps the count (corrections.json, variant).

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this mantra.

Morphology

भवतः
Present 3 du. of bhū, agreeing with the pair ब्रह्म च क्षत्रं च (उभे); the predicate ओदनः stays singular: the two together are one meal.
उपसेचनम्
From upa-sic ‘pour on’: what is poured over rice, a sauce or condiment.
इत्था
A Vedic adverb, ‘thus, in this way, truly’; Śaṅkara glosses it with ittham.

Anvaya

यस्य ब्रह्म च क्षत्रम् च उभे ओदनः भवतः, मृत्युः यस्य उपसेचनम्, सः यत्र [अस्ति तत्] इत्था कः वेद ॥

yasya brahma ca kṣatram ca ubhe odanaḥ bhavataḥ, mṛtyuḥ yasya upasecanam, saḥ yatra asti tat itthā kaḥ veda ||

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Two relative clauses with yasya: the first with a dual verb (ubhe bhavata odanaḥ), the second nominal (mṛtyur yasyopasecanam). The main clause is a rhetorical question, ka itthā veda, with its own relative clause yatra saḥ as object.

Ambiguities

What do brahman and kṣatra stand for?
Śaṅkara: the two classes that uphold all dharma and protect all beings, सर्वधर्मविधारके अपि सर्वप्राणभूते, yet both are only food to the self. Raṅgarāmānuja: they stand for the whole moving and unmoving world, कृत्स्नचराचरात्मकमिदं जगत्, and odana means what is to be destroyed, so the one described is the Supreme who withdraws all, निखिलचराचरसंहर्ता परमात्मा; he cites the Brahmasūtra on the ‘eater’ (1.2.9).
Who ‘knows thus’?
Śaṅkara: an ordinary mind, not equipped with the means just taught, cannot know it as one so equipped does: इत्थमेवं यथोक्तसाधनवानिवेत्यर्थः. Raṅgarāmānuja: who knows in what mode the Supreme abides — the question stresses how hard he is to know.

Metre

anuṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 16 + 16

Counted as written, 16 and 16 syllables; the hiatus ca ubhe gives the second pāda its eight.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Power and mortality

Priests and kings were the most powerful people the poets knew. The verse makes them a single bowl of rice. Any age that is impressed by its own powerful might read it as a reminder of scale. (A modern reflection.)
