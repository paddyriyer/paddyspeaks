Kaṭha Upaniṣad · Adhyāya 1 · Vallī 1 · Mantra 1 of 29

The sacrifice of everything

The story opens with a father giving away all he owns for the sake of a reward, and names his son, Naciketas.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Once, hoping for heaven, the son of Vājaśravas gave away everything he owned. He had a son called Naciketas.

7 Explain it to a beginner

The story begins with a sacrifice. A man known as the son of Vājaśravas performs a great ritual in which the sacrificer gives away all he owns, hoping to win heaven in return. In one sentence the Upaniṣad tells us what he did and why: he wanted the reward. In the next sentence it names his son, Naciketas, still a boy. Nothing yet has been said about death, the self or liberation. The commentators note that the story is told to honour the knowledge that will come: a dramatic frame helps the listener care about the teaching. The father’s motive matters, because the whole Upaniṣad will contrast what we give in order to gain something with what cannot be gained by giving at all.

An analogy Like a story that begins with a rich man emptying his house for a prize, and only then mentions, almost in passing, the child who will become its hero.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the mantra say?

A man called Vājaśravasa, wanting [a reward], gave away all he had; he had a son named Naciketas. The motive (uśan, ‘desiring’) is stated, its object is not.

Traditional Interpretation

Why does an Upaniṣad about the self begin with a story?

Śaṅkara’s first sentence on the mantra: तत्राख्यायिका विद्यास्तुत्यर्था — the story is there to praise the knowledge. In his introduction he defines the word upaniṣad itself: it is primarily the knowledge, and only secondarily the book — उपनिषच्छब्देन च व्याचिख्यासितग्रन्थप्रतिपाद्यवेद्यवस्तुविषया विद्या उच्यते, because that knowledge loosens and destroys the seed of transmigration in those who devote themselves to it.

Traditional Interpretation

What rite was it, and what did he want?

Śaṅkara names the rite: the Viśvajit, a sacrifice in which all is given away — विश्वजिता सर्वमेधेनेजे तत्फलं कामयमानः, he sacrificed with the Viśvajit, the all-giving, wanting its fruit. Raṅgarāmānuja draws a consequence from the word uśan: because the rite was performed for a desired result, the correctness of its gifts mattered — उशन् इत्यनेन कर्मणः काम्यत्वात् दक्षिणासाद्गुण्यम् आवश्यकमिति सूच्यते. That prepares the boy’s objection in the next mantras.

Traditional Interpretation

What does the Dvaita tradition add?

Madhva’s bhāṣya opens with verses he cites from other works. One names the father and his aim: उद्दालकः स्वर्गलोकं ददौ गाश्च निरिन्द्रियाः — Uddālaka, wanting heaven, gave away even cows that were past use. (The verse is reported as cited by Madhva.)

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Kaṭhopaniṣad-bhāṣya 1.1.1 and introduction

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The narrative serves the teaching: तत्राख्यायिका विद्यास्तुत्यर्था. The Upaniṣad is the knowledge of Brahman that is one’s inner self, and the book is called upaniṣad only secondarily: तस्माद्विद्यायां मुख्यया वृत्त्या उपनिषच्छब्दो वर्तते.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja, Prakāśikā 1.1.1

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Raṅgarāmānuja reads the scene ritually and closely: the sacrifice is a desire-prompted (kāmya) rite, so the adequacy of its gifts is essential — कर्मणः काम्यत्वात् दक्षिणासाद्गुण्यम् आवश्यकमिति सूच्यते.

Dvaita · Madhva, Kāṭhakopaniṣad-bhāṣya 1.1.1 (a verse he cites)

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The father is Uddālaka, who gave all for the sake of heaven: इच्छन् वाजश्रवोनप्ता ददौ सर्वस्वदक्षिणाम्. In the same place Madhva cites a Brahmasāra verse that reads the whole Naciketas teaching as meditation on Viṣṇu in the fire.

Where they disagree

At this point the schools differ only in emphasis: Śaṅkara on the story as praise of knowledge, Raṅgarāmānuja on the ritual logic of the gift, Madhva (through the verses he cites) on Uddālaka’s wish for heaven.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • उशन्ह uśanha → उशन् + ह

    consonant before h

    The final n of the participle stands unchanged before h; the base writes the two words without a space.

  • नचिकेता नाम naciketā nāma → नचिकेताः + नाम

    āḥ before a voiced consonant

    After ā the visarga is dropped before a voiced sound: P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि) and P. 8.3.22 (हलि सर्वेषाम्).

  • पुत्र आस putra āsa → पुत्रः + आस

    aḥ before a vowel other than a

    The visarga of aḥ becomes y before a vowel, P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि), and is then dropped, P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य); the hiatus stays.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • सर्ववेदसम् sarvavedasam

    karmadhāraya · सर्व + वेदस्

    Vigraha सर्वं वेदः

    all (his) wealth; vedas here means property, not the Veda

Morphology

उशन्
Present active participle of vaś, ‘to wish’, with the weak grade (u for va): Raṅgarāmānuja derives it from vaś with samprasāraṇa. It tells why the father gives: he wants the reward of the rite.
आस
Perfect of as, ‘to be’. Classical Sanskrit uses babhūva; the Veda keeps āsa (Raṅgarāmānuja: in the Veda the perfect may count as sārvadhātuka, P. 3.4.117 (छन्दस्युभयथा), so as is not replaced by bhū).

Anvaya

उशन् ह वै वाजश्रवसः सर्ववेदसम् ददौ । तस्य ह नचिकेताः नाम पुत्रः आस ॥

uśan ha vai vājaśravasaḥ sarvavedasam dadau | tasya ha naciketāḥ nāma putraḥ āsa ||

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Two short narrative sentences in the perfect tense, the tense of story. The participle uśan (‘desiring’) gives the motive before the verb; the object of desire is left unsaid. The second sentence introduces Naciketas as the son, the person on whom the story will turn.

Ambiguities

Who is Vājaśravasa?
The name means ‘son of Vājaśravas’; Śaṅkara allows that it is either a patronymic from a man famed for giving food or simply a name (रूढितो वा). Later the father is called Gautama (1.1.10) and Auddālaki Āruṇi (1.1.11); Madhva’s cited verse calls him Uddālaka. How these names relate is a question the text does not settle.
Prose or verse?
The first mantra is prose, in the style of a brāhmaṇa narrative, though its first line happens to count sixteen syllables.

Metre

prose · syllables counted as written: 16 + 13

Narrative prose in two sentences (16 and 13 syllables as written). The Kaṭha’s verses begin with 1.1.3.

Textual variations between witnesses

The witnesses differ here only in spelling (listed in section 14).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Wealth and consumption

A man gives away everything — but in order to receive something greater. Generosity calculated for a return is still a transaction, and the story will soon test whether what he gives is really worth giving. This is a modern reflection, not the text’s claim.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin When have you given something mainly for what you hoped to get back?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Can a gift made for a reward still be a gift? What would distinguish it from a purchase?

A contemplation exercise Notice one act of giving today, however small, and ask honestly what you wanted from it.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
