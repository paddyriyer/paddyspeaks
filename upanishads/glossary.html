Texts and their parts

आरण्यक āraṇyaka Āraṇyaka

‘Forest’ texts, dealing with ritual of special power and its inner meaning.

Several Upaniṣads are embedded in Āraṇyakas (the Aitareya, the Taittirīya). The Bṛhadāraṇyaka calls itself both an Āraṇyaka and an Upaniṣad.

See also Brāhmaṇa · Upaniṣad

भाष्य bhāṣya commentary

A commentary that explains a text word by word and argues for an interpretation.

The edition draws on four: Śaṅkara’s (Advaita), Vedānta Deśika’s (Viśiṣṭādvaita), Madhva’s (Dvaita) and Mahīdhara’s Vedadīpa on the Mādhyandina Saṃhitā.

See also Advaita, ‘non-duality’ · Viśiṣṭādvaita, ‘non-duality of the qualified’ · Dvaita, ‘duality’

ब्राह्मण brāhmaṇa Brāhmaṇa

Prose texts that explain the ritual and its meaning.

The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa belongs to the Śukla Yajurveda; its final book contains the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad. Not to be confused with brahman (the absolute) or brāhmaṇa (a member of the priestly class).

See also Veda · Āraṇyaka

काण्व kāṇva Kāṇva

One of the two recensions of the Śukla Yajurveda; the source of the eighteen-mantra Īśā.

The Kāṇva Saṃhitā’s fortieth chapter is the Īśā in eighteen mantras. The recension is followed today in parts of Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and Odisha.

See also branch, recension · Mādhyandina

माध्यन्दिन mādhyandina Mādhyandina

The other recension of the Śukla Yajurveda; its VS 40 has seventeen mantras in another order.

In the Mādhyandina chapter the sambhūti mantras come before the vidyā mantras, the prayer to Pūṣan is shorter, and it ends ‘om khaṃ brahma’. Mahīdhara’s commentary, used in this edition, follows this recension.

See also branch, recension · Kāṇva

मन्त्र mantra mantra

A verse or formula of the Saṃhitā; here, each of the Upaniṣad's eighteen units.

Because the Īśā belongs to a Saṃhitā, its units are called mantras, not ślokas. Śaṅkara begins by saying that these mantras are not used in ritual (कर्मस्वविनियुक्ताः): they reveal the Self.

See also Saṃhitā · seer

पञ्चब्रह्म pañcabrahma pañca-brahma, ‘the five brahmans’

The five mantras sadyojātam, vāmadevāya, aghorebhyaḥ, tatpuruṣāya and īśānaḥ (Mahānārāyaṇa 10.17–10.21), each addressed to one face of Śiva.

Bhāskara names the group and its use in worship and meditation (अथ पञ्चब्रह्माख्याः पञ्चानुवाका ईश्वराराधनादिषु उपासनादिषु च विनियुक्ताः, his anuvāka 43). Sāyaṇa assigns each mantra to one of the god’s five faces, beginning with the western one for sadyojātam (महादेवसंबन्धिषु पञ्चवक्त्रेषु मध्ये पश्चिमवक्त्रप्रतिपादकं मन्त्रमाह, his anuvāka 43); the anonymous commentator gives the northern face to vāmadeva and the southern to aghora. These are the commentators’ ritual assignments, not words of the mantras.

See also Rudra · mantra · worship, meditation · liṅga, ‘mark, sign’

प्रस्थानत्रय prasthānatraya the three foundations

The Upaniṣads, the Bhagavad Gītā and the Brahmasūtra, the three sources every Vedānta school comments on.

The Upaniṣads are śruti; the Gītā is smṛti that the tradition treats as their essence; the Brahmasūtra systematises their teaching. A Vedānta ācārya establishes a school by commenting on all three.

See also Vedānta · commentary

ऋषि ṛṣi seer

A seer to whom a Vedic mantra was revealed.

Mahīdhara records that the fortieth adhyāya was seen by Dadhyañc Ātharvaṇa. The word also occurs in the Īśā itself, in ekarṣi, ‘sole seer’ (mantra 16).

In the Taittirīya 1.7

In the Mahānārāyaṇa 10.8 · 10.12 · 10.35 · 10.40 · 10.41 · 10.73 · 10.79

See also śruti, ‘what is heard’ · mantra

शाखा śākhā branch, recension

A school that preserves its own version of a Veda.

The Śukla Yajurveda survives in two recensions, Kāṇva and Mādhyandina. Their fortieth chapters both contain the Īśā, with differences of order and wording; the eighteen-mantra text commented on by Śaṅkara, Madhva and Deśika is the Kāṇva.

See also Saṃhitā · Kāṇva · Mādhyandina

सामन् sāman sāman, chant

A Vedic chant: a verse set to melody, with added syllables (stobhas).

The knower of Taittirīya 3.10 ‘sits singing this sāman’: hā3vu hā3vu hā3vu, I am food … Śaṅkara: Brahman itself is the sāman, being the same in all (samatvāt).

In the Taittirīya 1.2 · 1.5 · 1.8 · 2.3 · 3.10

In the Mahānārāyaṇa 10.14 · 10.79

See also Veda · Om

संहिता saṃhitā Saṃhitā

The collection of a Veda's mantras, recited with sandhi and svara.

The word also names the continuous, sandhi-joined way of reciting, as against the pada-pāṭha (word by word). The Īśāvāsya is unusual among the principal Upaniṣads in being part of a Saṃhitā: chapter 40 of the Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā of the Śukla Yajurveda.

See also pada-pāṭha · svara, Vedic accent · branch, recension

स्मृति smṛti smṛti, ‘what is remembered’

Authoritative tradition that depends on śruti: law books, epics, Purāṇas, the Gītā.

Smṛti is held to be authoritative because it rests on śruti. The Bhagavad Gītā is smṛti in this classification, though it is treated as one of the three foundations of Vedānta.

See also śruti, ‘what is heard’ · the three foundations

श्रुति śruti śruti, ‘what is heard’

Revealed scripture: the Vedas, including the Upaniṣads.

In the tradition the Vedas are not composed by any person but ‘heard’ by seers (ṛṣi) and transmitted orally. Śruti is authoritative in itself; everything else is judged by agreement with it. Historians date the Vedic texts by language and content; that is a different kind of claim and does not settle the traditional one.

See also smṛti, ‘what is remembered’ · Saṃhitā · seer

त्रिसुपर्ण trisuparṇa trisuparṇa, the ‘three fine-winged’ passages

The name of three passages of the Mahānārāyaṇa (10.38–10.40), each ending with the same closing words about those who recite the trisuparṇa.

Bhāskara calls them three anuvākas to be recited for the destruction of all sin, known and unknown (अज्ञातप्रज्ञातसमस्तपापक्षयार्थं जप्यास् त्रिसुपर्णाख्यास् त्रयोऽनुवाकाः, on his anuvāka 48), and takes the fine-winged bird of the name as Om. Sāyaṇa (his 48) says the deity of the trisuparṇa is the supreme self named by Om (प्रणवप्रतिपाद्यः परमात्मैव त्रिसुपर्णमन्त्रस्य देवतेत्यर्थः). Both readings are reported as the commentators’ own.

In the Mahānārāyaṇa 10.38 · 10.39 · 10.40

See also Om · Brahman · mantra

उपनिषद् upaniṣad Upaniṣad

The concluding, philosophical portion of a Vedic school's literature; a text of that kind.

Etymologically upa + ni + √sad, ‘to sit down near’. Śaṅkara, introducing his Kaṭha commentary, derives it from √sad in the senses of destroying, reaching and loosening (सदेर्धातोर्विशरणगत्यवसादनार्थस्योपनिपूर्वस्य क्विप्प्रत्ययान्तस्य रूपमिदम् ‘उपनिषत्’ इति): the word names first the knowledge that destroys ignorance and leads to Brahman, and the book only because it serves that knowledge. Modern scholars read it as ‘connection, equivalence’ — the hidden correspondences the texts teach — or ‘secret teaching’. The accounts answer different questions — what the word is made of, what it means to the tradition, how it was used — and are best kept apart.

See also śruti, ‘what is heard’ · Vedānta · Āraṇyaka

वेद veda Veda

‘Knowledge’; the four collections Ṛg, Yajus, Sāman and Atharvan with their layers of text.

Each Veda is preserved by schools (śākhā) and comprises Saṃhitā (mantras), Brāhmaṇa (ritual exegesis), Āraṇyaka (‘forest’ texts) and Upaniṣad. The layers overlap: the Īśā is itself the fortieth chapter of a Saṃhitā.

See also Saṃhitā · Brāhmaṇa · Āraṇyaka · Upaniṣad · branch, recension

वेदाङ्ग vedāṅga vedāṅga, auxiliary science of the Veda

The six disciplines that serve the reading of the Veda: śikṣā, kalpa, vyākaraṇa, nirukta, chandas, jyotiṣa.

The Muṇḍaka lists them with the four Vedas as ‘lower’ knowledge. Kūranārāyaṇa calls śikṣā ‘the fifth limb’ of the Veda in his comment on Taittirīya 1.2.

See also śikṣā, the science of pronunciation · Veda

वेदान्त vedānta Vedānta

‘The end of the Veda’: the Upaniṣads, and the schools of thought that interpret them.

‘End’ in two senses: the last part of the Vedic corpus, and its culmination. As a school of philosophy Vedānta interprets the Upaniṣads, the Gītā and the Brahmasūtra; its principal schools differ on how Brahman, the self and the world are related.

See also the three foundations · Advaita, ‘non-duality’ · Viśiṣṭādvaita, ‘non-duality of the qualified’ · Dvaita, ‘duality’

Schools of Vedānta

अद्वैत advaita Advaita, ‘non-duality’

The school of Śaṅkara: the Self is Brahman, and the world of many is not finally real apart from it.

For Advaita the self (ātman) and Brahman are identical; individuality and the world of distinctions are due to ignorance (avidyā) and are removed by knowledge. Liberation is knowing what one already is.

See also Viśiṣṭādvaita, ‘non-duality of the qualified’ · Dvaita, ‘duality’ · avidyā · Brahman · self, Self

द्वैत dvaita Dvaita, ‘duality’

The school of Madhva: the Lord (Viṣṇu), selves and matter are eternally distinct.

Madhva teaches five real differences (Lord and self, Lord and matter, self and self, self and matter, matter and matter) and the complete dependence of everything on the Lord. His commentary on the Īśā reads it as a hymn to Hari.

See also Advaita, ‘non-duality’ · Viśiṣṭādvaita, ‘non-duality of the qualified’ · the Lord

विशिष्टाद्वैत viśiṣṭādvaita Viśiṣṭādvaita, ‘non-duality of the qualified’

The school of Rāmānuja and Vedānta Deśika: Brahman is one, qualified by souls and matter, which are real and form his body.

The Lord (Nārāyaṇa) is the inner controller of all; selves and the world are real, distinct from him and inseparable from him, as a body is from its self. Rāmānuja wrote no commentary on the Īśā; the school’s commentary on it is Vedānta Deśika’s.

See also Advaita, ‘non-duality’ · Dvaita, ‘duality’ · the Lord

Concepts

आचार्य ācārya ācārya, teacher

The teacher who imparts the Veda and its meaning.

In the great joining of learning (Taittirīya 1.3) the teacher is the prior form, the pupil the latter, knowledge the junction and teaching the connector.

In the Taittirīya 1.3 · 1.11

See also brahmacārin, Vedic student · vidyā

अध्यात्मयोग adhyātmayoga inward yoga

Turning the mind from objects and settling it in the self (Kaṭha 1.2.12).

Śaṅkara defines it in his comment on Kaṭha 1.2.12: withdrawing the mind from the objects of the senses and settling it in the self. By its attainment the wise one knows the God hidden in the cave and leaves joy and sorrow. Raṅgarāmānuja takes the attainment as knowledge of the individual self, which leads to knowledge of the supreme.

See also the cave · the wise · self, Self

अदिति aditi Aditi, ‘the eater’

In Kaṭha 2.1.7, the one made of the deities who arises with breath and dwells in the cave; the commentators hear the name as ‘eater’.

Śaṅkara: Hiraṇyagarbha, who ‘eats’ sound and the other objects, शब्दादीनामदनात् अदितिः. Raṅgarāmānuja, quoting the Bhāṣyakāra: the individual self, who eats the fruits of action, कर्मफलानि अत्तीति. Madhva, in a verse he cites: Viṣṇu, अदनादितिर्विष्णुर्यः प्राणसहितः स्थितः.

In the Kaṭha 2.1.7

In the Mahānārāyaṇa 10.28

अघमर्षण aghamarṣaṇa aghamarṣaṇa, ‘the effacing of wrong’

The three-verse hymn ṛtaṃ ca satyaṃ ca (‘right order and truth were born from blazing heat’), and the name of the one it addresses, the remover of wrong.

In the Mahānārāyaṇa (10.1.13–10.1.14) the hymn tells how right order, truth, night, the ocean and the year were born, and Varuṇa is called aghamarṣaṇa, the remover of wrong. The anonymous commentator says it is recited under water with breath restraint (जले निमग्नस्य प्राणायामार्थमघमर्षणसूक्तमाह, on 10.1); Bhāskara places three bathing verses after it (अघमर्षणोत्तरकालम् उत्तराभिस्तिसृभिः स्नायात्, his anuvāka 1). This is the commentators’ account of its use; no effect of reciting it is claimed.

In the Mahānārāyaṇa 10.1

अग्निहोत्र agnihotra agnihotra, the daily fire offering

The offering into the household’s sacred fires every evening and morning; in Mahānārāyaṇa 10.78–10.80 one of the ‘highest’ things, and an image for the knower’s whole life.

Mahānārāyaṇa 10.79 calls it the discharge of the household and the beginning of all the sacrifices and soma rites. The anonymous commentator reads ‘discharge’ as the price that redeems the house, गृहाणां निष्कृतिः क्रयसाधनं मूल्यम; Bhāskara as the cleansing of the household’s faults, गृहसम्बन्धिनां पापानां शोधनं. In 10.80 the knower’s life itself is the agnihotra that lasts until old age and death.

In the Taittirīya 1.8 · 1.9

अजा / अज ajā / aja the unborn (she and he)

The riddle-verse ajām ekām (10.12): one unborn female, red, white and black, and two unborn males, one who clings to her and one who leaves her.

The words also mean she-goat and he-goat. Sāyaṇa: the unborn female is māyā as root nature (न जायत इत्यजा मूलप्रकृतिरूपा माया), and the unborn male is the individual self (न जायत इत्यजो जीवः), attached or dispassionate. Bhāskara: she is prakṛti (अजाम् अजननशीलाम् अप्रसवधर्मिणीं प्रकृतिम्), her colours the three guṇas (लोहितं रजः शुक्लं सत्त्वं कृष्णं तम). The verse also occurs in the Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad.

In the Kaṭha 1.2.18 · 2.2.1

अक्षर akṣara akṣara, the imperishable; syllable

Both ‘the imperishable’ and ‘a syllable’; in Kaṭha 1.2.16 the syllable Om, called Brahman and the highest.

Kaṭha 1.2.16 uses both senses at once: ‘this syllable is Brahman, this syllable is the highest’. Śaṅkara: the syllable is the symbol of both the lower and the higher Brahman. Raṅgarāmānuja: it is called Brahman because it is the means of reaching Brahman. A verse Madhva cites names the imperishable as Viṣṇu.

आलम्बन ālambana support (of meditation)

What one leans on; in Kaṭha 1.2.17 the syllable Om, ‘the best support, the highest support’.

From ā-√lamb ‘lean on’. Śaṅkara: Om is the best of the supports for reaching Brahman, higher or lower. Raṅgarāmānuja: meditation resting on Om is the best meditation. In the verse Madhva cites, the support is the Lord, the support of all.

अलिङ्ग aliṅga aliṅga, without a mark

Having no mark by which it could be known; said of the person beyond the unmanifest (Kaṭha 2.3.8).

A liṅga is the sign by which something is known. Śaṅkara: the mark is the intellect and the rest, लिङ्ग्यते गम्यते येन तल्लिङ्गं बुद्ध्यादि, which the puruṣa does not have, being free of every quality of saṃsāra, सर्वसंसारधर्मवर्जित इत्येतत्. Raṅgarāmānuja reads it as ‘not to be known by a mark’.

In the Kaṭha 2.3.8

आनन्द ānanda bliss

Bliss, joy; in the Taittirīya, the nature of Brahman and the innermost of the five selves.

The Brahmānandavallī measures it in steps of a hundred, from the happiest human life to Brahman (2.8), and ends ‘knowing the bliss of Brahman, one fears nothing’ (2.9). For Śaṅkara worldly joy is a fragment of Brahman’s bliss, which is natural and non-dual; for the Viśiṣṭādvaita commentators it is boundless; Madhva holds that bliss differs even among the released.

आनन्दमय ānandamaya the self made of bliss

अङ्गुष्ठमात्र aṅguṣṭhamātra aṅguṣṭhamātra, thumb-sized

The person ‘of the measure of a thumb’ dwelling in the heart (Kaṭha 2.1.12–13, 2.3.17).

A measure taken from the heart in which the self is meditated on, not a size. अन्न anna food

Food; the first ‘self’ of the Taittirīya is the body ‘made of the essence of food’.

The verse of Taittirīya 2.2 explains the word as what is eaten and what eats (adyate, atti). Those who meditate on food as Brahman obtain all food. For Madhva, ‘food’ names Viṣṇu, who eats all the worlds.

अन्तरात्मन् antarātman the inner self

The self within; in Kaṭha 2.2.9–12 the ‘inner self of all beings’ (sarvabhūtāntarātmā).

Śaṅkara: the self within all beings, सर्वेषां भूतानामभ्यन्तर आत्मा, which takes the shape of each body yet stays unchanged outside them. Raṅgarāmānuja: the supreme Self entering each thing as its inner ruler, प्रतिवस्तु प्रतिसंक्रान्तान्तर्यामिविग्रहः. Madhva: the one independent Lord dwelling within all souls, एकस्स्वतन्त्रो नान्योऽस्ति सर्वजीवान्तरस्थितः.

See also self, Self · individual self · the Lord

अरणि araṇi araṇi, the fire-stick

Bhāskara reads the brahman of that passage as the supreme one beyond the world (ब्रह्मणः परस्य प्रपञ्चोत्तीर्णस्य सायुज्यं सयुग्भावं समानलोकतां च आप्नोति). The term recurs at 10.33 and 10.80.

लक्षण lakṣaṇa defining mark, definition

The mark by which a thing is known and set apart from everything else.

Varuṇa’s ‘that from which beings are born, by which they live, into which they return’ (Taittirīya 3.1) is the classic lakṣaṇa of Brahman, the basis of Brahmasūtra 1.1.2. Raṅgarāmānuja, Rāmānanda and Gopālānanda insist the three together make the definition; Raṅgarāmānuja contrasts this mark of Brahman as cause of the world with satyaṃ jñānam anantam, which marks Brahman in itself.

प्रतीक pratīka pratīka, a passage cited by its opening words

The Mahānārāyaṇa cites whole passages this way, often closing with a count (‘… ityaṣṭau’, ‘the eight beginning …’).
