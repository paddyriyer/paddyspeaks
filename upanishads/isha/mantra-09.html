Īśāvāsya Upaniṣad · Mantra 9 of 18

Into blind darkness — and into greater darkness

A double warning: against devotion to avidyā alone, and, more sharply, against devotion to vidyā alone.

5 Faithful English translation

Into blind darkness they enter who worship (attend upon) avidyā. Into a darkness greater, as it were, than that [enter] they who delight in vidyā.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Those who devote themselves to avidyā enter blind darkness; into a darkness greater still go those who delight in vidyā alone.

Key concepts अविद्या avidyā विद्या vidyā उपासना worship, meditation तमस् darkness कर्मन् action, ritual work समुच्चय combination (of knowledge and action)

7 Explain it to a beginner

This verse is a puzzle on purpose. It says that people who give themselves to one thing — the text calls it ‘not-knowledge’ — walk into a blinding darkness. Then it says something stranger: people who give themselves only to ‘knowledge’ walk into an even deeper darkness. That cannot simply mean that knowing things is bad. The old teachers read it as a warning against lopsidedness. One group only does — rituals, duties, busy activity — and never asks what it all means. The other group only thinks, and drops the work and responsibility that would have steadied and prepared them. The verse seems to say that the second mistake is the more dangerous, perhaps because it feels like progress. The next two verses explain how the two belong together.

An analogy A pilot who only flies and never studies is dangerous; a pilot who studies the manuals but has stopped flying is more dangerous still, because he believes he is ready.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the mantra itself say?

Two groups of people and two darknesses. Those who ‘worship’ or ‘attend upon’ (upāsate) avidyā enter ‘blind darkness’ (andhaṃ tamaḥ). Those who ‘delight in’ (ratāḥ) vidyā enter a darkness ‘greater, as it were, than that’. The mantra does not say what vidyā and avidyā are, and does not yet say what the right course is; that is given in mantra 11. Read alone, it condemns both one-sided devotions and the second more than the first. It also changes the verb: the first group ‘worships’ avidyā, the second ‘delights in’ vidyā.

Traditional Interpretation

Why does Śaṅkara think this mantra is addressed to a particular reader?

In a long introduction Śaṅkara divides the Upaniṣad by its readers. Mantra 1 taught the path of knowledge to the renouncer, mantra 2 the path of works to one who wishes to live; mantras 3–8 taught the Self to renouncers. Mantras 9–14 are for the other reader: ये तु कामिनः कर्मनिष्ठाः कर्म कुर्वन्त एव जिजीविषवः, तेभ्य इदमुच्यते — ‘to those who have desires, who are committed to works and wish to live doing works, this is said’. The knowledge of the oneness of the Self described in mantra 7 cannot be combined with anything, and no clear-minded person would try: तन्न केनचित्कर्मणा ज्ञानान्तरेण वा ह्यमूढः समुच्चिचीषति. So vidyā here is not knowledge of the Self but knowledge of deities: देवताविषयं ज्ञानं कर्मसम्बन्धित्वेनोपन्यस्तं न परमात्मज्ञानम्.

Traditional Interpretation

If vidyā and avidyā are both good, why are both condemned?

Śaṅkara’s answer is that each is condemned only when practised alone, in order to recommend practising them together: तयोर्ज्ञानकर्मणोरिहैकैकानुष्ठाननिन्दा समुच्चिचीषया, न निन्दापरैव एकैकस्य — ‘the censure of practising either one alone is for the sake of combining them, not a censure of each for its own sake’ — because each has its own fruit, and न हि शास्त्रविहितं किञ्चिदकर्तव्यतामियात्, ‘nothing enjoined by scripture can become something not to be done’. Mahīdhara repeats the point: विद्याविद्ययोः समुच्चिचीषया प्रत्येकं निन्दोच्यते. Deśika also reads the mantra as condemning those who rely on works alone and those who rely on knowledge alone (केवलकर्मावलम्बिनः केवलविद्यावलम्बिनश्च), but he draws a different conclusion: liberation comes by knowledge supported by the duties of one’s station (वर्णाश्रमधर्मानुगृहीतया विद्ययैव निश्श्रेयसावाप्तिः).

Traditional Interpretation

What is the ‘darkness’?

Śaṅkara glosses ‘blind darkness’ only as अदर्शनात्मकं तमः, darkness that consists in not seeing. Mahīdhara takes it as ignorance experienced as the continuing round of births: तमोऽज्ञानं प्रविशन्ति । संसारपरम्परामनुभवन्तीत्यर्थः. Deśika gives two options, ignorance or the darkness of hell (अज्ञानम्, त्रिवर्गाभिषङ्गात् नान्तरीयकं नारकं तमो वा), and supports his reading of the first half with the Muṇḍaka’s image of rituals as unsteady boats (Muṇḍaka 1.2.7). The image of blind darkness has already appeared in mantra 3, of the worlds reached by ‘slayers of the self’.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

How do the two Saṃhitā witnesses and the pada-pāṭha bear on the reading?

Every witness has the same words. The Kāṇva and Mādhyandina Saṃhitās write the preverb apart (प्र वि॑शन्ति), as Vedic allows; the Upaniṣad witnesses write प्रविशन्ति. The Mādhyandina pada-pāṭha divides ये। अवि॑द्याम्। उ॒पास॑त॒ इत्यु॑प॒ऽआस॑ते। and treats u as a separate, uncontracted particle (ये। ऊँ॒ऽइत्यूँ॑।), which confirms the division ye u rather than a single word. In the Mādhyandina recension this mantra is VS 40.12 and comes after the three mantras on sambhūti; in the Kāṇva text it comes before them. The same verse stands word for word in Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.10.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, introduction to mantra 9 and bhāṣya on 9

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

avidyā is ritual action, so called because it is other than knowledge and opposed to it: विद्याया अन्या अविद्या कर्मेत्यर्थः, कर्मणो विद्याविरोधित्वात् — specifically the agnihotra and other rites, performed alone. vidyā is knowledge of deities: those who abandon ritual and delight in it alone fare worse (कर्म हित्वा ये उ ये तु विद्यायामेव देवताज्ञाने एव रताः). The mantra is addressed to people of desire committed to works, not to seekers of the Self, whose knowledge cannot be combined with anything.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Vedānta Deśika, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantra 9

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

avidyā is mere action without knowledge (ज्ञानविधुरकर्ममात्रम्), and he supports the word by a remembered verse that calls action avidyā (अविद्या कर्मसंज्ञाऽन्या तृतीया शक्तिरिष्यते); those devoted to it are attached to pleasure and power. The second group abandons the duties proper to their station and delights in knowledge alone (स्वाधिकारोचितकर्मपरित्यागेन विद्यामात्रे रताः). vidyā here is knowledge of the Supreme Self, but cut off from the works that should accompany it.

Dvaita · Madhva, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantras 9–11 (one comment)

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Madhva reads the pair in terms of worshipping the Lord rightly or wrongly. Those who worship otherwise than as he is go to blind darkness: अन्यथोपासका ये तु तमोऽन्धं यान्त्यसंशयम्; and those who do not censure them go to a still greater darkness: ततोऽधिकमिव व्यक्तं यान्ति तेषामनिन्दकाः. The digital text of his comment is abridged here, and it does not state word for word how vidyā is to be construed in this mantra.

Where they disagree

All four commentators deny that the mantra condemns knowledge as such, and all take avidyā as something other than mere ignorance. They differ on what vidyā is (knowledge of deities for Śaṅkara; knowledge of the Lord, but without works, for Deśika; for Madhva the issue is right and wrong worship of the Lord) and on whom the mantra addresses (only people of desire, for Śaṅkara; every seeker, for the theistic commentators).

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • अन्धं तमः andhaṃ tamaḥ → अन्धम् + तमः

    consonant sandhi (m → anusvāra)

    A word-final m becomes anusvāra before a consonant: P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः). The Mādhyandina witness writes the nasal of the following class, अन्धन्तमः.

  • येऽविद्यामुपासते ye'vidyāmupāsate → ये + अविद्याम् + उपासते

    vowel sandhi (pūrvarūpa) and m before a vowel

    A word-final e before a short a absorbs it, the a being marked by avagraha: P. 6.1.109 (एङः पदान्तादति). Advaita Sharada prints the junction open, ये अविद्याम्, as a reading aid. avidyām + upāsate: m before a vowel is simply written with it.

  • ततो भूय इव tato bhūya iva → ततः + भूयः + इव

    visarga sandhi

    -aḥ before a voiced consonant becomes -o (tataḥ + bhūyaḥ → tato bhūyaḥ). Before a vowel other than a, the visarga of -aḥ is lost and the hiatus stands (bhūyaḥ + iva → bhūya iva); the visarga first becomes y (P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि)), which may be dropped (P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य)).

  • तमो य उ tamo ya u → तमः + ये + उ

    visarga sandhi and vowel sandhi (ayādi with loss of y)

    tamaḥ + ye → tamo ye (-aḥ before a voiced sound). ye + u: final e before a vowel becomes ay (P. 6.1.78 (एचोऽयवायावः)), and the y is dropped (P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य)), leaving ya u in hiatus. The Mādhyandina pada-pāṭha confirms the words: ये। ऊँ॒ऽइत्यूँ॑।.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this mantra.

Morphology

अविद्याम्
a-vidyā, ‘not-vidyā’. A negative compound (nañ) that functions as a single feminine stem. Grammatically the negative can mean ‘the absence of vidyā’ or ‘what is other than vidyā’; the commentators here take the second: something positive — action — that is other than knowledge (विद्याया अन्या अविद्या कर्मेत्यर्थः, Śaṅkara).
उपासते
upa + √ās, ‘to sit near’, hence ‘to attend upon, wait on, worship’; middle (ātmanepada) present, 3rd plural. The verb is used of sustained devotion or meditation (upāsanā). Śaṅkara takes it here in the wider sense of ‘perform, being intent on’.
प्रविशन्ति
pra + √viś, ‘enter’. The two Saṃhitā witnesses write the preverb apart, प्र वि॑शन्ति — the older, separable position of a Vedic preverb; the Upaniṣad witnesses write the verb as one word, and the padaccheda follows them.
भूयः
bhūyas, the comparative of bahu ‘much’ (P. 6.4.158 (बहोर्लोपो भू च बहोः) replaces bahu by bhū before the comparative suffix). Neuter accusative, agreeing with the second tamaḥ: ‘a greater darkness’. tataḥ gives the standard of comparison.
रताः
Past participle of √ram ‘to delight’, used actively (‘those who have delighted in’, ‘devoted to’); the copula is understood. It takes the locative of what one delights in: vidyāyām.

Anvaya

ये अविद्याम् उपासते [ते] अन्धम् तमः प्रविशन्ति । ये उ विद्यायाम् रताः ते ततः भूयः इव तमः [प्रविशन्ति] ।

ye avidyām upāsate te andham tamaḥ praviśanti | ye u vidyāyām ratāḥ te tataḥ bhūyaḥ iva tamaḥ praviśanti |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Two parallel relative constructions. In the first the main clause comes first (‘they enter blind darkness’) and the relative clause second (‘who worship avidyā’), with the correlative ‘they’ understood. In the second the order is main clause (‘they [enter] a darkness greater, as it were, than that’) and relative clause (‘who delight in vidyā’), with the verb praviśanti carried over from the first half. The particle u sets the second group against the first. The comparison tataḥ bhūyaḥ makes the second warning the stronger one.

Ambiguities

What are vidyā and avidyā here?
The words are not defined in the mantra. Taken in the everyday sense — ‘knowledge’ and ‘ignorance’ — the mantra says that those devoted to knowledge fare worse than the ignorant, which every commentator rejects. Śaṅkara and Mahīdhara take avidyā as ritual action and vidyā as knowledge (for Śaṅkara, meditative knowledge of deities); Deśika takes them as works without knowledge and knowledge without works; Madhva reads the pair in terms of wrong and right worship of the Lord. See Study IV.
Why is the second darkness greater?
The mantra gives no reason. Śaṅkara: because those who abandon ritual for knowledge of deities alone lose what ritual would have given (कर्म हित्वा). Mahīdhara: without ritual the mind is not purified, so knowledge does not arise (कर्माकरणे प्रत्यवायोत्पत्यान्तःकरणशुद्ध्यभावेन ज्ञानानुदयादिति भावः). Deśika: they abandon the duties proper to their station (स्वाधिकारोचितकर्मपरित्यागेन).
‘as it were’ (iva)
iva may soften the comparison (‘greater, as it were’) or, as Deśika takes it, indicate that the degree of that darkness cannot be measured. It does not make the second darkness unreal.

Metre

anuṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 15 + 16

Counted as written the half-lines have 15 and 16 syllables. The first half-line is one short because ye + avidyām is written with the a absorbed (येऽविद्याम्); read with the hiatus ye avidyām, as Advaita Sharada prints it and as the Mādhyandina pada-pāṭha divides it, it has 16, the regular anuṣṭubh count. Restoring an absorbed a in this way is common in Vedic metre.

Textual variations between witnesses

The witnesses differ here only in spelling (listed in section 14).

11 Connections with other scriptures

  • Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.4.10 Direct textual parallel

    अन्धं तमः प्रविशन्ति येऽविद्यामुपासते

    andhaṃ tamaḥ praviśanti ye'vidyāmupāsate

    The same verse, word for word, in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka (also of the Śukla Yajurveda). There it is followed at once by the verse ‘anandā nāma te lokāḥ …’ (4.4.11), the counterpart of Īśā 3, so the two verses that the Īśā keeps apart stand together in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka, in the reverse order.

  • Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad 1.2.7 Doctrinal comparison

    प्लवा ह्येते अदृढा यज्ञरूपा

    plavā hyete adṛḍhā yajñarūpā

    ‘Unsteady boats are these forms of sacrifice’: the fools who rejoice in ritual alone come again to old age and death. Deśika cites this verse in his comment on the first half of the mantra. A doctrinal comparison: both texts warn against ritual relied on by itself.

  • Bhagavad Gītā 2.42 Modern thematic association

    वेदवादरताः

    vedavādaratāḥ

    The Gītā’s ‘those who delight in the words of the Veda’, who say ‘there is nothing else’. The same participle rata, ‘delighting in’, for a one-sided devotion. A thematic association, not a parallel of doctrine.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Ego and self-image

The second warning is the uncomfortable one. It is aimed at people who have moved past mere routine and now ‘delight in’ ideas — the reader of many books on mindfulness who no longer keeps a promise, the strategist who has stopped doing the work, the seeker who looks down on duties as beneath a spiritual life. The mantra suggests that this is a deeper darkness than unthinking routine, perhaps because it is harder to see: the routine-bound person at least knows that something is missing. Whatever vidyā meant to the Upaniṣad’s first hearers, the structure of the warning still applies wherever understanding becomes a reason to stop acting well. This is a reflection on the teaching, not part of it.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Is there something you only ever ‘do’ without asking why — and something you only ever think about without doing?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Why might devotion to knowledge alone be a greater darkness than devotion to action alone? What does that imply about what the Upaniṣad means by ‘knowledge’ here?

A contemplation exercise Choose one daily duty you do mechanically. For a week, before doing it, take one breath and name to yourself what it is for; and choose one idea you value, and for the same week do one small thing each day that acts on it.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.

Corrections to the base witness

  • य उविद्यायां → य उ विद्यायां (orthographic) — A missing space in G; उ is a separate particle (AS, K, Mv, VD).

Further notes

  • Śaṅkara’s introduction to mantra 9 is the longest in his commentary: it divides the Upaniṣad between renouncers (mantras 1, 3–8) and people committed to works (mantras 2, 9–14), and cites the Bṛhadāraṇyaka (1.4.17, 1.5.16, 4.4.22) and Śvetāśvatara 6.21 for that division.
  • Mahīdhara comments on this mantra as VS 40.12 (Mādhyandina order).

Uncertainties recorded

  • interpretation The edition does not translate vidyā and avidyā in this mantra: every rendering (‘knowledge/ignorance’, ‘meditation/ritual’, ‘right/wrong worship’) already chooses a commentary.
  • uncertain Madhva’s comment on mantras 9–11 is abridged in the digital text used (it breaks off with ‘…’); his construction of vidyāyāṃ ratāḥ should be checked against a printed edition of his bhāṣya.
