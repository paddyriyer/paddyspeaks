Īśāvāsya Upaniṣad · Mantra 6 of 18

One who sees all beings in the self does not shrink from anything

Seeing all beings in the self and the self in all beings, and the freedom from aversion that follows.

7 Explain it to a beginner

Most of our dislike of others begins with a line we draw: here am I, and over there is someone different, strange, perhaps threatening. This verse describes a person for whom that line has faded. Such a person looks at every living thing and finds it held within their own deepest self; and looks into their own self and finds it present in every living thing. The verse then says what follows: that person does not recoil from anyone or anything. Disgust and contempt need distance; there must be an ‘other’ to push away. When the other is seen as part of oneself, the push has nowhere to go. The verse does not tell us to force ourselves to like everyone. It says that a certain way of seeing, once it is real, makes aversion fall away by itself.

An analogy Your left hand does not feel disgust when your right hand is hurt and dirty; it simply reaches over to help, because both belong to one body.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the mantra itself say?

It describes a person (yas tu, ‘but whoever’) by what that person sees — ‘all beings in the self alone’ and ‘the self in all beings’ — and states one consequence: ‘from that, he does not shrink away’ (tato na vijugupsate). It is a description, not a command; the only verb of seeing is anupaśyati, which carries the sense of seeing along, or again and again. The mantra does not define the self, nor say whether the seeing is a practice or an achievement.

Traditional Interpretation

How does Śaṅkara read the two directions of seeing?

For Śaṅkara the seer is a renunciant seeking liberation, and both directions express one knowledge. He sees all beings, from the unmanifest down to the motionless, in the self, and none apart from it (आत्मव्यतिरिक्तानि न पश्यतीत्यर्थः); and he sees in all beings the same self, his own, the witness of every cognition, without distinction (सर्वभूतेषु चात्मानं निर्विशेषं). The absence of aversion is not a further command but a description of what follows naturally: प्राप्तस्यैवानुवादोऽयम् — ‘this restates what is already the case’. His reason: सर्वा हि घृणा आत्मनोऽन्यद्दुष्टं पश्यतो भवति — all disgust arises in one who sees something other than the self as bad; for one who sees only the utterly pure self, there is no other thing to give rise to it.

Traditional Interpretation

How does Deśika read it?

Deśika continues his reading of mantra 1: everything has Brahman as its self. Here ātman means the inner self of all, the Lord, and the restrictive eva means that the beings, though supported by the elements, rest finally in the Supreme Self (परमात्मन्येव स्थितमित्येवकाराभिप्रायः). ‘The self in all beings’ only says that he pervades them, for he is not supported by them (व्याप्तिमात्रपरम् । तस्य तैर्धार्यत्वाभावात्). Because the knower sees everything as the Lord’s own glory, he censures nothing: क्वचिदपि निन्दां न करोतीत्यर्थः.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What is the shrinking away that ends?

The Kāṇva verb vi-jugupsate is built on gup, ‘to guard’, with a suffix (P. 3.1.5 (गुप्तिज्किद्भ्यः सन्)) that gives it the sense ‘to shrink from, recoil, despise’. The commentators draw three senses from it: aversion or disgust (Śaṅkara), censure (Deśika) and, keeping the root’s sense of guarding, the wish to protect oneself from fear (Madhva’s cited verse: यः पश्येत् स भयाभावान्नात्मानं गोप्तुमिच्छति). The Mādhyandina recension reads a different verb, vi cikitsati, ‘doubts’, and Mahīdhara explains: आत्मनोऽन्यत्पश्यतः सर्वः संदेहो भवत्यात्मानमेवात्यन्तं शुद्धं निरन्तरं पश्यतो न संदेहावकाश इति भावः — all doubt arises in one who sees something other than the self. The two recensions end the same thought differently: freedom from aversion, or freedom from doubt.

Comparative Vedānta

What is the relation of the self to Brahman here?

The mantra does not use the word brahman. Śaṅkara’s reading makes ‘the self’ of the seer and ‘the self in all beings’ one and the same, so the verse becomes a statement of non-duality. Deśika and Madhva distinguish the seer’s self from the Self that indwells all beings; for them the verse describes seeing the Lord everywhere, and everything as resting in him. The grammar — one word ātman in both phrases — allows either; the decision is made by the rest of each commentator’s system.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantra 6

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The self seen in all beings is the seer’s own self, pure consciousness without qualities: अहं सर्वप्रत्ययसाक्षिभूतश्चेतयिता केवलो निर्गुणोऽनेनैव स्वरूपेणाव्यक्तादीनां स्थावरान्तानामहमेवात्मेति. Aversion presupposes something other than the self; where nothing else is seen, it cannot arise.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Vedānta Deśika, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantra 6

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The self here is the inner self of all, the Lord (सर्वान्तरात्मविषयः); all beings rest in him and he pervades them. Seeing everything as having Brahman for its self, the knower finds nothing to censure, as one does not censure one’s own glory (स्वात्मविभूतिन्यायात्).

Dvaita · Madhva, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantra 6

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Madhva comments only with a verse he cites as śruti of Saukarāyaṇa: the one who sees the all-pervading Paramātman, and all in the Paramātman, has no fear and so does not wish to guard himself (सर्वगं परमात्मानं सर्वं च परमात्मनि । यः पश्येत् स भयाभावान्नात्मानं गोप्तुमिच्छति). The seer and the Paramātman remain distinct. The verse is reported as cited by Madhva; its source has not been verified.

Where they disagree

All three agree that the knower sees the self in all and all in the self, and that a negative state ends. They disagree on whose self it is — the seer’s own, identical with all (Śaṅkara), or the Lord as the inner self of all (Deśika, Madhva) — and on what ends: aversion (Śaṅkara), censure (Deśika) or the fear that makes one guard oneself (Madhva).

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • यस्तु yastu → यः + तु

    visarga sandhi

    A visarga before t becomes s: P. 8.3.34 (विसर्जनीयस्य सः).

  • भूतान्यात्मन्येवानुपश्यति bhūtānyātmanyevānupaśyati → भूतानि + आत्मनि + एव + अनुपश्यति

    vowel sandhi (yaṇ, twice; then savarṇa-dīrgha)

    Final i before an unlike vowel becomes y (P. 6.1.77 (इको यणचि)): bhūtāni ātmani → bhūtāny ātmani, ātmani eva → ātmany eva. Then a + a → ā (P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः)): eva anupaśyati → evānupaśyati. Advaita Sharada and Deśika’s mūla print भूतानि आत्मन्येव in hiatus as a reading aid.

    vowel sandhi (savarṇa-dīrgha)

Morphology

आत्मनि
The Upaniṣad witnesses read ātmani, the classical locative. The Saṃhitā witnesses (K, M) read ātman, an endingless locative of the Vedic language, and Mahīdhara comments on exactly this: आत्मन् । सप्तम्या लुक् । — ‘ātman: the locative ending is dropped’. Such loss of a case ending in Vedic is covered by P. 7.1.39 (सुपां सुलुक्पूर्वसवर्णाऽऽच्छेयाडाड्यायाजालः), which lists luk among the Vedic substitutes for a case ending. The meaning is the same.
अनुपश्यति
paśyati is the present of dṛś ‘to see’ (the present stem paśya- replaces the root). The preverb anu adds ‘along, after, repeatedly’: to see something as running through everything, or to see it again and again. The pada-pāṭha of VS 40.6 marks it as a preverb compound: अ॒नु॒पश्य॒तीत्य॑नु॒ऽपश्य॑ति.
विजुगुप्सते
Formally a desiderative-shaped stem of gup (‘to guard’), jugups-, used not as a desiderative but with its own meaning ‘to shrink from, despise’. The suffix san after gup in this sense is taught by P. 3.1.5 (गुप्तिज्किद्भ्यः सन्), the same rule Mahīdhara cites for the Mādhyandina reading vi cikitsati (‘doubts’) from kit: 'गुप्तिज्किद्भ्यः सन्' (पा० ३ । १।५) इति स्वार्थे सन्प्रत्ययः. The middle ending -te belongs to the Kāṇva form; the Mādhyandina form cikitsati is active.
ततः
The ablative adverb of tad: ‘from that’. Śaṅkara takes it causally (‘from that seeing’); it can also be temporal (‘thereafter’) or refer to the beings (‘from them’). Deśika: कुतश्चिदपि न विजुगुप्सते, ‘he shrinks from nothing whatever’.

Anvaya

यः तु सर्वाणि भूतानि आत्मनि एव अनुपश्यति सर्वभूतेषु च आत्मानम् [अनुपश्यति] [सः] ततः न विजुगुप्सते ।

yaḥ tu sarvāṇi bhūtāni ātmani eva anupaśyati sarvabhūteṣu ca ātmānam anupaśyati saḥ tataḥ na vijugupsate |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

A relative-correlative sentence. The relative clause (yas tu … anupaśyati … ātmānam) has one verb, anupaśyati, with two objects joined by ca: ‘all beings in the self’ and ‘the self in all beings’. The main clause, tato na vijugupsate, has no expressed subject: the correlative saḥ (‘he’) is understood, as Deśika notes. tataḥ links the two: the not-shrinking comes from, or after, the seeing.

Ambiguities

Which self?
ātman can be one’s own self or the self of all. Śaṅkara reads it as one’s own self, the witness of all cognitions, which is the self of all beings: अहमेवात्मेति सर्वभूतेषु चात्मानं निर्विशेषं. Deśika reads it as the inner self of all, the Lord: अत्रात्मशब्दः सङ्कोचकाभावात् प्रकरणादर्थस्वभावाच्च सर्वान्तरात्मविषयः. Madhva, citing a verse, takes it as the Paramātman present in all.
विजुगुप्सते: aversion, blame, hiding or doubt
The Kāṇva verb is read as ‘feel aversion or disgust’ (Śaṅkara), ‘blame, censure’ (Deśika: क्वचिदपि निन्दां न करोतीत्यर्थः) or, from gup ‘to guard’, ‘wish to hide/protect oneself’ (Madhva’s cited verse: नात्मानं गोप्तुमिच्छति, ‘he does not wish to guard himself’, because there is nothing to fear). The Mādhyandina recension has a different verb, vi cikitsati, ‘doubts’, which Mahīdhara glosses न संशेते.
आत्मनि or आत्मन्
A difference of form between the Upaniṣad and Saṃhitā witnesses, not of meaning (see the morphology note and corrections.json).

Metre

anuṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 15 + 16

Counted as written, the half-lines have 15 and 16 syllables. The first half-line reaches 16 when the semivowel of bhūtāny ātmany is pronounced as a vowel — bhūtāni ātmani, the hiatus Advaita Sharada prints — a resolution Vedic metre often requires. The edition reports the count and does not alter the text.

Textual variations between witnesses

  • Variant reading Kāṇva Saṃhitā 40 (svara): आत्मन्नेवानुपश्यति — The Saṃhitā witnesses (K and M) read आत्मन्नेव = ātman eva, the Vedic locative without ending (‘in the self’); the Upaniṣad witnesses read आत्मन्येव = ātmani eva, the classical locative. Same meaning, older form in the Saṃhitā.
  • Variant reading Mādhyandina VS 40 (svara, pada-pāṭha): आत्मन्नेवानुपश्यति — As K: the endingless Vedic locative ātman.
  • Other recension Mādhyandina VS 40 (svara, pada-pāṭha): ततो न वि चिकित्सति — VS 40.6 (Mādhyandina) reads ‘tato na vi cikitsati’ (‘then he does not doubt’); Mahīdhara: न विचिकित्सति न संशेते. Every Kāṇva witness reads ‘na vijugupsate’ (‘does not shrink away / hate’).
  • Other recension Mādhyandina VS 40 (svara, pada-pāṭha): विचिकित्सति — The same Mādhyandina reading: active ending -ti (cikitsati) against the middle -te (jugupsate).
11 Connections with other scriptures

  • Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.4.15 Direct textual parallel

    न ततो विजुगुप्सते

    na tato vijugupsate

    The same closing words, in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka: ‘when one sees this self as God, the lord of what was and what will be, one does not shrink from him’. The Bṛhadāraṇyaka belongs to the same Śukla Yajurveda tradition.

  • Kaṭha Upaniṣad 2.1.5 Direct textual parallel

    भूतभव्यस्य न ततो विजुगुप्सते

    bhūtabhavyasya na tato vijugupsate

    The Kaṭha repeats the same half-verse as the Bṛhadāraṇyaka, of one who knows the self ‘close at hand’.

  • Bhagavad Gītā 6.29 Doctrinal comparison

    सर्वभूतस्थमात्मानं सर्वभूतानि चात्मनि

    sarvabhūtasthamātmānaṃ sarvabhūtāni cātmani

    The Gītā’s yogin ‘sees the self abiding in all beings, and all beings in the self’ — the two directions of Īśā 6, in the same order reversed, close enough in wording to be a conscious echo, though no commentator here says so.

  • Bhagavad Gītā 6.30 Doctrinal comparison

    यो मां पश्यति सर्वत्र सर्वं च मयि पश्यति

    yo māṃ paśyati sarvatra sarvaṃ ca mayi paśyati

    The next Gītā verse puts the same vision in the first person of the Lord: ‘who sees me everywhere and everything in me’. It supports the theistic reading as the Gītā’s own development of the idea.

  • Bhagavad Gītā 13.27 Doctrinal comparison

    समं सर्वेषु भूतेषु तिष्ठन्तं परमेश्वरम्

    samaṃ sarveṣu bhūteṣu tiṣṭhantaṃ parameśvaram

    ‘The supreme Lord standing equally in all beings’: the seeing that removes partiality.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Ethical leadership

Leaders are often taught to ‘manage stakeholders’ — a phrase that already places others outside oneself, to be handled. Contempt in organisations usually begins as distance: the department that does not understand us, the customers who complain, the people a decision affects but who are not in the room. The mantra’s vision runs the other way: it begins with seeing oneself in others, and only then describes the result, that aversion falls away. A leader need not hold any metaphysical view to notice that decisions made from contempt and decisions made from a sense of shared stake tend to differ. This is a reflection on the teaching, not part of it.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Think of someone you find hard to like. Can you name one thing in them that is also in you?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry If aversion needs an ‘other’, is the self that the mantra speaks of something I have, or something I am, and does it leave room for anyone to be other?

A contemplation exercise On a walk or a commute, silently say ‘in me’ for three people you pass, and ‘in them’ for yourself. Afterwards, note whether you noticed them differently.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.

Further notes
