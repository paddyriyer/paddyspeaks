ईशावास्य उपनिषद्Īśāvāsya Upaniṣad

Living in the World Without Being Possessed by It

What truly belongs to us? Can we live fully without becoming enslaved by possession? Can action and renunciation coexist? What is the Self that remains unchanged amid all change?

The name and what it means

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Like many Vedic texts, the Upaniṣad is named after its opening words, īśā vāsyam, ‘to be dwelt in (or covered) by the Lord’. It is also called the Īśopaniṣad, ‘the Upaniṣad of the Lord’, and, from its place in the Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā, the Vājasaneyi Saṃhitopaniṣad. The name does not settle how its first words are divided: Madhva reads one compound, īśāvāsyam, ‘the dwelling of the Lord’ (ईशस्यावासयोग्यमीशावास्यम्).

Why this short text matters

Traditional Interpretation

The Īśā is the only one of the principal Upaniṣads that is part of a Saṃhitā, the Veda’s collection of mantras, and it stands at the very end of it. The tradition takes that placement as meaningful: Śaṅkara begins by saying that these mantras, unlike those before them, are not used in ritual, because they reveal the Self, which no action produces or alters (कर्मस्वविनियुक्ताः); Vedānta Deśika says the same in verse. It is traditionally placed first in the list of the ten principal Upaniṣads, and all three Vedānta schools compared here wrote commentaries on it.

In eighteen mantras it raises most of the questions the longer Upaniṣads explore at length: the relation of the Lord to the world, of action to knowledge, of the individual to the whole, and what a person can hope for at death.

A narrative introduction

Contemporary Reflection

The Upaniṣad opens without a story or a speaker. It begins with a claim about everything — all this, whatever moves in this moving world, is to be seen as dwelt in by the Lord — and turns at once to the listener: let go, and so enjoy; do not covet; whose is wealth? It then speaks to a person who must go on acting in the world, a hundred years of action that need not stain. It warns of worlds of blind darkness for those who obscure the self. Then it falls silent about the listener and describes, in paradox, what the self is: unmoving yet faster than thought, far and near, within all this and outside it. It promises that the one who sees all beings in the self will not shrink from anything, and asks what delusion or grief could remain for one who sees oneness.

Then the text does something unexpected. Twice it tells us that those who follow one way enter blind darkness, and those who follow its opposite enter a greater darkness; twice it says the wise taught that each way has its own result; twice it says that the one who knows both together crosses death and reaches immortality. The commentators have argued for a thousand years over what these pairs are. The Upaniṣad ends in prayer: a person at the end of life asks the Sun to draw back its golden lid so that the face of the real can be seen, recognises the person in the sun as himself, gives his breath back to the air and his body to the fire, and asks Agni to lead him by a good path.

The questions it asks — what belongs to us, how to act without being consumed by action, what in us does not change, what to ask for at the end — are not tied to any century. That is why so short a text has been read so closely for so long.

The principal themes

Direct Textual Meaning

The Lord in all things (1). Action without attachment (2). The consequences of ignorance of the self (3). The paradoxical nature of the Self (4–5). Seeing all beings in the self, and the end of fear, delusion and grief (6–7). The Self described by negation: bodiless, pure, untouched by evil (8). The relation of two kinds of practice, each incomplete alone (9–14). The prayers of the dying (15–18).

Recensions and variants

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The Śukla Yajurveda survives in two recensions, and the Upaniṣad in both. The Kāṇva text, followed here and by every commentary used, has eighteen mantras. The Mādhyandina text (VS 40) has seventeen: it puts the sambhūti mantras (12–14) before the vidyā mantras (9–11), reads ablatives in place of instrumentals in mantra 10, has ‘vi cikitsati’ for ‘vijugupsate’ in mantra 6 and ‘pretyāpi gacchanti’ for ‘pretyābhigacchanti’ in mantra 3, lacks the prayer to Pūṣan of 15b–16a, replaces the repeated line of 17 with ‘klibe smara’, and ends with ‘yo ’sāv āditye puruṣaḥ so ’sāv aham | om khaṃ brahma’. Within the Kāṇva tradition, too, readings differ: Mahīdhara and the Kāṇva Saṃhitā witness read arśat in mantra 4, the Upaniṣad witnesses arṣat. Every such difference is listed and classified on the Sources page.

What you will learn

  • Read the eighteen mantras in Sanskrit with a transliteration you can trust, and know what each word is doing.
  • Explain in your own words what the first mantra asks of a person, and why ‘renounce and enjoy’ is only one of its possible readings.
  • State how Śaṅkara, Vedānta Deśika and Madhva each understand ‘the Lord’ and ‘the Self’, and where they disagree.
  • Explain why vidyā and avidyā in mantras 9–11 are not simply ‘knowledge’ and ‘ignorance’.
  • Recognise the Vedic imagery of the final prayers and tell it apart from later symbolic readings.
  • Tell the ancient text from the modern voice explaining it, on any page.

A beginner’s reading guide

  1. Mantras 1–3: the vision and the choice The Lord in all things; letting go; working without being stained; the fate of those who obscure the self.
  2. Mantras 4–8: the Self Paradox (4–5), vision (6–7) and description by negation (8). The centre of the Upaniṣad.
  3. Mantras 9–14: two triads The same pattern twice — two paths that each lead to darkness, a teaching received from the wise, and a combination that ‘crosses death’. The most disputed mantras.
  4. Mantras 15–18: the prayers The prayer of a dying person to the Sun, the breath, the mind and Agni. Shared with the Bṛhadāraṇyaka (5.15.1); the last is a Ṛgveda verse.

Questions the text answers

  • What is the world, and whose is it? 1
  • Should one act, or renounce action? 1 · 2
  • What happens to those who ‘slay the self’? 3
  • How can the same reality be still and swift, near and far? 4 · 5
  • What changes in a person who sees all beings in the self? 6 · 7
  • What is the Self like, considered in itself? 8
  • Why do both ‘avidyā’ and ‘vidyā’ lead into darkness — and how can both lead beyond death? 9 · 10 · 11
  • What are sambhūti and asambhūti? 12 · 13 · 14
  • What does a person ask for at death? 15 · 16 · 17 · 18

The 18 mantras

  1. १ईशा वास्यमिदं सर्वं यत्किञ्च जगत्यां जगत्Everything here is to be dwelt in by the Lord›
  2. २कुर्वन्नेवेह कर्माणि जिजीविषेच्छतं समाःDoing one's work here, one may wish to live a hundred years›
  3. ३असुर्या नाम ते लोका अन्धेन तमसावृताःThe worlds of blind darkness and the slayers of the self›
  4. ४अनेजदेकं मनसो जवीयो नैनद्देवा आप्नुवन्पूर्वमर्षत्Unmoving, one, swifter than the mind›
  5. ५तदेजति तन्नैजति तद्दूरे तद्वन्तिकेIt moves and it does not move; it is far and it is near›
  6. ६यस्तु सर्वाणि भूतान्यात्मन्येवानुपश्यतिOne who sees all beings in the self does not shrink from anything›
  7. ७यस्मिन्सर्वाणि भूतान्यात्मैवाभूद्विजानतःWhere all beings have become the self, what delusion, what sorrow?›
  8. ८स पर्यगाच्छुक्रमकायमव्रणमस्नाविरं शुद्धमपापविद्धम्He went round: the bright, the bodiless, untouched by evil›
  9. ९अन्धं तमः प्रविशन्ति येऽविद्यामुपासतेInto blind darkness — and into greater darkness›
  10. १०अन्यदेवाहुर्विद्ययान्यदाहुरविद्ययाOne thing, they say, by vidyā; another by avidyā›
  11. ११विद्यां चाविद्यां च यस्तद्वेदोभयं सहKnowing both together: crossing death, reaching the deathless›
  12. १२अन्धं तमः प्रविशन्ति येऽसम्भूतिमुपासतेBlind darkness for those who worship non-becoming, more for those who delight in becoming›
  13. १३अन्यदेवाहुः सम्भवादन्यदाहुरसम्भवात्One thing from becoming, another from non-becoming›
  14. १४सम्भूतिं च विनाशं च यस्तद्वेदोभयं सहKnowing becoming and destruction together›
  15. १५हिरण्मयेन पात्रेण सत्यस्यापिहितं मुखम्The face of the real is covered by a golden vessel›
  16. १६पूषन्नेकर्षे यम सूर्य प्राजापत्य व्यूह रश्मीन्समूह तेजःGather your light; that person yonder, I am he›
  17. १७वायुरनिलममृतमथेदं भस्मान्तं शरीरम्Breath to the immortal air; this body ends in ashes›
  18. १८अग्ने नय सुपथा राये अस्मान्विश्वानि देव वयुनानि विद्वान्Agni, lead us by a good path›

Special philosophical studies

  1. I. Īśāvāsyam idaṃ sarvam — What does it mean for everything to be pervaded by the Lord? (mantras 1, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8)
  2. II. Tena tyaktena bhuñjīthāḥ — Four words, five readings: the grammar and philosophy of a celebrated phrase (mantras 1)
  3. III. Karma and Jñāna — Action, renunciation and knowledge in mantras 1–2 and 9–11 (mantras 1, 2, 9, 10, 11)
  4. IV. Vidyā and Avidyā — Mantras 9–11: why the Upaniṣad warns against knowledge, and what its commentators think ‘knowledge’ means here (mantras 9, 10, 11)
  5. V. Sambhūti and Asambhūti — Becoming, non-becoming and perishing in mantras 12–14 (mantras 12, 13, 14)
  6. VI. The Final Prayers — Mantras 15–18: the golden vessel, the gathered light, the breath and the ashes, and Agni on the good path (mantras 15, 16, 17, 18)
