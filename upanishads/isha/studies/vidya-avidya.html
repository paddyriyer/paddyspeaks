Study IV · Īśāvāsya Upaniṣad

Vidyā and Avidyā

Mantras 9–11: why the Upaniṣad warns against knowledge, and what its commentators think ‘knowledge’ means here

Mantras 9, 10, 11

Three mantras warn against devotion to avidyā, warn more sharply against devotion to vidyā, and then commend knowing both together. None of the four commentaries used in this edition takes the words in their modern everyday sense of ‘knowledge’ and ‘ignorance’; this study sets out what each takes them to mean, what the grammar allows, and where the readings divide.

A mantra that seems to condemn knowledge

Direct Textual Meaning

Mantra 9 says: अन्धं तमः प्रविशन्ति येऽविद्यामुपासते — ‘into blind darkness enter those who worship avidyā’ — and then, ततो भूय इव ते तमो य उ विद्यायां रताः — ‘into a darkness greater, as it were, than that, those who delight in vidyā’. If avidyā is ignorance and vidyā is knowledge, the verse says that the learned are worse off than the ignorant. Mantra 10 then says that the two lead to different things, and mantra 11 commends the person who knows ‘both together’ (ubhayaṃ saha), who ‘crosses death by avidyā and attains immortality by vidyā’.

Read in the everyday sense, the sequence is close to contradiction: ignorance is condemned and then made a means of crossing death; knowledge is condemned more harshly and then made the means of immortality. No commentator accepts that reading. The words are deliberately set against each other, and the Upaniṣad itself gives no definition; everything therefore depends on what vidyā and avidyā are taken to name. That is a question of interpretation, and it should be visible as one.

Three features of the Sanskrit constrain any answer. First, the verbs differ: the first group ‘worships’ or ‘attends upon’ (upāsate) avidyā, the second ‘delights in’ (ratāḥ) vidyā. Second, the condemnation in mantra 9 is of each taken alone, since mantra 11 commends knowing both. Third, mantra 11 assigns the two different functions in a fixed order — avidyā first, to cross death; vidyā then, to reach the deathless — so the two cannot simply be the same thing under two names.

What the words can mean

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

avidyā is a negative compound, a- + vidyā. Such a negative can mean the absence of the thing (‘not-knowing’) or something other than it and like it in kind (‘what is other than knowledge’). The commentators all choose the second. vidyā itself is wider than ‘knowledge’ in English: it covers learning, a science, and, in the Upaniṣads, meditative knowledge directed to a deity or to Brahman — an ‘upāsanā’ as much as an understanding. The verb upāsate, from upa + ās, ‘to sit near, attend upon’, is the verb of such worship and meditation, and the Upaniṣad uses it here of avidyā.

Mantra 10 adds a grammatical problem. अन्यदेवाहुर्विद्यया is literally ‘something other, they say, by vidyā’. With anya, ‘other’, Sanskrit normally puts the thing compared in the ablative: P. 2.3.29 (अन्यारादितरर्त्तेदिक्शब्दाञ्चूत्तरपदाजाहियुक्ते). The Kena (1.4) has exactly that construction in a verse whose second line is all but identical to the Īśā’s: अन्यदेव तद्विदितादथो अविदितादधि — ‘it is other than the known, and beyond the unknown’ — followed by इति शुश्रुम पूर्वेषां ये नस्तद्व्याचचक्षिरे. So the Īśā’s instrumental either means what an instrumental usually means (‘by vidyā’) or stands for the ablative (‘other than vidyā’).

The other recension takes a side. In the Mādhyandina Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā the corresponding mantra (VS 40.13) reads विद्यायाः … अविद्यायाः, a form that is ablative or genitive; and in that recension the three vidyā mantras come after the three sambhūti mantras instead of before them (VS 40.12–14). The Kāṇva Saṃhitā witness used here has विद्यया … अविद्याया, which mixes the two forms and is marked for verification against a printed edition. All the Upaniṣad witnesses — GRETIL, Advaita Sharada, Madhva’s and Deśika’s texts — read the instrumental.

Śaṅkara: ritual, and the knowledge of deities

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara’s introduction to mantra 9 is the longest in his commentary, and it decides his reading of all six mantras that follow. The Upaniṣad has two readers. To the renouncer it taught, in mantra 1 and mantras 3–8, the knowledge of the Self; to the person who wishes to live doing works, mantra 2 taught the path of works. Mantras 9–14 are for that second reader: ये तु कामिनः कर्मनिष्ठाः कर्म कुर्वन्त एव जिजीविषवः, तेभ्य इदमुच्यते. The reason is that the knowledge of oneness described in mantra 7 cannot be combined with anything else: तन्न केनचित्कर्मणा ज्ञानान्तरेण वा ह्यमूढः समुच्चिचीषति. Since mantras 9–11 do recommend a combination, the knowledge they speak of cannot be Self-knowledge.

What, then, is it? तद्दैवं वित्तं देवताविषयं ज्ञानं कर्मसम्बन्धित्वेनोपन्यस्तं न परमात्मज्ञानम् — knowledge of deities, put forward as connected with ritual, not knowledge of the Supreme Self. avidyā is ritual itself: विद्याया अन्या अविद्या कर्मेत्यर्थः, कर्मणो विद्याविरोधित्वात् — ‘avidyā, other than vidyā, means action, because action is opposed to knowledge’ — specifically the agnihotra and other rites performed alone. Those who give up ritual for meditation on deities alone fare worse: कर्म हित्वा ये उ ये तु विद्यायामेव देवताज्ञाने एव रताः.

Why condemn either, if both are enjoined? Each is condemned only as practised alone, so as to recommend practising them together: तयोर्ज्ञानकर्मणोरिहैकैकानुष्ठाननिन्दा समुच्चिचीषया, न निन्दापरैव एकैकस्य. They have distinct results, which mantra 10 states and which Śaṅkara identifies through Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.5.16: कर्मणा पितृलोकः, विद्यया देवलोकः. In mantra 11, ‘death’ is ordinary, unregulated action and knowing — स्वाभाविकं कर्म ज्ञानं च मृत्युशब्दवाच्यम् — crossed by ritual; and ‘immortality’ is becoming one with the deity meditated on: तद्ध्यमृतमुच्यते, यद्देवतात्मगमनम्. It is a real attainment, but a finite one, and it is not the liberation that the Upaniṣad has already taught.

Mahīdhara: the Mādhyandina reading

Traditional Interpretation

Mahīdhara comments on the same three mantras as VS 40.12–14, in the Mādhyandina order. He follows Śaṅkara’s scheme closely, often in Śaṅkara’s words: the censure of each is for the sake of combining them (विद्याविद्ययोः समुच्चिचीषया प्रत्येकं निन्दोच्यते), avidyā is ritual (विद्याया अन्या अविद्या कर्म), and the immortality of the last mantra is identity with a deity (देवतात्मभावमश्नुते).

Two details are his own. On the first mantra he gives a reason why abandoning ritual is the greater darkness: without ritual the mind is not purified, and knowledge does not arise — कर्माकरणे प्रत्यवायोत्पत्यान्तःकरणशुद्ध्यभावेन ज्ञानानुदयादिति भावः. And his gloss on vidyā shifts. On VS 40.12 he writes विद्यायामात्मज्ञाने देवताज्ञाने एव रताः, naming both Self-knowledge and knowledge of deities; on VS 40.13, with the Mādhyandina form विद्यायाः, he takes the word as a genitive and the result as immortality, विद्याया आत्मज्ञानस्य फलमन्यदेवाहुरमृतरूपम्; on VS 40.14 he returns to देवताज्ञानं. The oscillation is worth noticing: it shows how hard it is to keep vidyā in these mantras fixed to one sense.

Deśika: works as the auxiliary of meditation on the Lord

Traditional Interpretation

Vedānta Deśika reads the three mantras as one argument for liberation by knowledge that is supported by works. Mantra 9 censures two one-sided positions, those who rely on works alone and those who rely on knowledge alone (केवलकर्मावलम्बिनः केवलविद्यावलम्बिनश्च), in order to teach that release comes ‘by knowledge alone, assisted by the duties of one’s station and stage of life’: वर्णाश्रमधर्मानुगृहीतया विद्ययैव निश्श्रेयसावाप्तिरित्याह. avidyā in mantra 9 is action without knowledge, ज्ञानविधुरकर्ममात्रम्, and he supports the word by a remembered verse that calls action avidyā: अविद्या कर्मसंज्ञाऽन्या तृतीया शक्तिरिष्यते. The second group are those who give up the duties proper to them and delight in knowledge alone: स्वाधिकारोचितकर्मपरित्यागेन विद्यामात्रे रताः.

For mantra 10 he takes the grammatical road described above: the instrumental has the force of the ablative — अत्र व्यत्ययानुशासनात् पञ्चम्यर्थे तृतीया, अन्यथा अनन्वयात् — supported by the parallel ablative of mantra 13 (sambhavāt). The line then says that the means of liberation is other than works alone and other than knowledge without works: केवलकर्मणः कर्मनिरपेक्षविद्यातश्चान्यत् मोक्षसाधनमित्युक्तं भवति. He also comments on the unusual first-person perfect shuśruma, ‘we have heard’: it conveys that this knowledge is too deep to be grasped entire (ब्रह्मविद्याया दुरवगाहत्वेन निश्शेषग्रहणाशक्यत्वाभिप्रायोऽत्र लिडुत्तमः).

In mantra 11, vidyā is meditation on the Supreme Self and avidyā the works subordinate to it: विद्याम् - परमात्मोपासनरूपाम्, अविद्याम् - तदङ्गभूतकर्मात्मिकां. The two are known ‘together’ because both are to be undertaken, although one is principal and the other auxiliary (अङ्गाङ्गिनोरप्यनुष्ठेयत्वसाम्यादुभयं सह). Works overcome ‘death’, the past karma that contracts knowledge; meditation reaches the deathless, which for Deśika is the Lord himself. He defends the word avidyā for works by an analogy: a negative word refers to what is nearest to the thing negated, as ‘non-brāhmaṇa’ refers to a kṣatriya (क्षत्रियादिविषयाब्राह्मणशब्दादिवत् आसन्नतदन्यवृत्तिरन्तरङ्गकर्मविषय इति भावः). He quotes the Bhāṣyakāra, as his tradition names Rāmānuja, for the same point — अत्राविद्याशब्दाभिहितं वर्णाश्रमविहितं कर्म — and argues against an interpretation with two means and two goals (साध्यद्वित्वं).

Madhva: right and wrong worship of the Lord

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva comments on mantras 9–11 in a single passage in verse, and the digital text used by this edition breaks off before its end. What it contains moves the question from knowledge and action to the rightness of worship. Those who worship the Lord otherwise than as he is go to blind darkness — अन्यथोपासका ये तु तमोऽन्धं यान्त्यसंशयम् — and those who do not censure them go to a darkness greater still — ततोऽधिकमिव व्यक्तं यान्ति तेषामनिन्दकाः.

The resolution corresponds to mantra 11: those who know Nārāyaṇa as he is, together with the censure of what is untrue — यथास्वरूपं तु नारायणमनामयम् । अयथार्थस्य निन्दां च ये विदुः सह सज्जनाः — cross sorrow and ignorance and, through full knowledge of what is true (यथार्थस्य परिज्ञानात्), attain a nature of happiness and knowledge. On this reading the pair known ‘together’ is true knowledge of the Lord and the rejection of false views of him. The passage does not, in the text available, assign each word of the mantra; this edition reports its sense and leaves the construction open.

Where the readings divide

Comparative Vedānta

Set side by side, the readings agree on more than one might expect. All four deny that the Upaniṣad condemns knowledge as such. All four take avidyā as something positive that is other than vidyā — for three of them, prescribed action — and not as mere ignorance. All accept that mantra 11 recommends holding two things together. They divide on four questions.

What is vidyā? For Śaṅkara, meditative knowledge of deities, not knowledge of the Self (न परमात्मज्ञानम्); for Deśika, meditation on the Supreme Self (परमात्मोपासनरूपाम्); for Madhva, true knowledge of Nārāyaṇa; for Mahīdhara, now knowledge of deities, now Self-knowledge. Who is addressed? For Śaṅkara, only the person committed to works; for Deśika and Madhva, every seeker. What does ‘together’ mean? For Śaṅkara, two practices of one person, each with its own result; for Deśika, a principal practice and its auxiliary. What is the ‘immortality’ of mantra 11? For Śaṅkara and Mahīdhara, a finite divine state (देवतात्मभावम्); for Deśika and Madhva, liberation.

The decisive difference lies outside these three mantras. Śaṅkara holds that knowledge of the Self cannot be combined with action, because action requires the sense of being an agent that such knowledge removes; so whatever these mantras combine with action must be some other knowledge. The theistic commentators hold that the knowledge that liberates is itself a sustained meditation on the Lord, which works support; so these mantras can be about liberation. Each reading is coherent within its system, and each must strain somewhere: Śaṅkara must restrict vidyā here to a lower knowledge, and Deśika must read the instrumental of mantra 10 as an ablative. The Upaniṣad’s own words do not settle the matter, and an edition should not pretend that they do.

The mantras among their neighbours

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Mantra 9 is also Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.10, word for word: अन्धं तमः प्रविशन्ति येऽविद्यामुपासते. In the Bṛhadāraṇyaka it stands among verses on the knower of the Self and is followed directly by a verse that is the counterpart of Īśā 3 (‘joyless are those worlds…’). The Bṛhadāraṇyaka has the warning but neither the reconciliation of mantras 10–11 nor any recommendation to combine; whatever the Īśā means by ‘both together’, it is its own addition to a verse that circulated without it.

Mantra 10’s second line circulated too. The Kena’s इति शुश्रुम पूर्वेषां ये नस्तद्व्याचचक्षिरे differs only by pūrveṣām for dhīrāṇām and the preverb of the last word. The shared formula presents the teaching as received from a line of teachers rather than discovered; and the Kena’s first line, with its ablatives after anyat, is the clearest evidence that Deśika’s ‘other than’ is a natural construction for this kind of verse.

The Muṇḍaka’s warning about ritual, which Deśika quotes on mantra 9 — प्लवा ह्येते अदृढा यज्ञरूपा — shows a related use of the same theme: sacrifices are unsteady boats, and those who delight in them alone return to old age and death. The Muṇḍaka’s ‘two knowledges’ (1.1.4), higher and lower, are often compared with the Īśā’s pair; but in none of the four commentaries are the Muṇḍaka’s two the same as the Īśā’s vidyā and avidyā, and the comparison is thematic only.

  • Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.4.10: अन्धं तमः प्रविशन्ति येऽविद्यामुपासते andhaṃ tamaḥ praviśanti ye'vidyāmupāsate
  • Kena Upaniṣad 1.4: अन्यदेव तद्विदितादथो अविदितादधि anyadeva tadviditādatho aviditādadhi
  • Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad 1.2.7: प्लवा ह्येते अदृढा यज्ञरूपा plavā hyete adṛḍhā yajñarūpā
  • Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad 1.1.4: द्वे विद्ये वेदितव्ये dve vidye veditavye

Reading the three mantras today

Contemporary Reflection

A modern reader will hear in these mantras a warning that every tradition of practice knows: doing without understanding becomes routine, and understanding without doing becomes self-regard. The Upaniṣad’s sharper warning is against the second, and the commentators, for different reasons, agree. That is a real point of contact with the texts, as long as it is held as a reflection and not as a translation. It is not what Śaṅkara, Deśika or Madhva say the mantras mean; it is a way of noticing that their readings share a structure — two things, each insufficient alone, held together in an order — that survives outside their systems.

What does not survive the transfer is the specific content: the ritual of the agnihotra, the meditation on deities, the worship of Nārāyaṇa, the rejection of false doctrine. A careful reader keeps both in view: the structure that speaks across centuries, and the particular practices without which, for the tradition, the structure is empty.

Open questions

  • Does the Kāṇva Saṃhitā print अविद्याया in mantra 10, as the digital witness keys it? A printed Kāṇva Saṃhitā is needed.
  • How does Madhva construe each word of mantras 9–11? The digital text of his comment is abridged; a printed edition of his bhāṣya (with its sub-commentaries) should be consulted.
  • Does the order of the Mādhyandina recension, with the sambhūti mantras first, reflect an older arrangement of the chapter? The materials of this edition do not decide it.
  • Is the closeness of mantra 10b to Kena 1.4 evidence of a shared source, or of a formula of transmission used in several schools? A question for textual history, not answered here.
