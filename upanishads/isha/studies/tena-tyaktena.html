Study II · Īśāvāsya Upaniṣad

Tena tyaktena bhuñjīthāḥ

Four words, five readings: the grammar and philosophy of a celebrated phrase

Mantras 1

‘Renounce and enjoy’ is the best-known rendering of the second half of mantra 1, and it is not the reading of any of the four commentaries used here. This study takes the phrase word by word — what tena refers to, what kind of instrumental tyaktena is, what bhuñjīthāḥ can mean in the middle voice — sets out the readings of Śaṅkara, Mahīdhara, Deśika and Madhva side by side, and closes with mā gṛdhaḥ kasya svid dhanam, which can be heard in two ways.

What the phrase contains

Direct Textual Meaning

tena tyaktena bhuñjīthāḥ has three words. tena is the instrumental of the pronoun ‘that’ (masculine or neuter). tyaktena is the instrumental of the past participle of tyaj, ‘to leave, abandon, give up’, agreeing with tena: ‘by/with that [which is] given up’. bhuñjīthāḥ is the optative, second person singular, middle voice (ātmanepada), of bhuj: ‘you should enjoy / eat / use’ — or, in a sense to be examined, ‘protect’.

Nothing in the phrase says who gives up, what is given up, or what is enjoyed. ‘That’ most naturally points back to what the first half has just named — idaṃ sarvam, ‘all this’, or jagat, ‘the moving world’ — but the pronoun could also look back to the Lord (īśā), and one commentator takes it so. Every translation of the phrase supplies an answer to at least one of these questions.

What kind of instrumental?

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The Sanskrit instrumental has several uses. It can mark the means (‘by renunciation’), the accompanying circumstance or the thing one is ‘with’ (‘with that given up’), or the agent of a passive. Each of these yields a different sentence.

Śaṅkara chooses the instrumental of means and makes tyaktena an abstract noun: तेन त्यक्तेन त्यागेनेत्यर्थः, ‘by that renunciation’. He rules out the alternative explicitly: an abandoned or dead son or servant does not look after anyone, because the relation to oneself has been cut — न हि त्यक्तो मृतः पुत्रो भृत्यो वा आत्मसम्बन्धिताभावादात्मानं पालयति — so ‘by the abandoned [person]’ makes no sense, and ‘by renunciation’ must be meant.

Mahīdhara takes the instrumental of accompaniment with an adjective: तेन सर्वेण त्यक्तेन त्यक्तस्वस्वामिसंबन्धेन भुञ्जीथाः — ‘with all that, given up, that is, with its relation to an owner given up, you should enjoy’. Deśika does the same with the world as the thing given up: तेन - जगता भोग्यताभ्रमविषयेण, त्यक्तेन - दोषभूयस्त्वदर्शनात् परित्यक्तेन उपलक्षितः सन् भुञ्जीथाः — ‘marked by that [world], the object of the mistaken idea that it is to be enjoyed, given up because one sees its many faults’. Madhva takes tena as the Lord and tyakta in the sense of a gift: तेन ईशेन । त्यक्तेन दत्तेन — ‘with what has been given by him’.

Madhva’s sense is less unexpected than it may sound. tyaj means to let go of something, and letting go of a thing in someone’s favour is giving it; the noun tyāga is also the ordinary ritual word for the sacrificer’s relinquishing of an offering to a deity. Whether the Upaniṣad’s composer had that sense in view is another matter: the mantra says nothing of a giver.

bhuñjīthāḥ: enjoy, or protect?

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The root bhuj (7th class) has two clusters of meaning: ‘to enjoy, eat, use, experience’ and ‘to protect, rule’. Pāṇini ties them to the two voices. P. 1.3.66 (भुजोऽनवने) prescribes the middle voice for bhuj ‘when it does not mean protecting’: bhuṅkte, ‘he eats’, but bhunakti, ‘he protects’. The form in the mantra, bhuñjīthāḥ, is middle. By that rule it should mean ‘you should enjoy, eat’.

Śaṅkara nevertheless glosses भुञ्जीथाः पालयेथाः, ‘you should protect [yourself]’, and his whole reading depends on it: the one who has renounced protects the Self by renunciation, by not identifying with anything outside it. The tension with the grammarian’s rule is real. It is not conclusive against him, for two reasons that should be stated fairly: Vedic usage is older than Pāṇini’s systematisation and does not always follow it, and a commentator may take a word in a sense the context demands. But it means that Śaṅkara’s gloss is an interpretation, argued for, and not the plain sense of the form.

The other commentators take the verb in its middle-voice sense. Mahīdhara: भुञ्जीथाः भोगमनुभवेः, ‘experience enjoyment’. Deśika restricts what is enjoyed, without changing the verb’s meaning: what the body needs in order to be sustained for a life of discipline (अप्रतिषिद्धयोगधर्मोपयुक्तदेहधारणमात्रौपयिकं भोक्तव्यवर्गम्), or, in his alternative, the Lord himself, ‘enjoyable beyond all’ (सर्वावासत्वेन प्रकृतं निरतिशयभोग्यं).

Five readings side by side

Comparative Vedānta

Śaṅkara: by that renunciation, protect [the Self]. The addressee is one fit for renouncing the three desires; what is given up is everything other than the Self. — Mahīdhara: with all that, its ownership given up, enjoy. What is given up is the relation ‘mine’ (ममेदमिति धियं त्यजेत्यर्थः). — Deśika (first option): with the world given up, seen as full of faults, enjoy only what sustains the body. — Deśika (second option): enjoy the Lord, the supreme object of enjoyment. — Madhva: with what has been given by the Lord, enjoy; therefore ask nothing of another (तद्दत्तेनैव भुञ्जीथा अतो नान्यं प्रयाचयेत्, in a verse he cites).

The readings fall along two lines. On the first, what is given up: the world itself (Śaṅkara, Deśika), only one’s claim on it (Mahīdhara), or nothing at all, the word naming a gift (Madhva). On the second, what the verb asks: protection of the Self (Śaṅkara), a disciplined and limited use (Deśika), enjoyment free of ownership (Mahīdhara), or grateful enjoyment of what is given (Madhva). None of them proposes that renunciation is a technique for enjoying more.

‘Renounce and enjoy’

Comparative Vedānta

The popular rendering keeps two features of the Sanskrit — the sequence of the two ideas and the plain sense of the verb — and drops the rest. It is closest to Mahīdhara, who also reads ‘enjoy’ and also has something given up. It is furthest from Śaṅkara, for whom the phrase prescribes renunciation in the full sense and the verb means ‘protect’. And it hides the question that every commentator answers and the slogan leaves open: what is given up?

Read carelessly, ‘renounce and enjoy’ can sound like a paradox designed to reassure — give up a little, gain a great deal. Read against the commentaries, it is better heard as a statement about the conditions of enjoyment: what one clutches cannot be enjoyed, because clutching is already the coveting the next words forbid. That is a fair summary of the ground the readings share. It should not be presented as the meaning of the mantra, which this edition does not claim to know beyond what the words and their interpreters say.

mā gṛdhaḥ kasya svid dhanam

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The prohibition is an aorist form without its augment, used after mā: P. 3.3.175 (माङि लुङ्) for the aorist and P. 6.4.74 (न माङ्योगे) for the missing augment. gṛdh is ‘to be greedy, to covet’; Mahīdhara gives the dictionary entry गृधु अभिकाङ्क्षायाम्.

The last three words can be construed in two ways, and Śaṅkara gives both. As the object of the prohibition: ‘do not covet anyone’s wealth, another’s or your own’ (कस्यचित् परस्य स्वस्य वा धनं मा काङ्क्षीः), with svit as a particle without meaning of its own. As a separate, rhetorical question: ‘do not covet — why? whose is wealth?’, meaning that wealth is no one’s (न कस्यचिद्धनमस्ति, यद्गृध्येत). Mahīdhara takes svit as a particle of doubt and reads the question: धनं कस्य स्वित् । न कस्यापीत्यर्थः. Deśika reads the object: ‘do not desire the wealth of anyone, kin or not’.

The question reading gives the mantra a striking end — a question that undoes the idea of ownership on which coveting depends — and it fits the first half: if all this is to be covered or dwelt in by the Lord, ‘whose is wealth?’ has an answer that is not ‘mine’. The object reading is simpler and also fits. The Sanskrit allows both, and the edition’s translation keeps the ambiguity.

Why the phrase matters for the whole Upaniṣad

Traditional Interpretation

How one reads tena tyaktena decides how one reads mantra 2. For Śaṅkara the two mantras address two kinds of person: mantra 1 the renouncer, mantra 2 the one who cannot renounce and should therefore go on doing works — अथेतरस्य अनात्मज्ञतयात्मग्रहणायाशक्तस्य इदमुपदिशति मन्त्रः. For the commentators who read ‘enjoy’, the two mantras describe one life: enjoyment without greed, and action without attachment. The study on karma and jñāna follows that division into the rest of the text.

A modern reflection

Contemporary Reflection

Many people know the difference between using something and being used by the wish to have more of it. The mantra puts the line between them in an unexpected place: not between having and not having, but between holding and clutching. Whether one follows Śaṅkara to renunciation, Mahīdhara to enjoyment without ownership, Deśika to a disciplined sufficiency or Madhva to gratitude for what is given, the mantra’s final question remains available to anyone: whose is this, really? This paragraph is a modern reflection, not a reading of the commentaries.

Open questions

  • Whether the composer intended bhuj in the sense ‘protect’ cannot be settled from this one occurrence; a study of bhuj in the middle voice across the Vedic corpus would be needed.
  • Whether tena refers to the world (most commentators) or to the Lord (Madhva) is grammatically open.
