Study VI · Īśāvāsya Upaniṣad

The Final Prayers

Mantras 15–18: the golden vessel, the gathered light, the breath and the ashes, and Agni on the good path

Mantras 15, 16, 17, 18

The Īśā ends not with a doctrine but with four prayers in the voice of a petitioner, the same four that close the Bṛhadāraṇyaka (5.15.1), the last of them a verse of the Ṛgveda. This study separates what the Vedic images say — a golden vessel over a face, rays withdrawn, breath returning to wind, a body ending in ashes, a fire that knows the way — from what the commentators made of them, and sets out the textual problems of the two recensions.

Where the teaching becomes a prayer

Direct Textual Meaning

Mantras 1 to 14 teach. They state how the world is to be seen, how one is to act, what the Self is like, and what becomes of those devoted to one-sided practices. Mantra 15 changes the grammatical person and the mood. From here on someone speaks in the first person — ‘I see’ (paśyāmi), ‘I am’ (asmi), ‘us’ (asmān), ‘let us offer’ (vidhema) — and addresses deities in the vocative and the imperative: Pūṣan, uncover; Sun, gather your light; kratu, remember; Agni, lead. The teaching voice does not return.

The four mantras are not the Īśā’s own composition in any simple sense. The same four, in the same order, make up the fifteenth section of the fifth chapter of the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad (Kāṇva), which begins हिरण्मयेन पात्रेण सत्यस्यापिहितं मुखम् and ends with Agni and the words of homage. The last of them is also the opening verse of a hymn to Agni in the Ṛgveda (1.189.1), अग्ने नय सुपथा राये. The Īśā therefore ends by citing, or sharing, a group of prayers that circulated in the Śukla Yajurveda tradition and drew on the oldest stratum of Vedic poetry.

Read only for what the words say, the sequence has a clear shape. A face — ‘the face of the real’ — is covered by a golden vessel, and the speaker asks for it to be uncovered so that he may see (15). The sun is called by five names and asked to draw back its rays and gather in its light; the speaker sees its most beautiful form and says ‘that person yonder, I am he’ (16). Breath goes to the immortal air, the body ends in ashes, and someone called kratu is asked twice to remember what has been done (17). Agni, who knows the ways, is asked to lead by a good path, to keep crooked wrong away, and to accept abundant homage (18). The movement is from seeing, through dying, to being led.

  • Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 5.15.1: हिरण्मयेन पात्रेण सत्यस्यापिहितं मुखम् hiraṇmayena pātreṇa satyasyāpihitaṃ mukham
  • Ṛgveda 1.189.1: अग्ने नय सुपथा राये agne naya supathā rāye

The Vedic imagery, on its own terms

Direct Textual Meaning

The images are concrete and belong to a world of ritual and of the sky. A pātra is a drinking vessel or bowl; the face it covers is golden-lidded, which is to say dazzling. The sun is not described abstractly but by its ‘rays’ (raśmīn) and its ‘brilliance’ (tejaḥ), and the speaker asks for two physical actions with one root: vyūha, ‘push apart’, and samūha, ‘push together’ — scatter the rays, concentrate the light. Only when the glare is reduced does the ‘most beautiful form’ (rūpaṃ kalyāṇatamam) appear.

The five names in mantra 16 are those of the sun and its circle: Pūṣan, the nourisher and guide of roads; the ‘sole seer’ or ‘lone traveller’ (ekarṣi); Yama, the controller and lord of the dead; Sūrya; and the child of Prajāpati. Pūṣan in Vedic religion is a guide on journeys, which is why he is the natural addressee of a request for passage; Yama and the sun belong to the imagery of the way the dead travel.

Mantra 17 names the elements of a person as they dissolve: vāyu, breath, returns to anila, the wind, called ‘immortal’ because the wind does not die; and ‘this body’ is bhasmānta, ‘having ashes as its end’, the plain consequence of cremation. Mantra 18 addresses the fire, which in Vedic ritual both carries offerings to the gods and consumes the dead. The good path (supathā), the wealth (rai) and the crooked wrong (juhurāṇam enaḥ) are the vocabulary of the Ṛgvedic sacrificer, who asks Agni for safe guidance and prosperity.

None of this requires a doctrine of the Self. The person in the sun of mantra 16 does have a Vedic background: the Bṛhadāraṇyaka teaches a meditation on ‘the person in this disk’ of the sun, identical with the person in the right eye (स आदित्यो य एष एतस्मिन्मण्डले पुरुषो), and the same Upaniṣad places the four prayers soon after. But in the mantras themselves the identification ‘I am he’ is stated, not explained.

  • Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 5.5.2: य एष एतस्मिन्मण्डले पुरुषो ya eṣa etasminmaṇḍale puruṣo

Two recensions, two endings

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The Mādhyandina recension of the Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā ends its fortieth chapter differently. Its fifteenth verse corresponds to Īśā 17 but reads ‘om krato smara | klibe smara | kṛtaṃ smara’: klibe in place of the Kāṇva repetition. Mahīdhara takes klibe as a Vedic form of klṛp, ‘the world that is fashioned’: क्लिबे स्मर कल्प्यते भोगायेति क्लृप् लोकः तस्मै स्मर. Its sixteenth is Agni’s verse, Īśā 18. Its seventeenth and last keeps only the first line of Īśā 15 and continues in prose: यो॒ऽसावा॑दि॒त्ये पु॑रुषः॒ सो᳙ऽसाव॒हम्। ओ३म् खं ब्रह्म॑ — ‘the person who is in the sun, that I am. Om, Brahman is space.’ The request to Pūṣan, and the whole of Īśā 16, are absent.

So the Mādhyandina chapter ends with a meditation of identity and a formula about Brahman as space, where the Kāṇva ends with Agni and homage. Mahīdhara reads the last unit as a meditation: सूर्यमण्डलस्थः पुरुषोऽहमेवेत्यभेदेन चिन्तयेत् — ‘one should think, without difference, “I am the person in the sun’s disk”’ — and om khaṃ brahma as the instruction to meditate on Brahman as pervasive like space, repeating Om (नभोवद्व्यापकं ब्रह्म ओमिति जपन्ध्यायेदित्यर्थः). He describes the metre of this last unit as an uṣṇik ending in two yajus (उष्णिक् यजुर्द्वयान्ता), and the verse corresponding to Īśā 17 as ‘two yajus’, prose formulas. He also reports, from Kātyāyana, that the yajus beginning ‘krato’ are those by which the yogin is reminded of his sacrifices at the end.

Within the Kāṇva tradition there is a further problem. The keyed text of the Kāṇva Saṃhitā used by this edition (witness K) lacks the first half of mantra 16 — ‘pūṣann ekarṣe yama sūrya prājāpatya vyūha raśmīn samūha’ — and begins it at tejaḥ, with रूपे for रूपं. Every Upaniṣad witness, and the Bṛhadāraṇyaka’s copy of the prayer, have the full text. From the materials available it cannot be decided whether the shorter form belongs to the Saṃhitā recitation or is an omission of the digital witness; the edition classes it as requiring verification against a printed Kāṇva Saṃhitā and follows the Upaniṣad witnesses.

The numbering, too, is not universal. Madhva’s text divides mantras 16 and 17 into two each, giving twenty units; his commentary treats 15–19 as one passage. The eighteen-mantra numbering is that of the Kāṇva Upaniṣad text commented on by Śaṅkara and Deśika.

Śaṅkara: the prayers of the meditator at death

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara reads the four mantras as the prayers of one particular person: the one who has lived by works together with meditation on the lower Brahman, as mantra 11 described, and who now, at the time of death, asks for the door. His introduction to mantra 15 sums up the whole Upaniṣad as teaching two aims, activity and withdrawal (एवं द्विप्रकारः प्रवृत्तिनिवृत्तिलक्षणो वेदार्थोऽत्र प्रकाशितः): the highest fruit of activity, however exalted, ends in dissolution into prakṛti, whereas becoming the Self of all, ‘for the one who knows’ (mantra 7), is the fruit of renunciation and knowledge. The meditator of mantra 11, who worshipped the true as the person in the sun and in the eye, ‘asks, when the end has come, for the door by which he may reach the true’ (सोऽन्तकाले प्राप्ते सत्यात्मानमात्मनः प्राप्तिद्वारं याचते).

Each mantra then has a literal sense within that frame. The golden vessel is the luminous disk that covers the entrance to Brahman within it; Pūṣan is asked to move it aside. The five names in 16 are the sun’s, and the speaker asks to see its form ‘by your grace’ — but not as a servant: अहं न तु त्वां भृत्यवद्याचे, for the person in the disk is himself. In 17 the dying man wishes that his breath return to the cosmic air and his subtle body go out, लिङ्गं चेदं ज्ञानकर्मसंस्कृतमुत्क्रामत्विति द्रष्टव्यम्, and that the body offered in the fire become ashes; Om, the symbol of Brahman called Agni, is addressed as kratu, ‘whose nature is resolve’, and asked to remember his works. In 18 Agni is asked to lead by the good path that does not return, in contrast with the southern path of coming and going.

Śaṅkara’s closing discussion makes the frame an argument. If vidyā in mantra 11 meant knowledge of the highest Self, the prayers for a door and a path would be pointless, since the knower of mantra 7 goes nowhere: विद्याशब्देन परमात्मविद्याग्रहणे ‘हिरण्मयेन’(ई. उ. १५) इत्यादिना द्वारमार्गयाचनमनुपपन्नं स्यात्. The final prayers are thus his principal evidence that the combination of mantra 11 is of works with meditation, not of works with Self-knowledge (उपासनया समुच्चयो न परमात्मविज्ञानेनेति). Mahīdhara, commenting on the Mādhyandina text, reproduces this argument almost word for word at the end of his commentary.

Deśika: the prayers of the devotee in meditation

Traditional Interpretation

Vedānta Deśika reads the same mantras as meditations to be practised by one already established in the knowledge of Brahman with its auxiliaries, not as a deathbed rite. The divine names refer to the Supreme Self, ‘directly or through the deity’, and their being names of one addressee is natural only on that reading (अत्र हि यमसूर्यादिशब्दानामेकविषयत्वं स्वरसगतं तथासत्येवोपपद्यते).

His reading of mantra 15 is the boldest departure from the solar imagery. ‘The true’ (satya) is the individual self (सत्यशब्दोऽत्र जीवपरः), its face is the mind, and the golden vessel is passion, golden because it attracts, which blocks the mind’s turn towards the Lord near in the heart. In mantra 16 the most beautiful form is the Lord’s divine form, and the five names become titles of his functions: the peerless seer, the inner controller, the one who impels his devotees’ minds. ‘He am I’ is meditation on the Lord as one’s own inner Self, the first person reaching through the individual self to its inner controller.

Mantra 17, for Deśika, first distinguishes the self from the body — the pure self is vāyu, anila and amṛta, immortal while the body becomes ash (अनन्तरं तु परिशुद्धात्मस्वरूपमुच्यते) — and then, with Om, names the Lord who impels both. kratu is the Lord in the form of the sacrifice; ‘remember’ asks him to regard the devotee with gracious mind. Mantra 18 addresses the Lord whose body is fire and asks him, who knows all the means, to lead those who have no other refuge. Deśika closes by summarising the end of the Saṃhitā as covering the highest reality, its glory, its worship and its fruit (एवं परतत्त्व-तद्विभूतियोग-तदुपासन-तत्फलविशेषान् सङ्गृह्य संहितेयं समपूर्यत).

Madhva: names of Hari

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva’s commentary on these mantras survives in the digital text with gaps, and much of it consists of verses he cites from texts he names — a Brahmāṇḍa, a Rāmasaṃhitā, a Brahmatarka, a Skānda. The edition reports these as cited by Madhva, without treating them as verified quotations of those works.

Within those limits his reading is consistent. The golden vessel is the sun’s disk, by which the face of Viṣṇu, the Real, is always covered (पात्रं हिरण्मयं सूर्यमण्डलं समुदाहृतम्); the names of mantra 16 are names of Viṣṇu (विष्णुरेकऋषिर्ज्ञेयो यमो नियमनाद्धरिः); in ‘so ’ham asmi’, aham and asmi are themselves names of Hari, who is distinct from every individual self (स्वयं तु सर्वजीवेभ्यो व्यतिरिक्तः परो हरिः); kratu and Agni are his names too (स क्रतुर्ज्ञानरूपत्वादग्निरङ्गप्रणेतृतः). The Lord’s ‘remembering’ is not recollection but his turning towards grace, since his knowledge is eternal. And the final verse is the prayer by which Manu praised Yajña for the wealth of liberation (नय नो मोक्षवित्तायेत्यस्तौद्यज्ञं मनुः स्वराड्).

Comparing the readings

Comparative Vedānta

The three schools differ less about the images than about who is speaking and to whom. For Śaṅkara the speaker is a dying meditator on the lower Brahman, and the prayers mark the limit of that path: they ask for a journey, and the knower of the Self makes none. For Deśika the speaker is a devotee practising meditation in life, and the prayers are part of the knowledge that liberates. For Madhva every name in them is a name of Viṣṇu, and the petitioner is his devotee.

The sharpest point of difference is the five words of mantra 16, yo ’sāv asau puruṣaḥ so ’ham asmi. Śaṅkara takes them as identity: सोऽहम् अस्मि भवामि. Deśika takes them as meditation on the Lord as one’s inner Self and rejects, by name, the reading that such sentences teach the identity of a self without attributes (ये पुनः 'तत्त्वमसि', 'सोऽहमस्मि' इत्यादिषु कार्यकारणोपाध्याकारविधूननेन निर्विशेषस्वरूपैक्यं वाक्यवेद्यमाहुः). Madhva removes the speaker from the sentence altogether by making aham and asmi names of the Lord. Each reading is internally coherent and each does some violence to one feature of the text: Śaṅkara’s to the context of prayer and petition, Deśika’s to the plain first person, Madhva’s to the ordinary sense of aham.

There is also common ground. All three read the four mantras as a unity, all three take the uncovering, the gathering of light, the remembering and the leading as gifts asked of a higher power rather than achievements, and all three understand the closing words of homage as the proper end of the Upaniṣad.

What the images are, and what they were made to mean

Comparative Vedānta

It helps to keep three layers apart. The first is the Vedic imagery: the sun’s disk and rays, Pūṣan the guide, the breath and the cremated body, fire and the path. These belong to the world of the Ṛgvedic sacrificer and of the funeral, and they need no further key. The second is the Upaniṣadic setting: placed after the teaching of mantras 1–14, and paralleled by the meditation on the person in the sun in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka, the images acquire a role in the passage of the soul at death — the door in the sun, the path that does not return. Śaṅkara and Mahīdhara read the mantras at this level.

The third layer is the symbolic transposition of the images into the inner life. Deśika’s golden lid of passion over the mind, his pure self called vāyu because it travels from body to body, his Agni as the Lord with fire as his body, are such transpositions; so, in another way, is Madhva’s reading of every name as a name of Hari. These readings are not arbitrary — each is argued from words and from other scriptures — but they are interpretations, and the text does not compel them. The same holds of modern readings that see in the golden vessel the attractions of the world or the ego: they are reflections that may illuminate the image, not its original sense.

One feature resists all three layers equally: the flat statement of mantra 17, ‘this body ends in ashes’. Śaṅkara reads it as a wish at a cremation (अथ इदं शरीरमग्नौ हुतं भस्मान्तं भस्मावशेषं भूयात्), Deśika as a statement about the body’s nature, Mahīdhara like Śaṅkara; but none softens it.

Reading the last prayers today

Contemporary Reflection

A modern reader may be surprised that a text so assured in its vision of the Self ends by asking to be led. The commentators each explain the surprise in their own way. Read outside their systems, the ending can be taken as a kind of honesty: whatever one has understood, death remains a passage one does not control, and the right attitude to it is the one the last mantra names — a request for guidance, a wish to be kept from the crooked turn, and thanks. This is a contemporary reflection, offered as such; it is not the teaching of any commentary.

Open questions

  • Does the Kāṇva Saṃhitā recite mantra 16 in the shorter form of witness K (beginning at tejaḥ, reading rūpe), or is that an omission of the digital text? A printed Kāṇva Saṃhitā with svara is needed.
  • What did the Mādhyandina klibe originally mean? The edition has only Mahīdhara’s derivation from klṛp.
  • Were the four prayers composed for the Īśā, or taken over from a funeral or meditation liturgy shared with Bṛhadāraṇyaka 5.15? The edition records the parallel and does not decide its direction.
  • Madhva’s digital text has gaps in its comment on mantras 15–16; a printed edition of his bhāṣya would fill them.
