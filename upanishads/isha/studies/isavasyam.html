Study I · Īśāvāsya Upaniṣad

Īśāvāsyam idaṃ sarvam

What does it mean for everything to be pervaded by the Lord?

Mantras 1, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8

The first words of the Upaniṣad do not say that everything is the Lord, nor even, strictly, that everything is pervaded by him: they say that all this is ‘to be’ covered or dwelt in by him. This study follows the grammar of that claim, the two roots hidden in one word, and the three ways the commentators read it — as the Self that covers an unreal world, as the Person who pervades and supports a real one, and as the Lord whose dwelling the world is — and asks how each avoids the naive claim that every object is God in every respect.

A task, not only a fact

Direct Textual Meaning

The mantra’s first half has no finite verb. Its predicate is vāsyam, a gerundive: a form that says what is to be, ought to be, or can be done to its subject. The sentence therefore reads, word for word, ‘by the Lord, to-be-covered (or: to-be-dwelt-in), this all, whatever moves in the moving [world]’. English translations that render it ‘all this is pervaded by the Lord’ turn a statement of what is to be done, or seen, into a report of how things are.

The difference matters for how the rest of the mantra hangs together. If the first half only described the world, the commands that follow — ‘you should enjoy’, ‘do not covet’ — would follow from it as conclusions from a premise. Read as a gerundive, the first half is itself the first of three instructions: see all this as covered or dwelt in by the Lord; with that given up, enjoy; do not covet. All three commentaries read it in this practical sense. Śaṅkara’s gloss is ‘to be covered’ (आच्छादनीयम्); Deśika gives ‘to be pervaded’ or ‘to be made to dwell’; Madhva gives ‘fit to be the dwelling’. None of them reads it as a simple description.

The scope is set by the relative clause: yat kiṃ ca jagatyāṃ jagat, ‘whatever moves in the moving one’. The world is named twice by the same word, jagat, ‘what goes’. Nothing is excepted; the stress falls on the world as perpetually changing, which is exactly what the next seven mantras will contrast with what does not change.

One word, two roots

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sanskrit has two roots of the form vas: vas ‘to clothe, cover’ (2nd class, vaste) and vas ‘to dwell’ (1st class, vasati). A gerundive vāsya can be formed from either. Mahīdhara, commenting on the same mantra in the Mādhyandina recension, chooses the first explicitly and gives the derivation: 'वस आच्छादने' 'ऋहलोर्ण्यत्' (पा० ३।१।१२४) इति ण्यत्प्रत्ययः — the root vas ‘to cover’, with the suffix ṇyat taught by P. 3.1.124 (ऋहलोर्ण्यत्). Śaṅkara’s gloss आच्छादनीयम् presupposes the same root.

Deśika keeps both open: वास्यम् - व्याप्यमित्यर्थः; सर्वाधारे स्वस्मिन् स्वेन वासनीयं वा — ‘to be pervaded; or, to be made to dwell in himself, the support of all’. The second option takes vāsya as the gerundive of the causative of vas ‘to dwell’. Madhva divides the letters differently again. Where the pada-pāṭha of the Mādhyandina Saṃhitā separates two words, ‘ई॒शा। वा॒स्य᳖म्।’, Madhva reads one compound, īśa + āvāsya: ईशस्यावासयोग्यमीशावास्यम्, ‘fit to be the dwelling of the Lord’.

The letters do not decide between these readings; the traditional pada-pāṭha of the Mādhyandina Saṃhitā (as printed in the witness used here) supports the division into two words, and that is the division this edition prints. The choice of root is a matter of interpretation. It is also a matter of image: covering is something done to a surface; dwelling is something done from within. The two images recur, in other forms, through the whole Upaniṣad.

Śaṅkara: the Self that covers an unreal world

Traditional Interpretation

For Śaṅkara the ‘Lord’ is not a being beside the world but the inner Self of every creature: ईशिता परमेश्वरः परमात्मा सर्वस्य । स हि सर्वमीष्टे सर्वजन्तूनामात्मा सन् प्रत्यगात्मतया. To ‘cover’ the world by him is to see it through the knowledge अहमेवेदं सर्वमिति, ‘I alone am all this’, so that what is false in it is hidden by what is true: परमार्थसत्यरूपेणानृतमिदं सर्वं चराचरमाच्छादनीयं स्वेन परमात्मना.

His image explains what kind of ‘covering’ he means. Sandalwood that has become damp acquires a bad smell; rubbing the wood brings out its own fragrance, which ‘covers’ the smell that was never the wood’s own. In the same way the world of doing and enjoying is superimposed on the Self — स्वात्मन्यध्यस्तं स्वाभाविकं कर्तृत्वभोक्तृत्वादिलक्षणं जगद्द्वैतरूपं — and is ‘covered’ when the Self is known. Nothing is added to the world; something false is no longer taken for true.

This is not the claim that every object is literally the Self in every respect. In Śaṅkara’s reading the name and form of a thing, its separateness and its belonging to someone, are exactly what is ‘covered’ and set aside. What remains is the one reality that the thing never was apart from. The vision has a practical consequence that Śaṅkara draws at once: for one who holds it, the right course is renunciation of the three desires, not ritual action — पुत्राद्येषणात्रयसंन्यासे एवाधिकारः, न कर्मसु.

Deśika: the Person who pervades and supports

Traditional Interpretation

Vedānta Deśika reads ‘this’ as everything other than the Lord, conscious and non-conscious alike: इदं - तत्तत्प्रमाणसिद्धमीश्वरव्यतिरिक्तं चिदचिदात्मकम्. The world is real, known by the ordinary means of knowledge, and it is distinct from the Lord. The Lord is the controller of all, ‘known as utterly different from the individual self’ (जीवादत्यन्तविलक्षणतया प्रख्यातेन सर्वनियन्त्रा पुरुषोत्तमेन).

Pervasion here is the relation of a support to what it supports and of an inner controller to what it controls. Deśika cites a verse that explains the name Vāsudeva by the same root: सर्वत्रासौ समस्तं च वसत्यत्रेति वै यतः । ततः स वासुदेवेति विद्वद्भिः परिपठ्यते — he dwells in everything and everything dwells in him. He also argues at length that īś in this mantra cannot mean Rudra, and identifies the Lord with Nārāyaṇa; that identification belongs to his school and is not in the words of the mantra.

On this reading the vision of mantra 1 removes a mistake of a different kind from the one Śaṅkara has in mind: not the mistake of taking the unreal for real, but the mistake of taking oneself and the world to be independent. Deśika calls the purpose of the opening ‘removing the delusion that the self is independent’ (स्वतन्त्रात्मभ्रमादिपरिजिहीर्षया).

Madhva: the world as the Lord’s dwelling

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva’s compound, īśāvāsyam, makes the world a dwelling fit for the Lord. His reason is dependence: nothing in the world can move or act by itself — स्वतः प्रवृत्यशक्तत्वादीशावास्यमिदं जगत्, in a verse he cites from a text he calls Brāhma — and so all its activity belongs to the Lord who dwells in it and moves it. Madhva reads jagatyām as ‘in prakṛti’, the material cause.

The Lord and the world remain distinct in every respect; Madhva’s school insists on that. What the mantra teaches, on his reading, is the world’s complete dependence, and the next clause follows from it: whatever one has is the Lord’s gift, and one should enjoy what he gives and not beg from another.

Sacred vision without a naive identity

Comparative Vedānta

A common modern summary of the mantra — ‘God is in everything’, or ‘everything is God’ — sounds like a single doctrine. The commentaries show three, and none of them says that each thing, as the thing it is, is identical with God. Śaṅkara says the one reality is present in everything and the separateness of things is not finally real: a pot is not God as a pot; its being is Brahman, and its pot-ness is name and form. Deśika says the Lord is in everything as its inner controller, and everything is in him as his body is in a self; the thing remains a thing and the Lord remains the Lord. Madhva says the world is the Lord’s dwelling and depends on him completely; the two are eternally different.

What they share is a direction of seeing. Each reading asks the reader to stop treating the world as a collection of independent objects available for possession. That shared direction is why all three can draw the same practical conclusion from the second half of the mantra — do not covet — while disagreeing about everything that grounds it.

The Upaniṣad itself gives the reader more to go on in mantras 4–8, which describe what the ‘Lord’ of mantra 1 is like without naming him again: unmoving yet swifter than the mind (4), within all this and outside all this (5), seen in all beings and all beings seen in it (6), the one in whom, for the knower, ‘all beings have become the self’ (7), bright, bodiless and pure (8). The paradoxes of 4–5 already rule out the simplest identity: what is ‘outside all this’ cannot simply be all this.

  • Bhagavad Gītā 9.4: मत्स्थानि सर्वभूतानि न चाहं तेष्ववस्थितः matsthāni sarvabhūtāni na cāhaṃ teṣvavasthitaḥ

Parallels in the Upaniṣads and the Gītā

Comparative Vedānta

The Chāndogya’s ‘all this is Brahman’ (3.14.1) is the parallel most often brought to mantra 1, but it is a statement of identity, not a gerundive of seeing; the closeness is doctrinal, and closest on Śaṅkara’s reading. The Gītā’s ‘all this world is pervaded by me in my unmanifest form’ (9.4) uses the same idaṃ sarvam and adds the qualification that mantras 4–5 also make: the beings are in him, and he is not in them. The Gītā’s description of the seer who sees ‘the supreme Lord standing equally in all beings, not perishing when they perish’ (13.27) is a doctrinal comparison for mantra 6 as much as for mantra 1.

  • Chāndogya Upaniṣad 3.14.1: सर्वं खल्विदं ब्रह्म sarvaṃ khalvidaṃ brahma
  • Bhagavad Gītā 9.4: मया ततमिदं सर्वं mayā tatamidaṃ sarvaṃ
  • Bhagavad Gītā 13.27: समं सर्वेषु भूतेषु तिष्ठन्तं परमेश्वरम् samaṃ sarveṣu bhūteṣu tiṣṭhantaṃ parameśvaram

A modern reflection: the house and its occupant

Contemporary Reflection

Readers who come to the Upaniṣad now often hear in its first line an ecological teaching: if the world is the dwelling of the Lord, it is not raw material. That reading is a modern application, not an interpretation found in the commentaries, and it should be offered as one. It does draw on something the mantra’s grammar really contains: vāsyam is an instruction about how to see, and how we see what we use shapes how we use it. A house one knows to be someone’s home is entered differently from a building one has bought to strip. Whether that someone is one’s own deepest Self, the Person who supports all things, or the Lord whose gift everything is, the commentaries agree on the posture: guest, not owner.

Open questions

  • Which root of vas the composer intended cannot be settled from the text; the pada-pāṭha settles only the word division.
  • Whether jagatyām means ‘on the earth’ (Śaṅkara), ‘in the three worlds’ (Mahīdhara) or ‘in prakṛti’ (Madhva) affects the scope of the vision; the mantra gives no further clue.
