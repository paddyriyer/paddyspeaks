Sambhūti and Asambhūti

Becoming, non-becoming and perishing in mantras 12–14

Mantras 12, 13, 14

Mantras 12–14 repeat the pattern of the vidyā triad with a new pair of terms, and then, in the resolving verse, change one of the terms. Every interpretation of the triad is an answer to that change. This study sets out what the words and the witnesses allow, how Śaṅkara, Mahīdhara, Vedānta Deśika and Madhva read the pair, and what remains open.

The problem the triad poses

Direct Textual Meaning

Mantra 12 declares that those who worship asambhūti, ‘non-becoming’, enter blind darkness, and that those who delight in sambhūti, ‘becoming’, enter a darkness greater still. Mantra 13 says that the wise declared one result from sambhava and another from asambhava, and that the speakers have received this from them. Mantra 14 then praises the one who knows ‘both together’: by one he crosses death, by the other he attains the deathless.

Read only for its surface, the triad teaches that devotion to either of two opposites, held alone, leads to darkness, and that knowing them together leads beyond death. That much is uncontroversial. Three things are not. The text never says what sambhūti and asambhūti are. It ranks the apparently better object — becoming, sambhūti — as the cause of greater darkness. And in mantra 14, where the reader expects sambhūtiṃ cāsambhūtiṃ ca, the pair is सम्भूतिं च विनाशं च: becoming and perishing, not becoming and non-becoming.

The last point is the hinge of the whole debate. If the pair in 14 is the same pair as in 12, then either vināśa is a name for asambhūti, or a negative prefix has been lost somewhere. If it is a different pair, the triad does not simply resolve its own opposition, and the reader must say why the terms shift.

Two triads, two orders

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The sambhūti triad is built on the same template as the vidyā triad. Mantra 12 differs from mantra 9 only in its objects; mantra 13 differs from mantra 10 only in the ablatives sambhavāt and asambhavāt where 10 has the instrumentals vidyayā and avidyayā; mantra 14 differs from mantra 11 only in its pair of terms. The first verse of the template is older than both triads in one sense at least: it stands in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka (4.4.10) as a single verse about vidyā and avidyā, without the two verses that follow it in the Īśā.

The two recensions of the Śukla Yajurveda arrange the triads differently. In the Kāṇva text, the one edited here and the one Śaṅkara, Madhva and Deśika comment on, the vidyā triad comes first (9–11) and the sambhūti triad second (12–14). In the Mādhyandina Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā the order is reversed: the sambhūti triad is VS 40.9–11 and the vidyā triad VS 40.12–14. Mahīdhara, commenting on the Mādhyandina text, therefore meets the sambhūti verses first, and introduces them as the beginning of six anuṣṭubh mantras on worship: अतः परमुपासनामन्त्रा उच्यन्ते षडनुष्टुभः.

The difference in order matters less than it seems for the meaning of the terms, but it is a caution against arguments that rest on sequence alone. An interpretation that makes the sambhūti triad a development of the vidyā triad, or the reverse, has to reckon with a recension in which the other order is the received one. The edition records the Mādhyandina order in corrections.json as a recension difference and does not treat either as the original.

What the words allow

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

sambhūti is formed from the root bhū, ‘to become, be’, with the preverb sam, ‘together’, and the suffix -ti that makes feminine action nouns (P. 3.3.94 (स्त्रियां क्तिन्)): ‘coming together into being, origination’. asambhūti is its negative, ‘non-origination’. sambhava in mantra 13 is a masculine noun from the same root and preverb with much the same sense; the change of word in 13 may be metrical, since the ablative of sambhūti would add a syllable to the half-line. vināśa, in mantra 14, is from vi + naś, ‘to perish’: ‘perishing, destruction’.

None of these is a technical term in the Upaniṣad itself. They can denote processes (coming to be, perishing), states (the manifest, the unmanifest), or, by a common shift, the things that have those properties (what comes to be, what perishes). Each commentator exploits one of these possibilities, and the grammar does not exclude any of them.

The pada-pāṭha printed with the Mādhyandina witness divides asam-bhūtim in VS 40.9 (mantra 12), and सम्भू॑ति॒मिति॒ सम्ऽभू॑तिम् and वि॒ना॒शमिति॑ विऽना॒शम् in VS 40.11 (mantra 14): asambhūti in the first verse, sambhūti and vināśa in the third. This is the analysis of a reciting tradition and an editor, not of a commentator. It shows that the text as transmitted has no negative in mantra 14, and that a reading which supplies one is an interpretation of the received letters, not a variant reading of them.

Śaṅkara: cause, effect and a hidden a-

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara reads the triad as concerned with two kinds of meditative worship, both within the world, and introduces mantra 12 as blame of each in order to commend their combination: अधुना व्याकृताव्याकृतोपासनयोः समुच्चिचीषया प्रत्येकं निन्दोच्यते. sambhūti is what comes into being — he turns the action noun into the thing that has it: सम्भवनं सम्भूतिः सा यस्य कार्यस्य सा सम्भूतिः — namely the effect-Brahman, Hiraṇyagarbha (कार्यब्रह्मणि हिरण्यगर्भाख्ये). asambhūti is what is other than that: the unmanifest cause, prakṛti, the seed of desire and action.

Mantra 13 then distributes the results. From the worship of Hiraṇyagarbha come powers, beginning with the power of becoming minute (अणिमाद्यैश्वर्यलक्षणम्). From the worship of the unmanifest comes what mantra 12 called blind darkness and what, Śaṅkara says, the Purāṇic writers call dissolution into prakṛti (प्रकृतिलय इति च पौराणिकैरुच्यते).

With these identifications, mantra 14 cannot be read as it stands. vināśa, ‘perishing’, Śaṅkara takes as the effect, the thing whose property is perishing (विनाशो धर्मो यस्य कार्यस्य) — that is, Hiraṇyagarbha, already called sambhūti in 12. The other member of the pair must therefore be the unmanifest, and the text’s sambhūti must stand for asambhūti. He states this as a rule of reading, and gives its ground: ‘सम्भूतिं च विनाशं च’ इत्यत्रावर्णलोपेन निर्देशो द्रष्टव्यः, प्रकृतिलयफलश्रुत्यनुरोधात् — the expression is to be understood with an elided a, in conformity with the scripture that states dissolution into prakṛti as the result.

The resolution follows. By the worship of Hiraṇyagarbha one gains powers and so crosses ‘death’, which here means the lack of power and the faults of unrighteousness and desire; by the worship of the unmanifest one attains an ‘immortality’ that is dissolution into prakṛti (असम्भूत्या अव्याकृतोपासनया अमृतं प्रकृतिलयलक्षणम् अश्नुते). That ‘immortality’ is relative. For Śaṅkara the liberation the Upaniṣad teaches is the knowledge of the Self of mantras 6–8, not the fruit of any combination of worships. The triad, on his reading, describes the best that meditation within the world can achieve, and thereby marks its limit.

The letters and the restoration

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

How much does Śaṅkara’s a- cost? In the second half-line, nothing. tīrtvā + asambhūtyā and tīrtvā + sambhūtyā are written with the same letters, because a final ā and an initial a merge into ā (P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः)). Only an avagraha would distinguish them, and the witnesses write none at this junction; the received तीर्त्वा सम्भूत्या is genuinely ambiguous.

In the first half-line the case is different. sambhūtiṃ is the first word of the verse; nothing precedes it into which an a- could have merged. Here the restoration is not a choice between two readings of the same letters but an instruction to understand a word that is not written. Śaṅkara calls it an elision of the vowel a, and justifies it, not by any manuscript, but by the coherence of the teaching.

Mahīdhara shows that the tradition was not agreed even on where the elision lies. His second explanation of the Mādhyandina verse (VS 40.11) follows Śaṅkara’s identifications but places the a- in vināśa instead: अत्र विनाशशब्दद्वये अवर्णलोपो द्रष्टव्यः पृषोदरादित्वात्. He treats the form as one of the irregular words that grammar accepts as they are taught (P. 6.3.109 (पृषोदरादीनि यथोपदिष्टम्)), reads avināśa, ‘the imperishable’, as the unmanifest, and so allocates the results differently: by the worship of the unmanifest (अविनाशेनाव्याकृतोपासनेन) one crosses death, and by the worship of Hiraṇyagarbha one attains the immortality of dissolution into prakṛti.

Two scholars, one interpretive need, two places for the missing vowel: the variation is itself evidence that the restoration answers to the interpreters’ systems rather than to the transmitted text. That is not an argument against it — commentators in every tradition read texts in the light of the whole — but it is a reason to keep the received letters in the edition and to report the restorations as readings.

One witness difference bears on the half-line. Madhva’s mūla, as keyed in the digital source, lacks the second ca (सम्भूतिं च विनाशं यस्तद्), which leaves the half-line a syllable short of the anuṣṭubh. corrections.json classifies it for review against a printed text; nothing in the interpretation depends on it.

Mahīdhara: two explanations

Traditional Interpretation

Mahīdhara gives every verse of the triad two explanations, and his first is independent of Śaṅkara. On VS 40.9 (= mantra 12) asambhūti is the view that the dead are not born again — मृतस्य पुनः संभवो नास्ति — which he ascribes to the Bauddhas, and sambhūti is exclusive devotion to the self, by people who reject works and deny any relation between the ritual and the knowledge portions of the Veda. Both groups are blamed. This is a polemical application, and the edition reports it as his.

On VS 40.11 (= mantra 14) his first explanation reads the pair without any elision. sambhūti is the supreme Brahman, the one cause of the world’s origin (सर्वजगत्संभवैकहेतुं परं ब्रह्म); vināśa is the body, whose property is perishing (विनाशधर्मकं शरीरम्). The one who knows the embodied self and the body together, does desireless works with the body; by the perishable body he crosses death — the impurity of the mind — and by knowledge of the self he attains liberation.

His second explanation, introduced each time as another sense of the verse (अस्या ऋचोऽर्थान्तरम्), takes up Śaṅkara’s identifications of the unmanifest and Hiraṇyagarbha, with the elision moved to vināśa. Mahīdhara’s willingness to set the two side by side, without deciding between them, is a useful model for a reader: the verses support more than one coherent reading, and a commentator can say so.

Vedānta Deśika: two stages of one path

Traditional Interpretation

Deśika reads the triad not as a pair of worships within the world but as the teaching that two contemplations, combined, are a limb of the knowledge of Brahman: the removal of obstacles and the experience of Brahman. sambhūti is the attainment of Brahman (ब्रह्मप्राप्तिरूपा सम्भूतिरुक्ता), the sense that the verb abhi-sam-bhū has in the Upaniṣadic passages he quotes. asambhūti, ‘what is not that attainment’, refers to the destruction of the obstacles close to it (तदासन्नप्रतिबन्धविनाशम्) — which is why mantra 14 can call it vināśa without any emendation.

He argues against the alternatives. asambhūti does not mean the non-arising or the destruction of sambhūti: न चात्रासम्भूतिशब्देन सम्भूतेरनुत्पत्तिः विनाशो वा प्रतिपाद्यः, since a mere absence of the goal could not be named as the means of crossing death. Nor do sambhūti and vināśa here mean creation and dissolution; read so, he says, the division of results into crossing death and attaining immortality would not have the least fitness.

On mantra 14 he offers two senses of vināśa: the destruction of the obstacle, or, more concretely, the destruction of pride and hypocrisy, of violence and theft, and of the senses turned outward (मानदम्भादीनां, हिंसास्तेयादीनां बहिर्मुखेन्द्रियवृत्तीनां च विनाशो विवक्षितः). Practised as an auxiliary, this removes the sin that hinders meditation; and sambhūti, as the perfection of meditation, brings the attainment of Brahman itself.

Deśika’s reading keeps every letter of the text and makes the triad a positive teaching about the path, at the price of a sense for asambhūti that is not its plainest one. Its strength is that it explains why vināśa can stand in 14 where asambhūti stood in 12.

Madhva: the Lord who creates and destroys

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva comments on the three mantras together, and in the digital source used here his comment survives only from a point marked with an ellipsis; what remains is a series of verses he cites from a text he names Kaurma (इति कौर्मे). They speak of those who do not accept Hari’s creatorship and of those who do not accept his being the destroyer, and say that both go to dreadful darkness. The Lord is to be known as the maker of all and the destroyer of all (सर्वकर्तारमीशेशं सर्वसंहारकारकम्); by knowing his destroying, one is freed from the bondage of the body, and by knowing him as the agent of bliss and knowledge one attains their manifestation.

On this reading the triad concerns right belief about the Lord’s powers. To accept only one power — creation without destruction, or the reverse — is a partial view that ends in darkness; to know both together is liberating. The same verses insist that the released are never equal to Viṣṇu, and that liberation comes from knowing his supremacy: a characteristically Dvaita conclusion drawn from the triad.

Because the beginning of the comment, where Madhva would have glossed the terms of mantra 12, is missing, the edition marks the Dvaita reading of 12 and 13 as reconstructed from the surviving verses. A printed edition of Madhva’s bhāṣya with Rāghavendra’s commentary should be consulted before the gloss is stated more precisely.

Comparing the readings

Comparative Vedānta

Each reading answers the hinge-question — why vināśa in mantra 14 — in a different way. Śaṅkara identifies vināśa with sambhūti’s object, the perishable effect, and so must find asambhūti in the word the text spells sambhūti. Mahīdhara’s second explanation, accepting the same identifications, finds the negative in vināśa instead. Deśika takes vināśa as the destruction of obstacles, which is what asambhūti meant all along, and needs no restoration. Mahīdhara’s first explanation and Madhva take the pair in 14 as a fresh pair — Brahman and the body, or the Lord’s creating and destroying — and need none either.

They also differ on what is at stake. For Śaṅkara the triad lies wholly below liberation: its ‘immortality’ is dissolution into prakṛti, and its value is to show what meditation without Self-knowledge can and cannot reach. For Deśika, Madhva and Mahīdhara’s first explanation, the triad describes a path that ends in liberation. The question of the terms is thus bound up with the larger question the Īśā raises in mantras 2 and 9–11: whether action and meditation can be combined with knowledge, and whether such a combination liberates.

Neither the grammar nor the witnesses settle the matter. The received letters and the pada-pāṭha favour the readings that restore nothing; the strict parallel with mantra 11 and the consistency of the cosmic identifications favour Śaṅkara. A study edition can say which reading keeps which feature of the text. It cannot, without going beyond its sources, say which reading is the Upaniṣad’s.

A reflection: holding both

Contemporary Reflection

Whatever sambhūti and vināśa denote, the triad’s shape is instructive. Exclusive attachment to either term of a pair is called darkness, and the higher term is not exempt. Then the resolving verse asks for knowledge of ‘both together’. A modern reader may hear in this a caution against every one-sided devotion — to growth without loss, to permanence without change, to beginnings without endings. That is a reflection on the form of the teaching, offered as such, and not a claim about what the commentators meant.
