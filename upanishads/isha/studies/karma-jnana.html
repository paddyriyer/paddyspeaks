Study III · Īśāvāsya Upaniṣad

Karma and Jñāna

Action, renunciation and knowledge in mantras 1–2 and 9–11

Mantras 1, 2, 9, 10, 11

The Īśā is the last chapter of a Saṃhitā otherwise devoted to ritual, and it speaks both of renouncing and of doing works for a hundred years. This study sets out what the mantras say about action and knowledge, and then how Śaṅkara, Vedānta Deśika, Madhva and Mahīdhara relate the two — the point on which the Vedānta schools divide most sharply.

What the mantras say

Direct Textual Meaning

Five mantras bear directly on the question. Mantra 1 gives a vision and two commands: all this is to be covered, or dwelt in, by the Lord; ‘by that which is given up’ one should enjoy; one should not covet. Mantra 2 speaks of action without qualification: कुर्वन्नेवेह कर्माणि जिजीविषेच्छतं समाः — ‘doing works here, one should wish to live a hundred years’ — and adds that for ‘you’ there is no other way, and that action does not cling to a person.

Mantras 9–11 then use a different vocabulary. Those who worship avidyā enter blind darkness; those who delight in vidyā enter a greater darkness, as it were (9). The result of vidyā is said to be one thing, the result of avidyā another — so ‘we have heard from the wise’ (10). Whoever knows both together crosses death by avidyā and attains the immortal by vidyā (11): अविद्यया मृत्युं तीर्त्वा विद्ययामृतमश्नुते.

Three things are worth noticing before any commentary. First, the Upaniṣad never uses the words karma-yoga, jñāna-yoga or saṃnyāsa; the only words for action are karma (twice in mantra 2) and the verbs of doing. Second, mantra 11’s ‘both together’ (ubhayaṃ saha) is said of knowing (veda), not of practising; whether knowing both together means practising both is already an interpretation. Third, the Upaniṣad does not say what vidyā and avidyā are. That silence is where the schools begin.

Where the chapter stands

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The setting of the text shapes the question. In both recensions of the Śukla Yajurveda the Īśā is the fortieth and last chapter of the Saṃhitā; the thirty-nine before it give the mantras of the ritual. Mahīdhara opens his comment on the chapter with exactly this: एकोनचत्वारिंशताध्यायैः कर्मकाण्डं निरूपितम् । इदानीं कर्माचरणशुद्धान्तःकरणं प्रति ज्ञानकाण्डमेकेनाध्यायेन निरूप्यते — the ritual portion has been set out in thirty-nine chapters; now the portion on knowledge is set out, in one chapter, for one whose mind has been purified by performing the ritual.

Śaṅkara, introducing the Upaniṣad, makes the opposite point from the same fact: these mantras are not used in ritual at all, because they reveal the Self, which is not something to be produced, changed, reached or purified by action — न ह्येवंलक्षणमात्मनो याथात्म्यम् उत्पाद्यं विकार्यम् आप्यं संस्कार्यं वा कर्तृभोक्तृरूपं वा, येन कर्मशेषता स्यात्. The chapter’s position at the end of the ritual Saṃhitā can be read as completion (the ritual leads here) or as contrast (here the ritual stops). Most of what follows is a working-out of those two readings.

Śaṅkara: two paths, one mountain

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara divides the opening mantras by the hearer’s eligibility. Mantra 1 is for the knower of the Self, whose renunciation of the three desires is the way to protect the Self: एवमात्मविदः पुत्राद्येषणात्रयसंन्यासेनात्मज्ञाननिष्ठतया आत्मा रक्षितव्य इत्येष वेदार्थः. Mantra 2 is for the other: अथेतरस्य अनात्मज्ञतयात्मग्रहणायाशक्तस्य इदमुपदिशति मन्त्रः. For such a person the prescribed rites, performed for a lifetime, are the only way by which inauspicious action does not cling.

The justification is a principle he states as if it needed no defence: ज्ञानकर्मणोर्विरोधं पर्वतवदकम्प्यं यथोक्तं न स्मरसि किम्? — ‘Do you not remember the opposition of knowledge and action, unshakeable as a mountain?’ Action requires a doer who wants a result; knowledge of the Self removes the notion of being a doer. The two cannot belong to one person at one time. He supports the division with the Mokṣadharma verse on the two paths, द्वाविमावथ पन्थानौ यत्र वेदाः प्रतिष्ठिताः, and with the Taittirīya saying that renunciation surpassed the rest.

Before mantra 9 he summarises the plan of the whole Upaniṣad in these terms. Mantra 1 gave the first sense of the Veda, devotion to knowledge through giving up all desire; mantra 2 gave the second, devotion to works for the ignorant who wish to live. Mantras 3–8 teach the Self to renouncers; mantras 9 onward are for those who desire, who do works and wish to live: ये तु कामिनः कर्मनिष्ठाः कर्म कुर्वन्त एव जिजीविषवः, तेभ्य इदमुच्यते — ‘ अन्धं तमः’. Self-knowledge, he says, no one who is not confused would wish to combine with any action or other knowledge — तन्न केनचित्कर्मणा ज्ञानान्तरेण वा ह्यमूढः समुच्चिचीषति.

Śaṅkara on vidyā and avidyā: a combination, but not with Self-knowledge

Traditional Interpretation

Given that principle, mantras 9–11 cannot for Śaṅkara be about Self-knowledge and action. avidyā is action — विद्याया अन्या अविद्या कर्मेत्यर्थः — and vidyā is knowledge of deities, the meditation that accompanies ritual: तद्दैवं वित्तं देवताविषयं ज्ञानं कर्मसम्बन्धित्वेनोपन्यस्तं न परमात्मज्ञानम्. The censure in mantra 9 of each taken alone is intended to recommend their combination (समुच्चिचीषया); each has its own result, as the Bṛhadāraṇyaka says: ‘कर्मणा पितृलोकः, विद्यया देवलोकः’.

Mantra 11 then describes the combined practitioner. By works he crosses ‘death’, which Śaṅkara glosses as natural action and knowledge (स्वाभाविकं कर्म ज्ञानं च मृत्युशब्दवाच्यम्); by meditation on deities he reaches ‘the immortal’, which here means identity with a deity: तद्ध्यमृतमुच्यते, यद्देवतात्मगमनम्. The immortality of mantra 11 is relative, not liberation.

Śaṅkara returns to the question at the end of his commentary, because, as he says, some people doubt this reading after hearing mantras 11 and 14. Why not take vidyā as knowledge of the Self and immortality as liberation, and allow scripture to reconcile knowledge with works, as it reconciles ‘do not harm’ with the sacrificial killing? His answer cites the Kaṭha — ‘दूरमेते विपरीते विषूची अविद्या या च विद्येति ज्ञाता’ — and argues that knowledge and ignorance differ in cause, nature and result, cannot coexist in one person at once, and cannot follow each other in one person either, since once knowledge arises ignorance has no place. Mantra 7 confirms it: for the one who sees oneness there is no delusion. Finally, if vidyā were Self-knowledge, the prayer for a path in mantras 15–18 would make no sense. So: तस्मात् उपासनया समुच्चयो न परमात्मविज्ञानेनेति यथाव्याख्यात एव मन्त्राणामर्थ इत्युपरम्यते.

  • Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.2.4: दूरमेते विपरीते विषूची dūramete viparīte viṣūcī
  • Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.5.16: कर्मणा पितृलोको विद्यया देवलोको karmaṇā pitṛloko vidyayā devaloko

The same mantra in the Brahmasūtra commentary

Traditional Interpretation

Mantra 2 has a second life in the Brahmasūtra, in the section (3.4) that asks whether knowledge of Brahman is independent of ritual or merely an auxiliary to it. Under sūtra 3.4.7, नियमाच्च, Śaṅkara presents the opponent who cites Īśā 2 among the texts that bind a person to works for life, and concludes that knowledge is therefore subordinate to works: नियमादपि कर्मशेषत्वमेव विद्याया इति.

The answer comes in two sūtras. Under 3.4.13, नाविशेषात्, the injunction to live doing works is said not to be specific to the knower: न विदुष इति विशेषोऽस्ति, अविशेषेण नियमविधानात्. Under 3.4.14, स्तुतयेऽनुमतिर्वा, Śaṅkara grants for argument’s sake that the context might make the knower the subject, and then reads the permission to act as praise of knowledge: even acting all his life, the knower is not touched by action — the sense, he says, of ‘न कर्म लिप्यते नरे’. This is a second Advaita reading of mantra 2, different from the Upaniṣad commentary’s division by eligibility, though it reaches the same conclusion: works do not produce liberation.

  • Śaṅkara on Brahmasūtra 3.4.7: नियमादपि कर्मशेषत्वमेव विद्याया niyamādapi karmaśeṣatvameva vidyāyā
  • Śaṅkara on Brahmasūtra 3.4.13: न विदुष इति विशेषोऽस्ति na viduṣa iti viśeṣo'sti
  • Śaṅkara on Brahmasūtra 3.4.14: विद्यास्तुतये कर्मानुज्ञान vidyāstutaye karmānujñāna

Vedānta Deśika: works as the limb of knowledge, for life

Traditional Interpretation

Deśika reads the same mantras with the opposite starting point. Mantra 2 is addressed to the knower: prescribed daily and occasional works, done without attachment to results or the sense of being the doer, as an auxiliary of knowledge, are to be done for life — एवं विदुषः फलसङ्गकर्तृत्वादित्यागयुक्तं नित्यनैमित्तिकरूपं (विद्याङ्गभूतं) कर्म यावज्जीवमनुष्ठेयमित्याह. He cites the same sūtras as Śaṅkara, ‘नाविशेषात्’ and ‘स्तुतयेऽनुमतिर्वा’, and quotes the Bhāṣya on 3.4.14, which in his tradition is Rāmānuja’s: the permission to perform works always is given to praise knowledge, since by the greatness of knowledge one is not stained by works even while always doing them. The words are close to Śaṅkara’s; the conclusion is not, for Deśika insists that works are never to be abandoned and rejects the view that the knower stands outside injunction and prohibition: ज्ञानाग्निदग्धाधिकारो विधिनिषेधानधिकारीति पक्षस्तु न वेदवित्सम्मतः.

On mantras 9–11 he follows the same line. avidyā is action — mere action without knowledge, in mantra 9 — and those who hold to it alone, or to knowledge alone while abandoning the works proper to their station, are both censured. In mantra 11 vidyā is meditation on the supreme Self and avidyā the works that are its limb: विद्याम् - परमात्मोपासनरूपाम्, अविद्याम् - तदङ्गभूतकर्मात्मिकां. ‘Death’ is the past karma that contracts knowledge, crossed by works enjoined as auxiliaries of knowledge; ‘the immortal’ is the supreme Self, reached by knowledge. Here too he cites his predecessor: इह त्वविद्याशब्दः प्रकरणादौचित्याच्च विद्याङ्गकर्मविषय इत्यभाषि भाष्यकारैः.

Deśika also argues against two readings he does not attribute to anyone by name: one that takes ‘by avidyā having crossed death’ as ‘having reached death by avidyā’, and one that finds here two means (knowledge and works) and two separate results (crossing death, attaining immortality). The second is close to the structure of Śaṅkara’s reading of mantra 11; Deśika’s alternative is a single result, attained by knowledge, with works related to it as limb to principal (कर्मज्ञानयोरङ्गाङ्गिभावं विद्ययैव मृत्युतरणं च).

Madhva and Mahīdhara

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva reads mantra 2 as a warning against inaction: अकुर्वतः कर्म न लिप्यत इति नास्ति — it is not true that action does not cling to one who does not act. Through verses he cites as from the Nāradīya, the knower too must always worship, since omitting it diminishes his bliss. On mantras 9–11 his verses shift the terms from action and knowledge to right and wrong knowledge of the Lord: those who worship wrongly go to blind darkness; those who know Nārāyaṇa as he is, together with the censure of what is false, cross sorrow and ignorance and attain bliss and knowledge (तस्माद्यथास्वरूपं तु नारायणमनामयम् । अयथार्थस्य निन्दां च ये विदुः सह सज्जनाः). In this reading ‘both together’ is knowledge of the true together with the rejection of the false.

Mahīdhara, commenting on the Mādhyandina chapter, stands close to Śaṅkara in wording but closer to a ritualist in emphasis. Mantra 2 is for one unable to attain Self-knowledge (आत्मज्ञानाशक्तस्य कर्मोपदेशमाह), but the works he means are desireless works that lead to liberation by purifying the mind (निष्कामानि मुक्तिहेतुकानि); he gives Śaṅkara’s reading only as an alternative. On the vidyā mantras he repeats Śaṅkara’s identifications — avidyā is ritual, vidyā knowledge of deities, the result identity with a deity — and adds a sentence that sums up his view of the whole Saṃhitā: कर्मकाण्डं ज्ञानकाण्डस्य गुणभूतमित्यर्थः, ‘the ritual portion is subordinate to the knowledge portion’.

Where the readings divide

Comparative Vedānta

Set side by side, the readings differ on four questions, and the differences follow from each school’s view of the self. Who is mantra 2 for? For Śaṅkara, the person not yet fit for renunciation; for Deśika and Madhva, everyone, the knower included. What are vidyā and avidyā in 9–11? For Śaṅkara, meditation on deities and ritual; for Deśika, meditation on the supreme Self and the works that are its limb; for Madhva, true knowledge of the Lord and the knowledge (and censure) of what is false. What is ‘the immortal’ of mantra 11? For Śaṅkara, the relative immortality of becoming a deity; for Deśika, the supreme Self itself, reached in liberation. Can knowledge and works be combined? For Śaṅkara, only works with meditation, never with knowledge of the Self (उपासनया समुच्चयो न परमात्मविज्ञानेनेति); for Deśika, works serve knowledge for life, as limb to principal.

Behind these lies one difference. For Advaita, knowledge of the Self removes the very standpoint — a doer seeking a result — from which action is possible, so there is nothing for action to add; the opposition is ‘like a mountain’. For Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita, the knower remains a real self, eternally dependent on the Lord, and action offered to him is part of what knowledge is in practice. The Brahmasūtra passage on Īśā 2 is shared ground: both Śaṅkara and Deśika read ‘न कर्म लिप्यते नरे’ as praise of knowledge. They disagree on whether that praise implies that works can be laid down.

None of these positions can be read off the Sanskrit alone. The mantras allow each, and each commentator supplies, from the rest of scripture and his system, the identifications the mantras do not make. A reader should keep the two levels apart: the text recommends a life of work in which action does not cling, and says that vidyā and avidyā known together lead beyond death; the schools say what those words mean.

The grammar of ‘together’ and ‘by’

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Two grammatical points carry much of the argument. The first is saha in mantra 11: yas tad vedobhayaṃ saha, ‘who knows that, both together’. saha can qualify the object (‘both, as a pair’) or the verb (‘knows them together’, at one time, in one person). Śaṅkara and Mahīdhara take it as combined practice by one person; Deśika insists that knowing both together does not mean both are equally to be adopted, only that the limb and the principal are both to be performed.

The second is the case of vidyayā and avidyayā in mantra 10. The Kāṇva text reads instrumentals — ‘one thing, they say, by vidyā; another by avidyā’ — which Śaṅkara construes as ‘one result is produced by vidyā’. Deśika finds the instrumentals hard to construe with anyat, ‘other’, and takes them in the sense of ablatives: अत्र व्यत्ययानुशासनात् पञ्चम्यर्थे तृतीया, अन्यथा अनन्वयात् — ‘[the means of liberation is] other than vidyā, other than avidyā’. He supports it from the parallel of mantra 13, अन्यदेवाहुस्सम्भवात्, which has ablatives. The Mādhyandina recension has ablatives here too (VS 40.13: विद्यायाः … अविद्यायाः), so Deśika’s construction, argued from the Kāṇva text, coincides with the reading of the other recension.

Finally, the instrumentals of mantra 11 — avidyayā mṛtyuṃ tīrtvā vidyayāmṛtam aśnute — pair a means with each result. Whether there are two results (Śaṅkara, Mahīdhara) or one result with a preparatory step (Deśika) is a question about how the absolutive tīrtvā, ‘having crossed’, relates to the main verb aśnute, ‘attains’: as a separate achievement, or as the removal of an obstacle to the one achievement.

A modern reflection

Contemporary Reflection

Readers today often meet these mantras with a question the commentators did not ask in this form: can one live a full working life — family, profession, public responsibility — and still pursue the deepest questions? The commentators’ answers are not simply yes or no. One tradition says that the deepest knowledge is incompatible with the sense of being a doer, and that a life of work, rightly done, prepares for it. Others say that rightly done work is never left behind, because it is part of how a dependent self lives toward what it depends on. Both treat the quality of action — without greed, without the clinging of mantra 2 — as the common ground. That is perhaps the part of the debate a modern reader can take up first, whatever view one comes to hold of the rest. This reflection is the editor’s, not the commentators’.

Open questions

  • Deśika quotes ‘the Bhāṣya’ on Brahmasūtra 3.4.14 and ‘the bhāṣyakāra’ on avidyā in mantra 11; these are attributed to Rāmānuja by the tradition, but the Śrībhāṣya is not among this edition’s sources and the quotations have not been checked against it.
  • The readings Deśika rejects in mantra 11 are not attributed by him to anyone; the resemblance of the second to Śaṅkara’s structure is the editor’s observation.
  • Madhva’s verses on mantras 9–11 are given without a named source in the keyed text, and the keyed passage is abbreviated (यान्ति ...); a printed text of his bhāṣya is needed.
  • Whether saha in mantra 11 qualifies the object or the act of knowing cannot be settled from the mantra alone.
