Īśāvāsya Upaniṣad

Special philosophical studies

The central interpretive problems of the Upaniṣad, each with the Sanskrit, the commentators in their own words, and the open questions.

  1. I. Īśāvāsyam idaṃ sarvam

    What does it mean for everything to be pervaded by the Lord?

    The first words of the Upaniṣad do not say that everything is the Lord, nor even, strictly, that everything is pervaded by him: they say that all this is ‘to be’ covered or dwelt in by him. This study follows the grammar of that claim, the two roots hidden in one word, and the three ways the commentators read it — as the Self that covers an unreal world, as the Person who pervades and supports a real one, and as the Lord whose dwelling the world is — and asks how each avoids the naive claim that every object is God in every respect.

  2. II. Tena tyaktena bhuñjīthāḥ

    Four words, five readings: the grammar and philosophy of a celebrated phrase

    ‘Renounce and enjoy’ is the best-known rendering of the second half of mantra 1, and it is not the reading of any of the four commentaries used here. This study takes the phrase word by word — what tena refers to, what kind of instrumental tyaktena is, what bhuñjīthāḥ can mean in the middle voice — sets out the readings of Śaṅkara, Mahīdhara, Deśika and Madhva side by side, and closes with mā gṛdhaḥ kasya svid dhanam, which can be heard in two ways.

  3. III. Karma and Jñāna

    Action, renunciation and knowledge in mantras 1–2 and 9–11

    The Īśā is the last chapter of a Saṃhitā otherwise devoted to ritual, and it speaks both of renouncing and of doing works for a hundred years. This study sets out what the mantras say about action and knowledge, and then how Śaṅkara, Vedānta Deśika, Madhva and Mahīdhara relate the two — the point on which the Vedānta schools divide most sharply.

  4. IV. Vidyā and Avidyā

    Mantras 9–11: why the Upaniṣad warns against knowledge, and what its commentators think ‘knowledge’ means here

    Three mantras warn against devotion to avidyā, warn more sharply against devotion to vidyā, and then commend knowing both together. None of the four commentaries used in this edition takes the words in their modern everyday sense of ‘knowledge’ and ‘ignorance’; this study sets out what each takes them to mean, what the grammar allows, and where the readings divide.

  5. V. Sambhūti and Asambhūti

    Becoming, non-becoming and perishing in mantras 12–14

    Mantras 12–14 repeat the pattern of the vidyā triad with a new pair of terms, and then, in the resolving verse, change one of the terms. Every interpretation of the triad is an answer to that change. This study sets out what the words and the witnesses allow, how Śaṅkara, Mahīdhara, Vedānta Deśika and Madhva read the pair, and what remains open.

  6. VI. The Final Prayers

    Mantras 15–18: the golden vessel, the gathered light, the breath and the ashes, and Agni on the good path

    The Īśā ends not with a doctrine but with four prayers in the voice of a petitioner, the same four that close the Bṛhadāraṇyaka (5.15.1), the last of them a verse of the Ṛgveda. This study separates what the Vedic images say — a golden vessel over a face, rays withdrawn, breath returning to wind, a body ending in ashes, a fire that knows the way — from what the commentators made of them, and sets out the textual problems of the two recensions.
