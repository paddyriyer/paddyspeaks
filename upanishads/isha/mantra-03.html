Īśāvāsya Upaniṣad · Mantra 3 of 18

The worlds of blind darkness and the slayers of the self

The consequence of missing the vision of mantra 1: those who ‘slay the self’ go, after death, to worlds wrapped in blind darkness.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

There are worlds called the worlds of the asuras, wrapped in blind darkness. After death, all who slay the self go to them.

Key concepts असुर्य sunless, demonic लोक world, realm आत्महन् slayer of the self तमस् darkness आत्मन् self, Self

7 Explain it to a beginner

After two hopeful verses, this one is stern. It speaks of places of thick, blind darkness, and says that people who ‘kill the self’ go there when they die. That phrase sounds frightening, but the great teachers did not take it literally as harming one’s body. They took it to mean living as if your deepest self did not exist — so absorbed in getting and fearing that the self is buried and forgotten, the way a lamp under a heavy pot gives no light to the room. The darkness, then, is not only a place after death; it is also the condition of a life that never looks inward. The verse is a warning given with care: the self can be hidden, but it cannot actually be destroyed, and what is hidden can be found.

An analogy A lamp covered by a heavy pot is still burning, but the room is dark; the light is ‘killed’ only in the sense that no one can see by it.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the mantra itself say?

It names a class of worlds, asurya, and describes them as covered by blind darkness; and it says that whoever are ‘slayers of the self’ go to them after death. It does not say who these people are, what killing the self consists in, or whether the worlds are places, births or states. The phrase ye ke ca, ‘whoever’, makes the statement general: no exception is made for rank or kind.

Traditional Interpretation

Who are the ‘slayers of the self’?

Śaṅkara introduces the mantra as a censure of the ignorant (अथेदानीमविद्वन्निन्दार्थोऽयं मन्त्र आरभ्यते) and explains the paradox at once: the Self cannot be killed, so how do they ‘kill’ it? By covering the Self that is always present, through the fault of ignorance — अविद्यादोषेण विद्यमानस्यात्मनस्तिरस्करणात् — so that what the Self is (unaging, deathless) is hidden as if it had been slain. Mahīdhara repeats this and applies it to those devoted to rites performed for desired results. Deśika reads self-killing as reducing one’s own self to something like non-being, and adds that the killing of the body is mentioned as an instance standing for the whole class of grave wrongs. Madhva reads the self-killers as those who worship wrongly and turn away from the Lord.

Traditional Interpretation

What are these worlds?

For Śaṅkara the worlds are births: places where the fruits of action are ‘seen’ and undergone, from the highest down to the stationary forms of life (तान् स्थावरान्तान् , प्रेत्य त्यक्त्वेमं देहम्). The word asurya extends further than a reader expects: measured against the non-dual Self, even the gods are asuras, so the heavens too are among these dark worlds. The darkness is ignorance itself, ‘whose nature is not seeing’. Deśika gives the worlds a more concrete sense, as the hells, and the darkness as the absence of light, extending without break (तान् - आलोकप्रसङ्गरहितान्, ते - स्वात्मघातिनः). Madhva stresses the universality of the rule: ये के चेत्यनेन नियम उक्तः — by ‘whoever’ a rule is laid down.

Traditional Interpretation

Does the mantra speak to renouncers or to everyone?

Śaṅkara, in his remarks before mantra 9, places mantras 3–8 in the teaching addressed to renouncers: through the censure of the ignorant (‘asuryā nāma te’) the true nature of the Self is taught to those devoted to knowledge. The censure here is therefore, in his reading, a means of turning the hearer toward the Self described in mantras 4–8, rather than a separate doctrine of punishment.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What do the witnesses and the grammar add?

Three points. First, every Kāṇva witness has ते twice (te lokāḥ … tāṃs te), but the Mādhyandina recension lacks the first and reads pretyāpi gacchanti for pretyābhigacchanti. Second, tamasāvṛtāḥ divides either as tamasā āvṛtāḥ or tamasā vṛtāḥ; Śaṅkara’s gloss shows the first, Deśika’s the second, with no difference of meaning. Third, the pronoun te in the second half is the correlative of ye: the order ‘they go to them, whoever are self-slayers’ puts the subject’s description last, where the mantra wants the weight to fall.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantra 3

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The self-slayers are the ignorant, who hide the Self that is always present: तेन ह्यात्महननदोषेण संसरन्ति ते. The worlds are births; even the gods, measured against the non-dual Self, are asuras, so no world of birth is exempt.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Vedānta Deśika, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantra 3

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The mantra is spoken so that the hearer turns quickly to the knowledge about to be taught (वक्ष्यमाणविद्यायां शीघ्रप्रवृत्त्यर्थम्). Those who lack knowledge of Brahman and are bound by desire for wealth and the rest ‘slay the self’ and fall to the hells, which are real places of suffering.

Dvaita · Madhva, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantra 3

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The worlds are called asurya because they are opposed to true delight and are reached by asuras; those who go there are the ones turned away from Hari (तान् यान्ति विमुखा हरौ), and the words ye ke ca state a rule without exception (नियमेन तमो यान्ति सर्वेऽपि विमुखा हरौ).

Where they disagree

All three read ‘slaying the self’ as a spiritual failure rather than its literal sense. Śaṅkara makes it ignorance of the Self and the worlds every world of rebirth, heavens included; Deśika and Madhva make it the absence of knowledge of, or turning away from, the Lord, and the worlds places of suffering. The disagreement follows from what each takes the self to be.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • असुर्या नाम asuryā nāma → असुर्याः + नाम

    visarga sandhi (āḥ before a voiced sound)

    After ā the visarga becomes y before a voiced sound, P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि), and the y is dropped before a consonant, P. 8.3.22 (हलि सर्वेषाम्).

  • लोका अन्धेन lokā andhena → लोकाः + अन्धेन

    visarga sandhi (āḥ before a vowel), hiatus

    As before, -āḥ becomes -āy by P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि); before a vowel the y may be dropped, P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य), and the two vowels then stand side by side without further sandhi.

  • तमसावृताः tamasāvṛtāḥ → तमसा + आवृताः

    vowel sandhi (lengthening)

    ā + ā → ā, P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः). The same letters would result from tamasā + vṛtāḥ written together; see Ambiguities.

  • तांस्ते tāṃste → तान् + ते

    consonant sandhi (n before t)

    A final n before t is replaced, P. 8.3.7 (नश्छव्यप्रशान्), and appears as s with a nasalised vowel before it: tān + te → tāṃs te. The Saṃhitā witnesses write ताँस्ते.

  • प्रेत्याभिगच्छन्ति pretyābhigacchanti → प्रेत्य + अभिगच्छन्ति

    vowel sandhi (lengthening)

    a + a → ā, P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः).

  • चात्महनो जनाः cātmahano janāḥ → च + आत्महनः + जनाः

    vowel sandhi; visarga sandhi (aḥ before a voiced consonant)

    ca + ātmahanaḥ → cātmahanaḥ, P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः). Before the voiced j, the final -aḥ becomes u, P. 6.1.114 (हशि च), and joins the a as o, P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः): ātmahano janāḥ.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • आत्महनः ātmahanaḥ

    upapada-tatpuruṣa (a noun + a verbal root used as its final member) · आत्मन् + हन्

    Vigraha आत्मानं घ्नन्ति इति आत्महनः

    those who kill the self

Morphology

असुर्याः
An adjective in -ya from asura. Deśika derives it by the rule असुरस्य स्वम्, P. 4.4.123 (असुरस्य स्वम्), ‘belonging to the asura’, and explains: worlds to be experienced by those of demonic nature (आसुरप्रकृतीनामनुभाव्या इत्यर्थः). Mahīdhara explains asura itself as ‘those who delight in the breaths (asu)’, devoted to nourishing life (असुषु प्राणेषु रमन्तेऽसुराः प्राणपोषणपराः). Madhva gives two grounds: the worlds are opposed to true delight and are reached by asuras (सुष्ठु रमणविरुद्धत्वादसुराणां प्राप्यत्वाच्चासुर्याः).
प्रेत्य
Absolutive of i ‘to go’ with the preverb pra: ‘having gone forth’, used of dying. With a preverb the absolutive takes -ya instead of -tvā, P. 7.1.37 (समासेऽनञ्पूर्वे क्त्वो ल्यप्).
आत्महनः
Nominative plural of ātmahan, a compound of ātman and the root han ‘to strike, kill’. The compound says only ‘self-killers’; the commentators agree that it does not here mean those who take their own lives, though Deśika allows that killing the body is included as one instance of grave wrong.
ते
The pronoun occurs twice with different reference: ते लोकाः, ‘those worlds’, and तान् ते, ‘they [go] to them’. The Mādhyandina recension lacks the first: असुर्य्या नाम लोकाः (VS 40.3).

Anvaya

असुर्याः नाम ते लोकाः अन्धेन तमसा आवृताः [सन्ति] । ये के च आत्महनः जनाः ते प्रेत्य तान् अभिगच्छन्ति ।

asuryāḥ nāma te lokāḥ andhena tamasā āvṛtāḥ santi | ye ke ca ātmahanaḥ janāḥ te pretya tān abhigacchanti |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Two sentences linked by the pronouns. The first names and describes the worlds (asuryāḥ nāma te lokāḥ, ‘those worlds are called asurya’), with a second predicate (andhena tamasāvṛtāḥ). The second is a relative-correlative construction: ye ke ca ātmahano janāḥ (‘whoever are people who slay the self’) … te … tān abhigacchanti (‘they go to them’), with pretya (‘after death’) as the prior action. The relative clause stands at the end of the line, after its correlative, a common order in verse.

Ambiguities

असुर्य: what kind of worlds?
All the commentaries consulted derive asurya from asura, ‘belonging to the asuras’ (Śaṅkara, Deśika, Mahīdhara, Madhva), though they explain the asuras differently. Śaṅkara’s reading is the most far-reaching: against the non-dual Self even the gods are asuras, so these worlds include every world of birth, the heavens not excepted. A rendering ‘sunless’ (as if a-sūrya) is sometimes met in modern translations; it would require a long ū, and every witness reads asurya with short u.
Births or hells?
Śaṅkara and Mahīdhara take lokāḥ as births, from lok ‘to see’: the places where the fruits of action are seen and undergone (लोक्यन्ते दृश्यन्ते भुज्यन्त इति जन्मानि), down to plant life. Deśika takes them as hells (नरकसंज्ञिता भीषणतमा लोकाः).
आवृताः or वृताः
Written together, tamasāvṛtāḥ can be tamasā āvṛtāḥ (‘covered over’, Śaṅkara, Mahīdhara) or tamasā vṛtāḥ (‘covered’, Deśika: अन्धेन तमसा वृताः; Advaita Sharada also prints a space, तमसा वृताः). The sense is the same; the padaccheda follows Śaṅkara’s gloss आवृताः आच्छादिताः. Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.11 has the same phrase.
Who are the slayers of the self?
Śaṅkara: the ignorant, who hide the ever-present Self as if it were killed (अविद्यादोषेण विद्यमानस्यात्मनस्तिरस्करणात्). Mahīdhara: those devoted to rites for desired ends, with the same explanation. Deśika: those who reduce their self to near non-existence, citing ‘असन्नेव स भवति, असद्ब्रह्मेति वेद चेत्’. Madhva: those turned away from Hari (तान् यान्ति विमुखा हरौ).
The Mādhyandina reading
VS 40.3 reads ‘asuryā nāma lokāḥ … tāṃs te pretyāpi gacchanti’ — without the first te, and with api gacchanti, ‘they go even after death’, for abhigacchanti, ‘they go to [them]’. Mahīdhara glosses अपिगच्छन्ति प्राप्नुवन्ति.

Metre

anuṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 16 + 16

Two half-lines of 16 syllables. Mahīdhara gives anuṣṭubh as the metre of the adhyāya.

Textual variations between witnesses

  • Other recension Mādhyandina VS 40 (svara, pada-pāṭha): असुर्या नाम लोकाः — The Mādhyandina recension (VS 40.3) has no ते: ‘asuryā nāma lokāḥ’. Kāṇva witnesses G, AS, K, Mv, VD all read ते.
  • Other recension Mādhyandina VS 40 (svara, pada-pāṭha): प्रेत्यापि गच्छन्ति — VS 40.3 (Mādhyandina) reads ‘pretyāpi gacchanti’ (‘go, even after death’) where every Kāṇva witness reads ‘pretyābhigacchanti’ (‘go to them after death’).
11 Connections with other scriptures

  • Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.4.11 Direct textual parallel

    तांस्ते प्रेत्याभिगच्छन्त्यविद्वांसोऽबुधो जनाः

    tāṃste pretyābhigacchantyavidvāṃso'budho janāḥ

    The Bṛhadāraṇyaka has almost the same verse: ‘Joyless (anandāḥ) are those worlds, covered by blind darkness; to them go after death those people who are not knowers, not awake.’ It names the worlds ‘joyless’ and the people ‘ignorant’ where the Īśā has asurya and ‘slayers of the self’ — itself a gloss in the direction Śaṅkara takes.

  • Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.1.3 Direct textual parallel

    अनन्दा नाम ते लोकास्तान्स गच्छति ता ददत्

    anandā nāma te lokāstānsa gacchati tā dadat

    Naciketas, watching his father give away worn-out cows, says ‘joyless, by name, are those worlds’ to which such a giver goes. The same formula ‘… nāma te lokāḥ’ used for the result of empty ritual.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Ego and self-image

It is possible to be very busy maintaining an image — on screens, at work, in the family — and to find, in a quiet moment, that one no longer knows what one actually thinks or wants. The commentators’ ‘slaying of the self’ is not this, but it points somewhere near it: the self is not destroyed, only covered, and a life can be lived entirely under the cover. Read this way the mantra is less a threat than a description of what forgetting oneself costs. This is a modern reflection, separate from the commentaries.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin When was the last time you felt you had lost touch with yourself? What were you so busy with?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry If the Self cannot be killed, in what sense can it be ‘slain’ — and who is it that does the slaying?

A contemplation exercise At three moments tomorrow, stop for a few seconds and ask, without answering in words: ‘Who is aware just now?’ Notice what happens to the busyness when the question is asked.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.

Further notes

  • Mahīdhara (on VS 40.3) introduces the mantra as a censure of those devoted to rites for desired ends (अथ काम्यकर्मपरान्निन्दति) and concludes: those who do not know the Self are born again and die again (अनात्मज्ञाः पुनर्जायन्ते म्रियन्ते चेत्यर्थः).

Uncertainties recorded

  • uncertain Deśika’s remark on the killing of the body is transmitted with editorial brackets in the keyed text (देहघा(पा)तमुखेन पातकवर्गोपलक्षणमि(णं त्वि)दम्); its exact wording should be checked against a printed edition.
