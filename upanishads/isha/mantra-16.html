Īśāvāsya Upaniṣad · Mantra 16 of 18

Gather your light; that person yonder, I am he

The sun is asked by five names to draw back its rays so that its most beautiful form may be seen; the speaker then says, ‘that person yonder — I am he’.

5 Faithful English translation

O Pūṣan, O sole seer, O Yama, O Sun, O son of Prajāpati, spread apart [your] rays, gather [your] light. That form of yours which [is] most beautiful, that of yours I see. The person who [is] yonder, yonder — he I am.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Pūṣan, lone seer, controller, Sun, child of Prajāpati: draw back your rays, gather in your light. I see your most beautiful form. That person there — I am he.

Key concepts पूषन् Pūṣan सूर्य the Sun ऋषि seer पुरुष person आत्मन् self, Self

7 Explain it to a beginner

The speaker now calls the sun by five names, the way someone calls to a friend by every name they know, and asks it to pull back its blinding rays and gather its light, so that its most beautiful form can be seen. Then comes one of the most famous short sentences in the Upanishads: ‘That person there — I am he.’ After all that looking outward, the speaker discovers that what is seen in the sun is not finally separate from the one who sees. Teachers disagree about what exactly this means. Some say the seeker and the divine are one; others say the divine lives within the seeker as the self of the self, close but never the same. Either way, the search that went outward ends by coming home.

An analogy It is like looking for a long time at a face in a window at dusk, and realising, as the light outside fades, that the face is your own reflection — or, for other teachers, the face of someone standing right behind you.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the mantra say?

It calls on the sun by five names, asks it to spread apart its rays and gather in its brilliance, says ‘I see your most beautiful form’, and ends ‘the person who is yonder, yonder — he am I’. The request of mantra 15 (‘uncover’) is made concrete: the uncovering is the withdrawing of the sun’s rays. The final sentence states an identity between the speaker and ‘that person’, without saying what the person is or how the identity is to be understood.

Traditional Interpretation

What do the five names mean?

Śaṅkara explains each name as a function of the sun: Pūṣan because it nourishes the world; ekarṣi because it goes alone (एक एव ऋषति गच्छतीत्येकर्षिः), taking ṛṣi from ṛṣ ‘to go’; Yama because it controls all (सर्वस्य संयमनाद्यमः); Sūrya because it takes in rays, breaths and waters; Prājāpatya as the offspring of Prajāpati. Deśika reads the same names as names of the Supreme Self: the peerless seer of what is beyond the senses, the inner controller of all (sarvāntaryāmin), the one who impels the minds of his devotees, the inner controller of all that Prajāpati produced. Madhva’s verses do the same for Viṣṇu (विष्णुरेकऋषिर्ज्ञेयो यमो नियमनाद्धरिः). The commentaries agree that the names belong to one addressee; they differ on whether he is the sun as meditated deity (Śaṅkara) or the Lord who is called by the sun’s names (Deśika, Madhva).

Comparative Vedānta

‘He am I’: what kind of identity?

This is the sentence the schools contest most sharply in the Upaniṣad. For Śaṅkara the meditator does not ask as a servant; he declares that the person in the solar disk — whose limbs are the vyāhṛtis, who fills the world or lies in the city of the body — is himself: सोऽहम् अस्मि भवामि. Deśika devotes most of his commentary on this mantra to the grammar of the first person: ‘I’ here reaches through the individual self to its inner controller, as ‘you’ does in tat tvam asi, and the sentence is a meditation on the Lord as one’s own inner Self. He names and rejects the view that such sentences teach the identity of a self without attributes (ये पुनः 'तत्त्वमसि', 'सोऽहमस्मि' इत्यादिषु कार्यकारणोपाध्याकारविधूननेन निर्विशेषस्वरूपैक्यं वाक्यवेद्यमाहुः). Madhva, in verses he cites from a text he calls the Brahmāṇḍa, makes aham and asmi names of Hari himself and adds that Hari is distinct from every self.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Why ‘asau’ twice?

yo ’sāv asau puruṣaḥ repeats the distant demonstrative. Śaṅkara reads the second asau as a name: the person in the sun is called asau, ‘that one’, and his limbs are the vyāhṛtis (असौ व्याहृत्यवयवः पुरुषः), alluding to the Bṛhadāraṇyaka’s meditation on the person in the sun (5.5). Deśika gives two readings: the repetition shows reverence (वीप्सा अत्यादरव्यञ्जनार्था), or the two pronouns go with two relative clauses, ‘he who is that, who is beyond the senses’. The Mādhyandina counterpart (‘yo ’sāv āditye puruṣaḥ so ’sāv aham’, VS 40.17) has only one asau in the relative clause, with āditye, ‘in the sun’, and puts the second in the main clause.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantra 16

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The dying meditator asks the sun to gather its light so that he may see its most beautiful form ‘by your grace’ (तवात्मनः प्रसादात्); he does not beg as a servant, for the person in the solar disk is himself: सोऽहम् अस्मि भवामि. In Śaṅkara’s overall reading this is the realisation of the meditator on the lower Brahman, on the path of light, not the knowledge of the attributeless Self that needs no path.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Vedānta Deśika, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantra 16

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The ‘most beautiful form’ is the Lord’s divine form, the sun-coloured one known from scripture (सर्वेभ्यः कल्याणेभ्योऽतिशयितकल्याणं शुभाश्रयभूतं ते दिव्यं रूपं). ‘He am I’ is meditation on the inner Self in the first person: अहंशब्दोऽत्र जीवद्वारा तदन्तरात्मपर्यन्तः. The Lord and the self are not identical; the self is his body, and ‘I’ refers through it to him.

Dvaita · Madhva, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantras 16–19 (his numbering)

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The five names are names of Viṣṇu (विष्णुरेकऋषिर्ज्ञेयो यमो नियमनाद्धरिः). In the final clause aham and asmi name Hari — ‘aham’ because he is not to be abandoned, ‘asmi’ because he always is (अस्मि नित्यास्तितामानात् सर्वजीवेषु संस्थितः) — and the verse Madhva cites adds that he is distinct from all selves. Madhva cites these verses from a Brahmāṇḍa; the edition reports them as cited by him.

Where they disagree

The three schools read the same five words, so ’ham asmi, as identity of the meditator with the person in the sun (Śaṅkara), as the Lord meditated on as one’s inner Self (Deśika), and as names of a Lord distinct from every self (Madhva). The text does not settle it, and this edition does not.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • पूषन्नेकर्षे pūṣannekarṣe → पूषन् + एकर्षे

    consonant sandhi (doubling of n)

    A word-final n after a short vowel is doubled before a vowel: P. 8.3.32 (ङमो ह्रस्वादचि ङमुण्नित्यम्).

  • एकर्षे ekarṣe → एक + ऋषि

    internal vowel sandhi in a compound (guṇa)

    a + ṛ → ar: P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः). eka + ṛṣi → ekarṣi, vocative ekarṣe.

  • रश्मीन्समूह raśmīnsamūha → रश्मीन् + समूह

    consonant sandhi (none)

    Final n before s is left unchanged and written together with the next word; nothing is inserted.

  • योऽसावसौ yo'sāvasau → यः + असौ + असौ

    visarga sandhi, then vowel sandhi (āv)

    yaḥ + asau: -aḥ before a becomes -o and the following a is elided, marked by avagraha (P. 6.1.109 (एङः पदान्तादति) after the visarga has become u). asau + asau: au before a vowel becomes āv: P. 6.1.78 (एचोऽयवायावः).

  • सोऽहमस्मि so'hamasmi → सः + अहम् + अस्मि

    visarga sandhi (saḥ) and pūrvarūpa

    saḥ before a becomes so, and the a of aham is elided after it (P. 6.1.109 (एङः पदान्तादति)): so ’ham. The m of aham joins the vowel of asmi.

  • तेजः tejaḥ → तेजः

    pause

    G pauses after tejaḥ at the half-line, so the visarga stays. AS, Mv and K join across it as तेजो यत्ते (corrections.json, orthographic).

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • एकर्षे ekarṣe

    karmadhāraya · एक + ऋषि

    Vigraha एकश्चासौ ऋषिश्च

    the one seer; (Śaṅkara) the one who goes alone

Morphology

व्यूह
Imperative of ūh ‘to push, move’ with vi ‘apart’: ‘push apart, remove’ (the rays). In the same line samūha, the same root with sam ‘together’: ‘gather in’ (the light). The pair is deliberate: scatter the rays, concentrate the light.
प्राजापत्य
A secondary (taddhita) derivative of prajāpati with vṛddhi of the first syllable, ‘descended from Prajāpati’. Deśika allows that the suffix may carry no meaning here, so that the word simply means ‘lord of creatures’.
कल्याणतमम्
Superlative of kalyāṇa ‘beautiful, auspicious’ with the suffix -tama: P. 5.3.55 (अतिशायने तमबिष्ठनौ).
पश्यामि
dṛś forms its present on the stem paśya-: P. 7.3.78 (पाघ्राध्मास्थाम्नादाण्दृश्यर्त्तिसर्त्तिशदसदां पिबजिघ्रधमतिष्ठमनयच्छपश्यर्च्छधौशीयसीदाः). A present in a prayer: Śaṅkara keeps ‘I see’ (by your grace); Deśika allows either a description of the present meditation or, by interchange of forms, ‘may I see’.
असौ
adas, the pronoun of the distant (‘that yonder’). It is repeated: yo ’sāv asau. Śaṅkara takes the second as the name of the person in the sun whose limbs are the vyāhṛtis (bhūḥ, bhuvaḥ, svaḥ; cf. Bṛhadāraṇyaka 5.5); Deśika as emphasis, or divides the pronouns between two clauses.
अस्मि
First person singular present of as. Deśika argues that the first person here, like the second person in ‘tat tvam asi’, extends through the individual self to the Lord who is its inner Self (अहंशब्दोऽत्र जीवद्वारा तदन्तरात्मपर्यन्तः).

Anvaya

पूषन् एकर्षे यम सूर्य प्राजापत्य रश्मीन् व्यूह तेजः समूह । यत् ते कल्याणतमम् रूपम् तत् ते पश्यामि । यः असौ असौ पुरुषः सः अहम् अस्मि ।

pūṣan ekarṣe yama sūrya prājāpatya raśmīn vyūha tejaḥ samūha | yat te kalyāṇatamam rūpam tat te paśyāmi | yaḥ asau asau puruṣaḥ saḥ aham asmi |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Five vocatives (Pūṣan, sole seer, Yama, Sun, son of Prajāpati) and two imperatives (vyūha, samūha) make up the request; then a relative–correlative sentence (yat … tat …): ‘that form of yours which is most beautiful, that I see’; and a second one (yaḥ … saḥ …): ‘the person who is yonder, he am I’. The last clause has no verb of seeing or praying; it is a statement of identity in the first person, and its sense depends on what the commentators take ‘I’ to refer to.

Ambiguities

सोऽहमस्मि: identity, or meditation on the inner Self
Śaṅkara: the speaker declares himself the person in the sun — ‘I do not beg you like a servant’ (अहं न तु त्वां भृत्यवद्याचे) — ‘he am I’. Deśika: ‘I’ reaches, through the individual self, to the Lord who is its inner Self; the sentence is a meditation of the Lord as one’s own inner self (ahaṅgraha), and he argues at length against reading it as the identity of an attributeless self. Madhva: aham and asmi are names of Hari himself, ‘not to be abandoned’ and ‘ever existing’, who is distinct from every individual self (स्वयं तु सर्वजीवेभ्यो व्यतिरिक्तः परो हरिः). The Sanskrit allows each, because it does not say whose ‘I’ this is.
पश्यामि: ‘I see’ or ‘may I see’
A present indicative in a prayer. Read literally it reports a vision already granted; Deśika allows the present to stand for the optative.
The shorter text of the Kāṇva Saṃhitā witness
The keyed Kāṇva Saṃhitā (K) lacks ‘pūṣann ekarṣe yama sūrya prājāpatya vyūha raśmīn samūha’ and reads रूपे for रूपं. Every Upaniṣad witness and Bṛhadāraṇyaka 5.15.1 have the full text; corrections.json classes both points as ‘review’ (verification against a printed Kāṇva Saṃhitā required). The Mādhyandina recension has no counterpart to this mantra except the final clause, in another form (‘yo ’sāv āditye puruṣaḥ so ’sāv aham’, VS 40.17).
Madhva’s division
Madhva’s text divides this mantra into two (his 16 and 17), the second being ‘yo ’sāv asau puruṣaḥ so ’ham asmi’ alone.

Metre

irregular · syllables counted as written: 22 + 25

Counted as written: 22 syllables in the first half-line and 25 in the second (47). No regular metre fits; the mantra reads as a prose prayer framed by a vision. Mahīdhara gives no metre for it because the Mādhyandina recension does not have it.

Textual variations between witnesses

  • Verification required Kāṇva Saṃhitā 40 (svara): तेजो यत्ते रूपे कल्याणतमं तत्ते पश्यामि — The Kāṇva Saṃhitā witness as keyed lacks ‘pūṣann ekarṣe yama sūrya prājāpatya vyūha raśmīn samūha’ and begins the mantra at तेजो. Every Upaniṣad witness, and Bṛhadāraṇyaka 5.15.1 where the same verse occurs, has the full text. Textual verification required against a printed Kāṇva Saṃhitā; whether the omission is the witness’s or the recension’s cannot be decided from the materials available.
  • Verification required Kāṇva Saṃhitā 40 (svara): रूपे — The Kāṇva Saṃhitā witness reads रूपे (‘in the form’) for रूपं; every Upaniṣad witness and BU 5.15.1 read रूपं. Probably a slip of the keyed text; textual verification required.

    रूपमादित्यवर्णं तमसः परस्तात्

    rūpamādityavarṇaṃ tamasaḥ parastāt

    The Gītā’s form ‘sun-coloured, beyond darkness’, remembered at death. Deśika speaks of the Lord’s form here as ‘sun-coloured’ (‘yat — ādityavarṇam ityādiprasiddham’), the epithet of Śvetāśvatara 3.8 and of this Gītā verse.

Every quoted phrase is checked against the text of the passage cited (sources in section 14).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Ego and self-image

Much of modern life asks us to look outward for the person we are supposed to become — the admired colleague, the image on the screen. This mantra makes the search outward explicit and then turns it round: the person in the light is ‘I’. A careful reader need not settle the metaphysics to notice what the sentence does to self-image. If the most beautiful form is not elsewhere, the restless comparison loses its object; and on the theistic readings, the self is honoured as the dwelling of something greater rather than inflated. This is a reflection, not a gloss on the text.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin When you admire someone very much, what is it in them that you are really looking for?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry In ‘he am I’, what is the ‘I’ that can say it — the person with a name and a history, the witness of that person, or something that dwells in both?

A contemplation exercise Sit for a few minutes facing the morning or evening light with your eyes closed. Notice the brightness through your eyelids, then let your attention turn to the one who notices it. Do not try to conclude anything.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
