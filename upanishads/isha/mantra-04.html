Īśāvāsya Upaniṣad · Mantra 4 of 18

Unmoving, one, swifter than the mind

The first description of what mantra 1’s vision sees: a reality that does not move, yet is ahead of everything that moves.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

It does not move, it is one, and it is swifter than the mind; the gods never reached it, for it had always gone ahead. Standing still, it outruns all who run. In it the wind sets the waters.

Key concepts आत्मन् self, Self देवता deity वायु breath, air कर्मन् action, ritual work

7 Explain it to a beginner

This verse describes something with a string of opposites. It never moves, yet it is faster than your mind. It stays still, yet it is ahead of everyone running. How can both be true? Think of the sky. However fast you travel toward the horizon, the sky is already there ahead of you, without having moved at all. The teachers say the self is like that: when your thoughts race to a distant city, you are already aware of the thought the moment it arrives, so in a sense awareness gets there first. The verse adds that even the gods, or your own senses, could never catch it, because it was there before they set out. And everything that happens in the world — winds blowing, rains falling, people acting — happens resting on it.

An analogy However fast you fly toward the horizon, the sky is already there ahead of you, and it has not moved at all.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the mantra itself say?

It describes an unnamed ‘it’ by paradox: unmoving yet swifter than the mind; never reached by the devas because it had gone ahead; standing, yet outrunning those who run. It adds that ‘in it’ Mātariśvan, the wind, ‘places the waters’. The Sanskrit does not name ‘it’ — the Self, Brahman or the Lord — and does not resolve the paradox. It is the first mantra of the Upaniṣad to describe what mantra 1’s vision sees, rather than what one should do.

Traditional Interpretation

How can the unmoving be swifter than the mind?

Śaṅkara states the objection himself — कथं विरुद्धमुच्यते — ध्रुवं निश्चलमिदम्, मनसो जवीय इति च? — and answers: नैष दोषः, निरुपाध्युपाधिमत्त्वेनोपपत्तेः. In its own nature, without limiting adjuncts, the Self is unmoving and one; considered with the mind as its adjunct, it seems to move with the mind. When the mind leaps in an instant to a distant world, the light of consciousness is found to be there already, as if it had arrived first. Mahīdhara repeats this reasoning. Deśika gives a different resolution: the expressions are figurative, governed by intent — it is ‘unmoving’ because it always pervades everything, and ‘swifter than the mind’ because it is always beyond the region the mind can reach (सर्वदा मनसो गोचरदेशमतिक्रम्य वृत्तेः मनसो जवीय इत्युपचर्यते). For him the paradox displays the Lord’s wondrous powers (विचित्रशक्तियोगेन).

Traditional Interpretation

Why did the devas not reach it?

For Śaṅkara the devas are the senses: the mind outruns them, and the Self is ahead of the mind, so that not even a reflection of the Self becomes an object of the senses (आभासमात्रमप्यात्मनो नैव देवानां विषयीभवति). For Deśika they are the gods led by Hiraṇyagarbha: the Lord is always present to them, yet selves whose knowledge is obstructed by karma do not attain him until knowledge arises. He cites the Chāndogya image of people walking every day over a buried treasure of gold without finding it (तत् यथा हिरण्यनिधिमक्षेत्रज्ञा उपर्युपरि सञ्चरन्तो न विन्देयुः). Madhva: it cannot be fully known even by the gods.

Traditional Interpretation

What does the wind do ‘in it’?

Śaṅkara takes Mātariśvan as the cosmic air, the life of all that breathes, called the ‘thread’ (sūtra) on which the world is strung, and the waters as actions: the activity of creatures and the shining, burning and raining of the natural powers. These go on only while the Self is present, the ground of all: सर्वा हि कार्यकरणविक्रिया नित्यचैतन्यात्मस्वरूपे सर्वास्पदभूते सत्येव भवन्तीत्यर्थः. He cites the Taittirīya ‘from fear of it the wind blows’. Deśika keeps the waters and sees another wonder: the wind, without the solidity that would hold anything up, bears the waters, clouds and stars by the strength of the one who supports all. Madhva makes the wind (Vāyu, in his system the chief of selves) place all actions in the Lord, so that every act is to be offered to Hari.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What are the textual and grammatical questions?

The one real variant is arṣat (Upaniṣad witnesses, accented Mādhyandina text) against arśat (Kāṇva Saṃhitā witness, Mahīdhara). Mahīdhara’s derivations of arśat — ‘going’, or ‘not perishing’ from riś — show a commentator working with the Saṃhitā reading. The participles carry the paradox: anejat and tiṣṭhat (‘not moving’, ‘standing’) against arṣat and the finite atyeti (‘going’, ‘overtakes’); the same subject receives both. The metre is triṣṭubh (Mahīdhara: त्रिष्टुप्छन्दस्केयमृक्), the first change from the anuṣṭubh of mantras 1–3.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantra 4

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

‘It’ is the Self, changeless in its own nature and appearing to move only through the mind, its adjunct; it seems many, one in each body, only to the undiscriminating (मूढानामनेकमिव च प्रतिदेहं प्रत्यवभासत). All activity in the world takes place in its presence.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Vedānta Deśika, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantra 4

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

‘It’ is the Lord introduced in mantra 1 as the dwelling of all (सर्वावासत्वेन प्रस्तुतमीश्वरतत्त्वं), whose seemingly contradictory description reveals his wondrous powers. He pervades all and therefore ‘does not move’; the gods do not attain him until knowledge removes the obstruction of karma; the wind bears the waters by his strength.

Dvaita · Madhva, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantra 4

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Through verses he cites as from the Brahmāṇḍa: the Lord is ‘unmoving’ because fearless, ‘one’ by being supreme, unattainable by the gods because they cannot know him fully, and he reaches all beforehand by his inconceivable power. The wind (Vāyu) places all actions in him, and so all activity is to be offered to Hari (मारुत्येव यतश्चेष्टा सर्वा तां हरयेर्पयेत्).

Where they disagree

The schools agree that the subject is the supreme reality of mantra 1. Śaṅkara resolves the paradox by distinguishing the Self as it is from the Self as it appears through the mind; Deśika and Madhva read the paradox as the Lord’s real and wonderful power. They also differ on who the devas are (senses, or gods) and on what the wind ‘places’ (actions, or waters).

  • मनसो जवीयो नैनद्देवा manaso javīyo nainaddevā → मनसः + जवीयः + न + एनत् + देवाः

    visarga sandhi; vowel sandhi (vṛddhi); consonant voicing

    -aḥ before a voiced consonant becomes o, P. 6.1.114 (हशि च) with P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः): manaso, javīyo. na + enat → nainat: a + e → ai, P. 6.1.88 (वृद्धिरेचि). enat + devāḥ → enad devāḥ, P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते).

  • देवा आप्नुवन्पूर्व devā āpnuvanpūrva → देवाः + आप्नुवन्

    visarga sandhi (āḥ before a vowel), hiatus

    -āḥ → -āy before a voiced sound, P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि), and the y is dropped, P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य), leaving the two vowels in hiatus. The Mādhyandina witness writes the hiatus with a sign (दे॒वाऽआ॑प्नुव॒न्).

  • तद्धावतोऽन्यानत्येति taddhāvato'nyānatyeti → तत् + धावतः + अन्यान् + अत्येति

    consonant voicing; visarga sandhi with elision of a

    tat + dhāvataḥ → tad dhāvataḥ, P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते). dhāvataḥ + anyān: the final becomes u before a, P. 6.1.113 (अतो रोरप्लुतादप्लुते), joins as o, and the initial a is absorbed, P. 6.1.109 (एङः पदान्तादति): dhāvato ’nyān.

  • तस्मिन्नपो tasminnapo → तस्मिन् + अपः

    consonant doubling; visarga sandhi

    n after a short vowel is doubled before a vowel, P. 8.3.32 (ङमो ह्रस्वादचि ङमुण्नित्यम्): tasminn apaḥ; apaḥ before the voiced m becomes apo, P. 6.1.114 (हशि च).

  • अनेजत् anejat

    nañ-tatpuruṣa (negative compound) · न + एजत्

    Vigraha न एजत् इति अनेजत्

    not moving

Morphology

जवीयः
Comparative in -īyas, ‘swifter’, compared with the ablative manasaḥ; the suffix īyasun forms a comparative between two, P. 5.3.57 (द्विवचनविभज्योपपदे तरबीयसुनौ). Mahīdhara analyses it as the comparative of javavat ‘possessing speed’, with the possessive suffix dropped, citing P. 5.3.65 (विन्मतोर्लुक्).
आप्नुवन्
Imperfect of āp: the augment a- fuses with the initial ā (for vowel-initial roots the augment is ā, P. 6.4.72 (आडजादीनाम्)). The past tense matters to the sense: the gods ‘did not reach’ it, because it had always already gone ahead.
अर्षत्
Present participle of ṛṣ, ‘to flow, move on’. Śaṅkara glosses it as a completed going, पूर्वमेव गतम्, व्योमवद्व्यापित्वात् — ‘gone ahead, because it pervades like space’. The participle is neuter, agreeing with the neuter subject (‘it’) that the whole mantra describes.
तिष्ठत्
Present participle of sthā, used concessively: ‘while standing’. Śaṅkara reads the clause as an appearance, ‘it goes beyond, as it were’: इवार्थं स्वयमेव दर्शयति — तिष्ठदिति.
मातरिश्वा
Nominative of mātariśvan, an old name of the wind (and in the Ṛgveda of a bringer of fire). Śaṅkara’s explanation, ‘he who moves in the mother (mātari, locative), the mid-air’, treats it as a compound keeping the locative of its first member; it is an etymology, not a grammatical analysis of a living compound, and is given here as his.
अपः
Accusative plural of the feminine ap, ‘water’, normally plural. Mahīdhara, explaining the sense ‘actions’, derives it from āp ‘to obtain’ (that by which pleasure and pain are obtained), citing a rule of the Uṇādi list ('आप्नोतेर्ह्रस्वश्च' ( उणा० २ ॥ ५९)).

Anvaya

[तत्] अनेजत् एकम् मनसः जवीयः । पूर्वम् अर्षत् एनत् देवाः न आप्नुवन् । तत् तिष्ठत् धावतः अन्यान् अत्येति । तस्मिन् मातरिश्वा अपः दधाति ।

tat anejat ekam manasaḥ javīyaḥ | pūrvam arṣat enat devāḥ na āpnuvan | tat tiṣṭhat dhāvataḥ anyān atyeti | tasmin mātariśvā apaḥ dadhāti |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

The subject is never named: it is the neuter ‘it’ (tat, enat) that the first three mantras have been about. The first half gives three predicates (unmoving, one, swifter than the mind) and a clause with its reason: the gods did not reach it, [it] having gone ahead (pūrvam arṣat). The second half repeats the paradox with a finite verb and a concessive participle: ‘it, standing, overtakes the others as they run’. The final clause, tasminn apo mātariśvā dadhāti, has its own subject and is linked only by the locative tasmin, which can be ‘in it’ or ‘it being there’.

Ambiguities

अर्षत् or अर्शत्
Every Upaniṣad witness (G, AS, Mv, VD) and the accented Mādhyandina witness read अर्षत्; the Kāṇva Saṃhitā witness reads अर्शत्, as does Mahīdhara, who gives two derivations: ‘ऋश गतौ’ (‘going’) and ‘रिश हिंसायां’ (‘not perishing’: न रिशदरिशत् अर्शत्). For arṣat the commentators take ṛṣ as ‘to go’ (Śaṅkara: पूर्वमेव गतम्) or, with Madhva, ‘to know’ (ऋष ज्ञाने): ‘it knew [all] beforehand’. The edition keeps अर्षत् and records the difference (corrections.json).
देवाः: senses or gods?
Śaṅkara takes the devas as the senses, ‘shining’ because they reveal objects; the mind is swifter than they, and the Self ahead even of the mind. Deśika takes them as Hiraṇyagarbha and the other gods, whose knowledge is obstructed by karma until knowledge comes; Madhva as the gods (suras), who cannot know it fully (सम्यग्ज्ञातुमशक्यत्वादगम्यं तत्सुरैरपि).
अपः: waters or actions?
Śaṅkara and Mahīdhara: actions — the movements of living beings and the burning, shining and raining of fire, sun and cloud — which the wind, as the cosmic life, distributes or sustains in the presence of the Self. Deśika keeps the waters: the wind, which has no solidity, bears the waters (clouds, stars and planets) through the Lord’s power. Madhva: the wind places actions in him (तस्मिन् कर्माण्यधान्मरुत्) — all activity being Vāyu’s, he offers it to Hari.
तिष्ठत्: real or apparent?
For Śaṅkara the overtaking is an appearance (‘as it were’) of what is changeless; for Deśika it is a real wonder of the Lord’s power: standing everywhere, he is already beyond the swiftest, even Garuḍa (धावतो गरुडादीन् अप्यत्येति).

Metre

triṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 22 + 21

Mahīdhara names it: त्रिष्टुप्छन्दस्केयमृक्. The first half-line counts 22 syllables (11 + 11). The second counts 21: in dhāvato ’nyān the initial a of anyān is elided in writing; read in hiatus (dhāvato anyān) it supplies the eleventh syllable of the pāda, giving 22. This kind of restoration of an elided a is common in Vedic metre.

Textual variations between witnesses

  • Variant reading Kāṇva Saṃhitā 40 (svara): पूर्वमर्शत् — The Kāṇva Saṃhitā witness reads अर्शत् (arśat). Mahīdhara, on VS 40.4, also reads अर्शत् and derives it from रिश् ‘to hurt’ (‘not perishing’); the accented Mādhyandina witness M and all Upaniṣad witnesses (G, AS, Mv, VD) read अर्षत् (arṣat), which Śaṅkara glosses ‘गतम्’ (‘gone’, from ऋष्). The edition keeps अर्षत् and shows both readings on the mantra page.

    ‘The eye does not go there, nor speech, nor the mind.’ The Kena’s statement of what the senses and the mind cannot reach — close to Śaṅkara’s reading of the devas as the senses.

  • Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.2.21 Doctrinal comparison

    आसीनो दूरं व्रजति शयानो याति सर्वतः

    āsīno dūraṃ vrajati śayāno yāti sarvataḥ

    ‘Sitting, he travels far; lying down, he goes everywhere.’ The same use of paradox — rest and motion predicated of one subject — in the Kaṭha.

  • Taittirīya Upaniṣad 2.8.1 Doctrinal comparison

    भीषास्माद्वातः पवते

    bhīṣāsmādvātaḥ pavate

    ‘From fear of him the wind blows.’ Cited by Śaṅkara (and Mahīdhara) on this mantra: the wind’s activity depends on the supreme.

Every quoted phrase is checked against the text of the passage cited (sources in section 14).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Technology and digital distractions

A phone can carry the mind across the world in a second, and much of modern attention is spent being carried. The mantra’s image is of something that is not carried at all and is nevertheless never left behind: whatever the mind races toward, awareness is already present to it. Someone who notices this does not need the mind to slow down in order to find stillness; the stillness was never the mind’s. This is a contemplative reading of the mantra’s paradox, offered as a modern reflection, not as part of its traditional exegesis.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Think of the farthest place you can imagine. How long did it take your mind to get there? Who noticed it arrive?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry If something is both unmoving and ahead of all motion, is the contradiction in the thing, in our language, or in the standpoint from which we look?

A contemplation exercise Sit for five minutes. Let thoughts move wherever they move, and each time you notice one, note simply ‘and this is known’. Do not follow the thought; notice the knowing that does not travel with it.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
