Īśāvāsya Upaniṣad · Mantra 7 of 18

Where all beings have become the self, what delusion, what sorrow?

For one who knows, when all beings have become the self, delusion and grief have no place.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

When, for one who knows, all beings have become the self, what delusion can there be, what grief, for one who sees the oneness?

Key concepts एकत्व oneness मोह delusion शोक grief, sorrow आत्मन् self, Self

7 Explain it to a beginner

This verse follows directly from the one before it. There, a person learned to see all beings in the self and the self in all beings. Here the verse takes one more step: for such a person, all beings have actually ‘become the self’. Then it asks two short questions — what confusion? what grief? — and leaves them unanswered, because the answer is obvious: none. Think about where grief and confusion come from. We grieve when we lose something separate from us that we needed, and we are confused when the world seems split into pieces that do not fit. If everything is seen as one, with nothing outside to lose and nothing that fails to fit, those two troubles lose their footing. The teachers who explained this verse disagree about what exactly ‘oneness’ means, as you will read below, but they agree on its fruit.

An analogy A wave that knows itself as the ocean does not mourn when it sinks back, nor wonder where it went: nothing has been lost from the water.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the mantra itself say?

The first half says that ‘in which’ — a time or a self — ‘all beings have become the self alone, for the one who knows’. The second half asks ‘there, what delusion, what sorrow, for the one who sees oneness?’, a rhetorical form that denies both. The mantra names two troubles, moha (delusion) and śoka (sorrow), and one cause of their absence, seeing oneness (ekatvam). It does not say what kind of oneness, and it does not say how the knower came to it; mantra 6 described the seeing that leads here.

Traditional Interpretation

Why does Śaṅkara say that saṃsāra itself is ended here?

Śaṅkara introduces the mantra as stating the same point as mantra 6 (इममेवार्थमन्योऽपि मन्त्र आह). For him grief and delusion are the effects of not knowing, the seed of desire and action: शोकश्च मोहश्च कामकर्मबीजमजानतो भवति, न त्वात्मैकत्वं विशुद्धं गगनोपमं पश्यतः — they arise in one who does not know, not in one who sees the oneness of the self, pure and like the sky. Because the questions deny the very possibility of the effects of ignorance, he concludes that the whole of saṃsāra, with its cause, is uprooted: सकारणस्य संसारस्यात्यन्तमेवोच्छेदः प्रदर्शितो भवति.

Traditional Interpretation

How can ‘all beings’ become ‘the self’ without ceasing to be?

Deśika answers by grammar. ‘All beings became the self’ is a statement of co-reference (sāmānādhikaraṇya), like calling an embodied person by the name of the body: the Supreme Self is perceived as qualified by all (परमात्मैव सर्वविशिष्टः प्रतीतः इत्यर्थः). He explicitly rejects readings in which the world is sublated or the two are identical in nature (बाधोपचारस्वरूपैक्यादिपक्षाः बहिष्कार्याः). The absence of grief follows because one who sees everything as the glory of another owns nothing; he quotes Janaka: मिथिलायां प्रदीप्तायां न मे किञ्चित्प्रदह्यते — ‘when Mithilā burns, nothing of mine burns’.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

How is the second half built?

Two parallel genitive participles frame the verse: vijānataḥ ‘for one who knows’ closes the first line, anupaśyataḥ ‘for one who sees’ closes the second. The verb anupaśyati of mantra 6 returns as a participle, so that mantra 7 reads as the outcome of mantra 6. The questions use the masculine interrogative kaḥ twice, matching the masculine nouns moha and śoka. The Saṃhitā witnesses read यस्मिन्त्सर्वाणि, the Vedic junction with an inserted t; the words are unchanged.

Traditional Interpretation

Is this said of the liberated, or of the seeker?

Deśika raises the question himself: mantras 6 and 7 could be applied to the liberated, but, in keeping with what precedes, they are better taken as praise of the seeker: एतौ च श्लोकौ यद्यपि मुक्तविषयतया नेतुं शक्यौ, तथाऽपि पूर्वानुगुण्यान्मुमुक्षुप्रशंसार्थत्वमुचितम्. For him ‘seeing’ here means knowledge born of scripture and the meditation rooted in it, and he says that this was established in the Śārīraka-bhāṣya (Rāmānuja’s Śrībhāṣya, as Deśika cites it): मोक्षोपायोपदेशपरेषु सर्वेषु वाक्येषु दर्शनशब्द उपासनविषय इति शारीरकभाष्ये प्रत्यपादि. Śaṅkara, by contrast, speaks of the knower of the real.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantra 7

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The beings themselves, for one who knows the real, have become nothing but the self (आत्मैवाभूत् आत्मैव संवृत्तः परमार्थवस्तु विजानतः). Oneness is identity; grief and delusion are products of ignorance, and with them saṃsāra and its cause end.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Vedānta Deśika, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantra 7

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Oneness is the unity of the Lord as qualified by all things (सर्वविशिष्टैक्यमनुपश्यतः), not the denial of everything other than one: न ह्ययमेकशब्द एकव्यतिरिक्ताभावपरः. The verse praises the seeker whose meditation sees everything as the body and glory of the Lord, and who therefore grieves over nothing as his own.

Dvaita · Madhva, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantra 7

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The Supreme Self in whom all beings are is himself present in all beings; one who knows him as one in all beings has no delusion: एवं सर्वभूतेष्वेकत्वेन परमात्मानं विजानतः को मोहः. Oneness is the oneness of the Lord everywhere, not identity of the self with the Lord. Madhva supports this with a verse he cites from a ‘Pippalāda recension’ (reported as cited, not verified).

Where they disagree

Whether ekatva is the identity of self and all (Śaṅkara), the unity of the Lord with all as his qualifiers (Deśika), or the single Lord present everywhere (Madhva); and whether the verse describes the liberated knower (Śaṅkara’s emphasis) or the seeker in meditation (Deśika).

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • यस्मिन्सर्वाणि yasminsarvāṇi → यस्मिन् + सर्वाणि

    consonant sandhi (n before s)

    The Upaniṣad witnesses leave final n unchanged before s. The Saṃhitā witnesses (K, M) write यस्मिन्त्सर्वाणि, with a t inserted between n and s, the insertion taught after ḍ by P. 8.3.29 (डः सि धुट्) and extended to n by P. 8.3.30 (नश्च). The words are the same.

  • भूतान्यात्मैवाभूद्विजानतः bhūtānyātmaivābhūdvijānataḥ → भूतानि + आत्मा + एव + अभूत् + विजानतः

    vowel and consonant sandhi

    i + ā → yā (P. 6.1.77 (इको यणचि)); ā + e → ai (P. 6.1.88 (वृद्धिरेचि)); a + a → ā (P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः)); final t voiced to d before the voiced v (P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते)).

  • को मोहः ko mohaḥ → कः + मोहः

    visarga sandhi (-aḥ before a voiced consonant)

    -aḥ before a voiced consonant becomes -o (P. 6.1.114 (हशि च), then P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः)).

  • शोक एकत्वम् śoka ekatvam → शोकः + एकत्वम्

    visarga sandhi (-aḥ before a vowel other than a)

    -aḥ before a vowel other than a loses its visarga and the hiatus remains: the visarga becomes y (P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि)), which is dropped (P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य)). Deśika’s mūla writes कश्शोक for कः शोक.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this mantra.

Morphology

अभूत्
Root aorist of bhū: ‘has become’. The verb is singular though bhūtāni is plural; it agrees with the predicate ātmā (‘have become the self’), a construction Śaṅkara’s paraphrase keeps: तान्येव भूतानि सर्वाणि परमार्थात्मदर्शनात् आत्मैवाभूत् आत्मैव संवृत्तः — ‘those very beings have become, through the vision of the real self, nothing but the self’.
विजानतः
Genitive singular of the present participle of vi-jñā, ‘to know distinctly’. With anupaśyataḥ at the end of the verse it frames the mantra: ‘for the one who knows … for the one who sees oneness’. The genitive marks the person in whose experience the statement holds (‘for him’).
एकत्वम्
eka ‘one’ with the abstract suffix -tva: ‘one-ness’. The pada-pāṭha of VS 40.7 marks the suffix: ए॒क॒त्वमित्ये॑क॒ऽत्वम्.
यस्मिन्
Locative of the relative pronoun, masculine or neuter. It can be taken as time (‘when’) or as place or person (‘in which self’, ‘in whom’); Śaṅkara gives both, and the other commentators choose one each (see Ambiguities).

Anvaya

यस्मिन् विजानतः सर्वाणि भूतानि आत्मा एव अभूत् तत्र एकत्वम् अनुपश्यतः कः मोहः कः शोकः ।

yasmin vijānataḥ sarvāṇi bhūtāni ātmā eva abhūt tatra ekatvam anupaśyataḥ kaḥ mohaḥ kaḥ śokaḥ |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

A correlative sentence: yasmin … tatra, ‘in which … there’. The first half states a condition: all beings have become the self alone for the one who knows. The second half asks two rhetorical questions, ‘what delusion, what sorrow?’, meaning that there is none, for the one who sees oneness. The two genitive participles, vijānataḥ and anupaśyataḥ, refer to the same person.

Ambiguities

यस्मिन्: when, in which self, or in whom
Śaṅkara: यस्मिन् काले यथोक्तात्मनि वा (at which time, or in the self described); Deśika: at the time of meditation (प्रणिधानसमये); Madhva: in the Supreme Self in whom all beings are (यस्मिन् परमात्मनि सर्वभूतानि). The temporal reading makes the verse describe a moment of knowledge; the personal reading makes it describe the Lord.
‘All beings became the self’: identity or something else
Śaṅkara reads a real identity seen by the knower. Deśika reads it as the Supreme Self being perceived as qualified by all things (परमात्मैव सर्वविशिष्टः प्रतीतः इत्यर्थः), on the model of saying ‘I am a god’ of one embodied as a god, and rejects readings of sublation or identity of nature. Madhva construes differently: the Self in whom all beings are became present in all beings (स परमात्मैव तत्र सर्वभूतेष्वभूत्).
एकत्वम्: what oneness
Identity of all with the self (Śaṅkara: आत्मैकत्वं विशुद्धं गगनोपमं); the unity of the Lord qualified by all (Deśika: सर्वविशिष्टैक्यमनुपश्यतः), adding that ‘one’ here does not deny that anything else exists; or the one Lord in all beings (Madhva: सर्वभूतेष्वेकत्वेन परमात्मानं विजानतः).

Metre

anuṣṭubh · syllables counted as written: 15 + 16

Counted as written, 15 and 16 syllables. As in mantra 6, the first half-line has 16 if the semivowel of bhūtāny ātmaiva is pronounced as a vowel (bhūtāni ātmaiva, as Advaita Sharada prints it), a resolution common in Vedic verse.

11 Connections with other scriptures

  • Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 2.4.14 Direct textual parallel

    सर्वमात्मैवाभूत्

    sarvamātmaivābhūt

    Yājñavalkya to Maitreyī: ‘where everything has become the self, then by what and whom should one see?’ The same words, sarvam ātmaivābhūt, in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka, where they lead to the question how there can be knowing of another at all.

  • Chāndogya Upaniṣad 7.1.3 Doctrinal comparison

    तरति शोकमात्मविदिति

    tarati śokamātmaviditi

    Nārada has heard that ‘the knower of the self crosses grief’. The Chāndogya and the Īśā make the same connection between self-knowledge and the end of śoka.

  • Bhagavad Gītā 13.27 Modern thematic association

    विनश्यत्स्वविनश्यन्तं यः पश्यति स पश्यति

    vinaśyatsvavinaśyantaṃ yaḥ paśyati sa paśyati

    ‘Who sees the imperishable in the perishing, he sees.’ The Gītā ties right seeing to what does not perish among things that do, the ground on which grief at loss has no hold.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Aging and mortality

Grief is not a mistake, and nothing in the mantra asks a mourner to stop mourning. What it offers is a diagnosis: sorrow takes hold where we experience the world as a set of separate things that can be taken from us. People who have sat with the dying sometimes describe a late, quiet shift — less clinging to what is ending, more sense of belonging to something that is not. The mantra names that shift as a way of seeing, ‘oneness’, rather than as resignation. Whether one reads oneness as identity or as the presence of one Lord in all, the point for an aging person is the same: what we are is not only what we are losing. This is a reflection on the teaching, not part of it.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Remember a time you were very sad about losing something. Was part of the sadness the feeling that it was separate from you and now gone?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry If ‘oneness’ ends delusion and sorrow, is it the absence of others, or the presence of one in all others — and does the answer change what it would be like to lose someone?

A contemplation exercise When a small loss happens today — a broken cup, a missed bus — pause and ask, ‘what of me is actually gone?’ Write down the answer in one line.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
