Īśāvāsya Upaniṣad · Mantra 17 of 18

Breath to the immortal air; this body ends in ashes

The dying person’s words: the breath returns to the air, the body to ashes, and the mind is called on to remember what has been done.

5 Faithful English translation

Breath — the immortal air; then this body — ending in ashes. Om. O resolve, remember; what has been done, remember. O resolve, remember; what has been done, remember.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Let my breath go to the immortal air, and this body end in ashes. Om. Mind, remember; remember what was done. Mind, remember; remember what was done.

Key concepts वायु breath, air अमृत immortality, the deathless ओम् Om क्रतु resolve, will; the sacrifice कर्मन् action, ritual work

7 Explain it to a beginner

This verse is spoken at the edge of death. It is very plain. The breath, it says, goes back to the wind, which does not die; and this body ends as ashes, as every cremated body does. Nothing is softened. Then comes a call: ‘Remember! Remember what has been done!’ — said twice, the way someone repeats an urgent request. Who is asked to remember? Some teachers say it is the fire, or the divine, asked to remember the good the dying person has done; others say it is the person’s own mind, gathering itself at the last moment. The verse holds two things side by side without contradiction: the honest fact that the body ends, and the conviction that a life’s doing and intending still count.

An analogy It is like the last minutes before handing in an exam: the paper will be collected whatever happens, and in those minutes you gather everything you have learned into what you can still write.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the mantra say, before interpretation?

Two verbless statements — ‘breath, the immortal air’; ‘and this body, ending in ashes’ — followed by Om and a twice-repeated call: ‘O kratu, remember; remember what has been done’. The first half describes the elements of a person dissolving: breath into wind, body into ash. The second half addresses someone, called kratu, and asks for remembering. It is the third of the four closing prayers, and like the others it occurs in Bṛhadāraṇyaka 5.15.1.

Traditional Interpretation

Is this a death rite?

Śaṅkara reads it as the words of the dying meditator: ‘now, as I am about to die’ (अथेदानीं मम मरिष्यतो), the breath is to attain the immortal air, the subtle body, refined by knowledge and works, is to go out (लिङ्गं चेदं ज्ञानकर्मसंस्कृतमुत्क्रामत्विति द्रष्टव्यम्), and the body, offered in the fire, is to become ashes (अथ इदं शरीरमग्नौ हुतं भस्मान्तं भस्मावशेषं भूयात्). He infers the subtle body’s going out from the fact that a path is asked for in the next mantra. Mahīdhara gives the same reading for the Mādhyandina text and adds Kātyāyana’s rule. Deśika does not read the first half as a death rite at all: it states the nature of the pure self, immortal while the body perishes.

Traditional Interpretation

What is kratu, and what does ‘remember’ ask?

For Śaṅkara the Om stands for Brahman under the name Agni, and kratu is its epithet, ‘whose nature is resolve’: हे क्रतो सङ्कल्पात्मक स्मर यन्मम स्मर्तव्यं तस्य कालोऽयं प्रत्युपस्थितः — ‘remember what is to be remembered for me; its time has come’ — and ‘remember the works I have done since childhood’. For Deśika kratu is the Lord as sacrifice, and to remember is to regard the devotee with grace (सानुग्रहया बुद्ध्या विषयीकुरु); he cites the Gītā’s ‘I am the kratu, I am the sacrifice’ (9.16) and the Chāndogya’s ‘a person is made of kratu’. Madhva, citing verses he attributes to the Brahmatarka, insists that the Lord’s ‘remembering’ is not an act of recall, since his knowledge is eternal, but his turning towards grace (अनुग्रहोन्मुखत्वं तु).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What is the grammar of the first half?

vāyur anilam amṛtam has a nominative, then two words that can be masculine accusatives or neuter nominatives. If accusative, a verb of going must be supplied: ‘breath [goes to] the immortal air’ — Śaṅkara’s प्रतिपद्यताम् is such a supplement, which he labels वाक्यशेषः, ‘the remainder of the sentence’. If nominative, the three words are predicates of one subject, as Deśika reads them. atha idaṃ bhasmāntaṃ śarīram is a nominal sentence whichever way the first is taken. The Kāṇva Saṃhitā and the Mādhyandina witness both write ‘om’ with the pluti figure (ओम्३, ओ३म्): the syllable is prolonged in recitation.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantra 17

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The dying meditator wishes that the breath return to the cosmic air, the subtle body go out, and the body become ashes in the fire; he then asks Agni, called Om and kratu, to remember what he has meditated on and done, for ‘the time has come’. The repetition expresses earnestness (क्रतो स्मर कृतं स्मर इति पुनर्वचनमादरार्थम्). The prayer belongs to the path of meditation and works, not to the knowledge of mantra 7.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Vedānta Deśika, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantra 17

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

The first half distinguishes the self from the body: the pure self is vāyu, anila and amṛta, immortal while the body ends in ashes (अनन्तरं तु परिशुद्धात्मस्वरूपमुच्यते); then Om names the Supreme Person, the controller, completing the triad of enjoyer, enjoyed and impeller. kratu is the Lord as sacrifice, and ‘remember what has been done’ asks him, who is grateful, to protect the devotee for whatever service he has done (मत्कृतं यत्किञ्चिदनुकूलमनुसन्धाय कृतज्ञस्त्वं मां रक्षेति भावः).

Dvaita · Madhva, Īśāvāsyopaniṣad-bhāṣya on mantras 17–19 (his numbering)

From the school’s commentary on this mantra

Madhva’s cited verses take anila as ‘whose abode is Brahman’ (अः ब्रह्मैव निलयनं यस्य वायोः सोऽनिलम्) and make Vāyu immortal because his knowledge is never obscured; kratu and Agni are names of Hari (स क्रतुर्ज्ञानरूपत्वादग्निरङ्गप्रणेतृतः); and the Lord’s ‘remembering’ of his devotees is his turning to them in grace, not recollection. Madhva cites these verses from texts he names (Brahmāṇḍa, Rāmasaṃhitā, Brahmatarka); the edition reports them as cited by him.

Where they disagree

The first half is a death rite for Śaṅkara and Mahīdhara and a statement about the self for Deśika; kratu is Agni-Brahman as resolve (Śaṅkara), the Lord as sacrifice (Deśika), or a name of Hari (Madhva); and the deeds to be remembered are the dying person’s works (Śaṅkara) or the devotee’s service seen with grace (Deśika, Madhva).

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • वायुरनिलममृतमथेदं vāyuranilamamṛtamathedaṃ → वायुः + अनिलम् + अमृतम् + अथ + इदम्

    visarga, consonant and vowel sandhi

    vāyuḥ: after u, a visarga that stands for s becomes r before a vowel (P. 8.2.66 (ससजुषो रुँः)); anilam and amṛtam keep their m before a vowel; atha + idam: a + i → e, P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः).

  • भस्मान्तं शरीरम् bhasmāntaṃ śarīram → भस्मान्तम् + शरीरम्

    consonant sandhi (m → anusvāra)

    P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः). The Saṃhitā witnesses write the nasal before ś as ँ (भस्मान्तँ शरीरम्).

  • भस्मान्तं bhasmāntaṃ → भस्मन् + अन्त

    compound-internal

    bhasman loses its final n as the first member of a compound (bhasma-), and a + a → ā: bhasma + anta → bhasmānta.

  • कृतं स्मर kṛtaṃ smara → कृतम् + स्मर

    consonant sandhi (m → anusvāra)

    P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः); written कृतँ स्मर in the Saṃhitā witnesses.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • भस्मान्तम् bhasmāntam

    bahuvrīhi · भस्मन् + अन्त

    Vigraha भस्म अन्तः यस्य तत्

    that whose end is ashes

Morphology

वायुः
The first clause has no verb. Śaṅkara supplies one, ‘may [the breath] attain’: सूत्रात्मानं प्रतिपद्यतामिति वाक्यशेषः, so that anilam amṛtam is its accusative goal: ‘[let my] breath [go to] the immortal air’. Mahīdhara supplies the same. Deśika takes vāyuḥ, anilam and amṛtam as three predicates of the pure self, with no verb supplied; then anilam and amṛtam are neuter nominatives.
ओम्
The Saṃhitā witnesses write the syllable with the pluti figure, ओम्३ / ओ३म्, marking it as prolonged; the Upaniṣad witnesses write ॐ or ओं. The prolongation is a recitational feature and is not shown in the edition’s text.
क्रतो
Vocative of kratu, a word with a wide range in Vedic: will, resolve, intelligence, and the sacrifice. Śaṅkara takes the sense ‘resolve’ (सङ्कल्पात्मक), Deśika the sense ‘sacrifice’ (with the Gītā’s ‘I am the kratu’) or, ‘from the context’, meditation.
स्मर
Imperative of smṛ. The repetition is explained by Śaṅkara as showing earnestness (पुनर्वचनमादरार्थम्) and by Deśika as showing urgency (त्वरातिशयात्).

Anvaya

वायुः अमृतम् अनिलम् [प्रतिपद्यताम्] । अथ इदम् शरीरम् भस्मान्तम् [भूयात्] । ओम् क्रतो स्मर कृतम् स्मर क्रतो स्मर कृतम् स्मर ।

vāyuḥ amṛtam anilam pratipadyatām | atha idam śarīram bhasmāntam bhūyāt | om krato smara kṛtam smara krato smara kṛtam smara |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Two parts. The first is two nominal sentences without a finite verb: ‘breath — the immortal air’; ‘and this body — ending in ashes’. They can be completed as wishes (Śaṅkara: ‘may the breath attain the immortal air, may this body end in ashes’) or as statements about the self and the body (Deśika). The second part is a formula: Om, then a vocative (krato) and imperatives (smara) with an object (kṛtam), said twice. The anvaya follows Śaṅkara’s construction and marks his supplied verbs.

Ambiguities

vāyuḥ: breath or self
Śaṅkara and Mahīdhara: the vital breath of the dying, leaving its individual limits for the cosmic air, the ‘thread-self’ (अध्यात्मपरिच्छेदं हित्वा अधिदैवतात्मानं सर्वात्मकम् अनिलम् अमृतं सूत्रात्मानं). Deśika: the pure self, called vāyu because it travels, anila because it has no fixed abode, amṛta because it does not die when the body does (अनन्तरं तु परिशुद्धात्मस्वरूपमुच्यते). Madhva: vāyu is the deity Vāyu, immortal because his knowledge is never obscured; anila ‘whose abode is Brahman’.
kratu: resolve, sacrifice, or the Lord
Śaṅkara: the vocative addresses Om, the Brahman called Agni, as ‘you whose nature is resolve’. Deśika: the Lord in the form of the sacrifice, or (by context) meditation itself. Madhva’s cited verse makes kratu a name of Hari ‘because he is knowledge’ (स क्रतुर्ज्ञानरूपत्वादग्निरङ्गप्रणेतृतः).
Who remembers what
‘Remember what has been done.’ Śaṅkara: Agni (as kratu) is asked to remember the works the dying person has performed since childhood. Deśika: the Lord is asked to regard with grace whatever good the devotee has done — or what the Lord himself has done for him. The mantra does not say whose deeds are meant.
The Mādhyandina text
VS 40.15 reads ‘om krato smara | klibe smara | kṛtaṃ smara’. Mahīdhara takes klibe as a Vedic form of klṛp: ‘remember, for the world that is fashioned [for enjoyment]’. He also reports Kātyāyana’s rule that the yogin is reminded of his sacrifices at the end with these yajus ('क्रतो इत्यादि त्रिभिर्यजुर्भिरन्ते यज्ञान्योगी स्मारयति' ( अनु० ४ । ९) इति कात्यायनः).
Madhva’s division
Madhva divides this mantra into two (his 18 and 19), and his text repeats Om before the second ‘krato smara’ (corrections.json, variant).

Metre

prose · syllables counted as written: 17 + 17

Not a metrical verse: counted as written, 17 syllables in the first half and 16 in the second. Mahīdhara calls the Mādhyandina form ‘two yajus’ (द्वे यजुषी), that is, prose formulas, and the second half is a ritual formula repeated.

Textual variations between witnesses

  • Other recension Mādhyandina VS 40 (svara, pada-pāṭha): क्रतो स्मर क्लिबे स्मर कृतं स्मर — VS 40.15 (Mādhyandina) reads ‘om krato smara | klibe smara | kṛtaṃ smara’: klibe in place of the repeated ‘kṛtaṃ smara krato smara’ of the Kāṇva text. Mahīdhara takes klibe as a Vedic form of klṛp, ‘the world that is fashioned [for enjoyment]’: कल्प्यते भोगायेति क्लृप् लोकः तस्मै स्मर.
  • Other recension Mādhyandina VS 40 (svara, pada-pāṭha): क्लिबे — Part of the same Mādhyandina reading.
  • Variant reading Madhva’s mūla: ॐ क्रतो स्मर कृतं स्मर ॐ क्रतो स्मर — Madhva’s text repeats ॐ before the second ‘krato smara’.
  • Witness error Vedānta Deśika’s mūla: भस्मान्ँत — The keyed mūla of Deśika has भस्मान्ँत (a misplaced nasal sign) for भस्मान्तँ.
  • Witness error Vedānta Deśika’s mūla: भस्मान्ँत — Same slip.

  • Chāndogya Upaniṣad 3.14.1 Doctrinal comparison

    अथ खलु क्रतुमयः पुरुषो

    atha khalu kratumayaḥ puruṣo

    ‘A person is made of resolve (kratu); as his resolve is in this world, so he becomes on departing.’ The doctrine that gives weight to a resolve at death; Deśika alludes to this passage.

  • Bhagavad Gītā 8.6 Doctrinal comparison

    यं यं वापि स्मरन्भावं त्यजत्यन्ते कलेबरम्

    yaṃ yaṃ vāpi smaranbhāvaṃ tyajatyante kalebaram

    Whatever state one remembers when leaving the body at the end, to that one goes. The Gītā develops the same link between remembering and the moment of death.

    ‘I am the kratu, I am the sacrifice.’ Deśika cites it to explain the vocative krato as the Lord in the form of the sacrifice.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Aging and mortality

Much modern discussion of death avoids its physical facts. This mantra states them flatly — breath to air, body to ashes — and then turns to what a life has been for. People who sit with the dying often describe something like the mantra’s second half: a gathering of attention, a reviewing of what was done, a wish to be remembered rightly. The mantra suggests that facing the end of the body plainly and caring about what one has done are not in tension. This is a reflection on the teaching, not part of the commentaries.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin If you had to tell someone, in one sentence, what you have done that you would most like remembered, what would you say?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry What in a person ‘remembers’ at the end — and is the one who is asked to remember outside us, within us, or both?

A contemplation exercise At the end of a day, sit quietly for five minutes and recall what you did, from morning to evening, without judging. Close by naming one thing you would like to carry into tomorrow.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
