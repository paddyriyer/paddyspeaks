The Upaniṣads

How to Study

Three reading paths, one text, and a way to tell the ancient words from the modern voice

Every mantra page contains everything the edition has: the verified Sanskrit and its analysis, translations, the commentaries, comparisons and modern reflections. The three reading paths change only what is open when the page loads. This page explains the paths, the parts of a mantra page, the five labels and how the text was verified.

Choose a path, not a level of access

Direct Textual Meaning

The Curious Reader path opens the Sanskrit (with the transliteration if you want it), the readable English, the plain-language explanation with its analogy, and one question to think about. It is written for younger readers and for anyone meeting Hindu philosophy for the first time; it avoids technical terms or explains them.

The Serious Student path opens, in addition, the padaccheda (the words separated), the word-by-word table, the literal translation, the traditional commentary, the connections with other scriptures and the key concepts.

The Scholar path opens everything: sandhi and compounds, morphology and anvaya, syntax and ambiguities, metre, the textual witnesses and their variants, the comparison of the schools with their disagreements, and the sources for every claim.

The paths are preferences, not gates. Every section is on every page in every path, as a heading you can open. Your choice is remembered on this device only.

The parts of a mantra page

Direct Textual Meaning

Each mantra appears in the same order. 1 The Sanskrit in Devanagari, with its number; below it, where the Saṃhitā witness has it, the accented text as that witness keys it. 2 The IAST transliteration, generated from the Devanagari so that the two cannot disagree. 3 The padaccheda. 4 Word by word: each word with its transliteration, meaning, grammatical role and form. 5 A faithful translation, word for word, with supplied words in brackets. 6 A readable English rendering, labelled as interpretive. 7 An explanation for a beginner, with one analogy. 8 The traditional commentary, as questions the mantra raises. 9 Comparative Vedānta: Advaita, Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita, each with its basis. 10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis. 11 Connections with other scriptures. 12 Contemporary relevance. 13 Reflect and contemplate. 14 Sources and verification.

Every mantra has its own address (for example /upanishads/isha/mantra-06.html), so a mantra can be cited or shared on its own. Previous and next links, and a list of all eighteen, are at the top and bottom of each page.

The five labels

Direct Textual Meaning

Every block of commentary carries one label. Direct Textual Meaning says what the Sanskrit itself states. Traditional Interpretation reports what a named commentary concludes, in its own words where possible. Comparative Vedānta sets the schools beside each other. Linguistic and Textual Analysis concerns grammar, sandhi, the witnesses and metre. Contemporary Reflection is the modern voice: it applies the teaching to present life and never claims to be what the text says. In the introductory guide a sixth label, Academic History, marks claims about dates and development made by modern scholarship.

School positions carry a second marker, their basis: from that school’s commentary on this mantra (the usual case here, since all three schools have commentaries on the Īśā), from a broader doctrine of the school, or a modern comparative inference.

How the text was verified

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The Sanskrit is not typed from memory. The base text is the GRETIL transcription of the Kāṇva recension, whose mūla GRETIL reports as checked against the printed edition of Limaye and Vadekar (Poona, 1958). Five other witnesses were collated with it letter by letter: the text printed with Śaṅkara’s commentary (Advaita Sharada), the accented Kāṇva Saṃhitā, the Mādhyandina Saṃhitā with its pada-pāṭha, and the texts printed with Madhva’s and Vedānta Deśika’s commentaries. Every difference is listed and classified on the Sources page.

Each padaccheda is proven by a program that rejoins the separated words by the rules of sandhi and checks that they spell the mantra. Every Sanskrit phrase quoted from a commentator is checked against that commentator’s text on that mantra. Every parallel passage is checked against the text of the work cited. Every citation of Pāṇini’s grammar is checked against the text of the sūtra. What cannot be verified this way — the translations, the explanations, the judgement in the commentary sections — has not yet been reviewed by a Sanskrit scholar, and the pages say so.

A way to read

Contemporary Reflection

Read the whole Upaniṣad straight through first, in the readable English (the ‘Read straight through’ page); it takes ten minutes. Then take one mantra a day, or one group — 1–3, 4–8, 9–11, 12–14, 15–18 — and read it in your chosen path. Say the Sanskrit aloud even if you cannot yet follow it; the transliteration is exact. Write down the question at the end of each page and come back to it. When a mantra puzzles you, read the study that discusses it: there are six, one for each of the Upaniṣad’s central problems.

The contemplation exercises are invitations to attend, not therapies; nothing in this edition makes a medical or psychological claim.
