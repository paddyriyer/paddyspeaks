The Upaniṣads

Introduction to the Upaniṣads

From first questions to self-knowledge: what these texts are, where they stand, and how to read them

The Upaniṣads are the closing portions of the Vedic schools and the foundation of the philosophies called Vedānta. This guide explains the word, the place of the Upaniṣads in Vedic literature, the vocabulary they share, why some are called principal, why they teach by story, paradox and negation, and how traditional accounts of revelation differ from academic accounts of date. Each section carries a label that says what kind of claim it makes.

What ‘Upaniṣad’ means

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Three explanations of the word are current, and they answer different questions. The first is etymological: upaniṣad is formed from the root sad, ‘to sit’, with the prefixes upa, ‘near’, and ni, ‘down’. The picture often drawn from this — students sitting down near a teacher — is a reasonable guess at the image behind the word, but the texts themselves never explain the word that way, and it should not be presented as its meaning.

The second is traditional. Śaṅkara, introducing his commentary on the Kaṭha, derives the word from sad in its senses of destroying, reaching and loosening: सदेर्धातोर्विशरणगत्यवसादनार्थस्योपनिपूर्वस्य क्विप्प्रत्ययान्तस्य रूपमिदम् ‘उपनिषत्’ इति. For him the word names first a kind of knowledge — the knowledge that destroys the seed of saṃsāra in those who approach it, and leads them to Brahman — and names the book only secondarily, because the book serves that knowledge: ग्रन्थस्यापि तादर्थ्येन तच्छब्दत्वोपपत्तेः.

The third is historical. Studying how the word is used in the older texts, where it often refers to a hidden connection or equivalence between things (between a ritual act and a cosmic power, for example), modern scholars commonly translate it ‘connection’, ‘correspondence’ or ‘secret teaching’. This explains the word from its use, not from its parts. The three accounts do not compete: one says what the word is made of, one what it means to the tradition that lives by it, and one how it was used when the texts were composed.

Śruti and smṛti

Traditional Interpretation

The tradition divides its authoritative texts into two classes. Śruti, ‘what is heard’, is the Veda: texts that, on the traditional account, no person composed, which were ‘seen’ or ‘heard’ by the seers (ṛṣi) and handed down orally with great care. Śruti is authoritative in itself. Smṛti, ‘what is remembered’, is everything that depends on śruti for its authority: the law books, the epics, the Purāṇas. The Upaniṣads are śruti. The Bhagavad Gītā, though it is part of the Mahābhārata and therefore smṛti, is treated by the Vedānta schools as carrying the Upaniṣads’ teaching, and is commented on alongside them.

The distinction concerns authority, not age or importance. A smṛti text may be loved more than a śruti text and recited more often; what it cannot do, in the tradition’s own terms, is contradict śruti and remain authoritative.

Saṃhitā, Brāhmaṇa, Āraṇyaka, Upaniṣad

Academic History

Each of the four Vedas — Ṛg, Yajus (in a ‘black’ and a ‘white’ form), Sāman and Atharvan — was preserved by schools (śākhā), and each school’s body of text came to have four layers. The Saṃhitā is the collection of mantras: hymns, formulas and chants used in ritual. The Brāhmaṇas are prose works that explain the ritual and its meaning. The Āraṇyakas, ‘forest texts’, deal with rites of special power and begin to reflect on their inner meaning. The Upaniṣads continue that reflection and turn it, more and more, to the self and to the ground of all things.

The layers are not sealed compartments. The Bṛhadāraṇyaka is both the end of a Brāhmaṇa (the Śatapatha) and, as its name says, an Āraṇyaka. The Taittirīya and the Aitareya stand inside Āraṇyakas. The Chāndogya is the end of a Brāhmaṇa. The Īśā is unusual: it is the last chapter of a Saṃhitā, the Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā of the Śukla Yajurveda, so that its units are called mantras. Vedānta Deśika makes a point of this placement: the Upaniṣad was set at the end of the Saṃhitā to show that, unlike the mantras before it, it serves knowledge rather than ritual (विद्यार्थं स्यादिति व्यङ्क्तुं निबन्धोऽस्य तदन्ततः).

Much of this is the language of modern description; the tradition itself speaks more often of two parts of the Veda, the part concerned with action (karmakāṇḍa) and the part concerned with knowledge (jñānakāṇḍa), with the Upaniṣads as the second. Mahīdhara opens his comment on the Īśā exactly so: thirty-nine chapters have set out the part concerned with action, and the knowledge part is now set out in one.

What is Vedānta?

Traditional Interpretation

Vedānta means ‘the end of the Veda’, in two senses: the last part of the Vedic corpus — the Upaniṣads — and its culmination, the knowledge to which the rest leads. The word also names the schools of philosophy that take the Upaniṣads as their authority and try to state, consistently, what they teach. Those schools agree that the Upaniṣads teach Brahman, the ultimate reality, and that knowing it is the highest human end. They disagree about how Brahman, the individual self and the world are related.

Three schools are compared throughout this edition. Advaita (‘non-duality’), the school of Śaṅkara, holds that the self is Brahman and that the world of distinct things is not real apart from it. Viśiṣṭādvaita (‘non-duality of the qualified’), the school of Rāmānuja, for which the commentator on the Īśā is Vedānta Deśika, holds that Brahman is the one Lord, qualified by real selves and a real world that are his body. Dvaita (‘duality’), the school of Madhva, holds that the Lord, the selves and matter are eternally distinct, and everything depends on the Lord. Each school reads every Upaniṣad, including this one, as teaching its view.

The three foundations (prasthānatraya)

Traditional Interpretation

A Vedānta school establishes itself by commenting on three works: the Upaniṣads (śruti), the Bhagavad Gītā (smṛti that summarises them) and the Brahmasūtra of Bādarāyaṇa, a work of short aphorisms that organises the Upaniṣads’ teaching and answers objections to it. Together they are called the prasthānatraya, ‘the three points of departure’. The relation is close in practice: the Brahmasūtra’s sūtras are mostly unintelligible without the Upaniṣadic passages they discuss, and its commentators quote those passages constantly. Śaṅkara’s commentary on Brahmasūtra 3.4, for example, quotes Īśā mantra 2 under three sūtras (3.4.7, 3.4.13, 3.4.14) in the course of deciding whether ritual action is binding on the person who knows.

The shared vocabulary

Direct Textual Meaning

A small set of words recurs across the Upaniṣads and the schools that read them. Brahman is the ultimate reality, ‘the great’. Ātman is the self — one’s own self, and, in Vedānta, the conscious principle that is one’s real nature. Jīva is the living, embodied individual that acts and experiences. Jagat is the world, literally ‘the moving’: what is always changing. Īśvara (in the Īśā, simply īś) is the Lord, the ruler of the world. Avidyā and vidyā are usually translated ‘ignorance’ and ‘knowledge’, though the Īśā uses them in a sense the commentators explain quite differently (see its mantras 9–11). Karma is action, especially the action the Veda prescribes, and the results it brings. Mokṣa is liberation from the cycle of birth and death.

How these words relate is exactly what the schools dispute. That ātman and Brahman are connected, the Upaniṣads say repeatedly; whether the connection is identity, inseparable relation, or dependence is not something a glossary can settle. The glossary on this site gives each school’s sense where it differs.

Why some Upaniṣads are ‘principal’

Academic History

More than two hundred works call themselves Upaniṣads. A late text, the Muktikā Upaniṣad, cast as a dialogue between Rāma and Hanumān, lists one hundred and eight and grades them: the Māṇḍūkya alone suffices for liberation (माण्डूक्यमेकमेवालं मुमुक्षूणाम् विमुक्तये); if knowledge does not arise, one should read the ten (दशोपनिषदं पठ); then thirty-two; then all one hundred and eight. Its list opens with the ten: ईशकेनकठप्रश्नमुण्डमाण्डूक्यतित्तिरिः । ऐतरेयम् च छान्दोग्यं बृहदारण्यकम् तथा. The Muktikā text used here is a digital text without a recorded printed source; the verses quoted were checked only against that text.

These ten are the ones on which commentaries attributed to Śaṅkara survive — accepted by the tradition, though modern scholars have questioned the authorship of one or two — and they are the ones every later Vedānta school treats as authoritative. That is what ‘principal’ means: not a judgement of quality, but the set the commentarial tradition is built on. Scholars working historically speak instead of the ‘early’ or ‘classical’ Upaniṣads, a slightly larger group that adds, for instance, the Śvetāśvatara, Kauṣītaki and Maitrī, because these share the language and concerns of the oldest layer. The hundred and eight of the Muktikā include many later works devoted to yoga, renunciation and particular deities.

Revelation and chronology

Academic History

Two kinds of account should not be confused. The traditional account holds that the Veda, the Upaniṣads included, is not the work of any author and is without beginning; the seers did not compose it but perceived it. That is a claim about the nature and authority of the texts, and it is not settled by evidence of the kind historians use.

The academic account dates the texts relatively, by their language, their metre, their references to one another and to datable events. On the account most widely given — summarised, for example, in Patrick Olivelle’s translations (Upaniṣads, 1996; The Early Upaniṣads, 1998) — the Bṛhadāraṇyaka and Chāndogya are the oldest, prose works usually placed before the Buddha, around the seventh and sixth centuries BCE; the Taittirīya, Aitareya and Kauṣītaki follow; the verse Upaniṣads — Kena, Kaṭha, Īśā, Śvetāśvatara, Muṇḍaka — belong to the last centuries BCE; the Praśna and Māṇḍūkya come later still, perhaps near the beginning of the common era. Every one of these dates is approximate and disputed, and for texts transmitted orally a ‘date’ means the period in which a text took roughly its present shape.

Holding the two accounts apart is not evasion. A reader can study the Īśā as śruti, as a document of the history of Indian thought, or as both, provided it is clear at each point which question is being asked.

Stories, paradoxes, dialogues and negation

Direct Textual Meaning

The Upaniṣads rarely argue in the manner of a treatise. They tell stories: a boy, Naciketas, sent to Death and refusing every gift but the knowledge of what lies beyond death (Kaṭha); a father teaching his son, with salt dissolved in water, that the invisible pervades the visible (Chāndogya 6). They stage dialogues and debates: Yājñavalkya with his wife Maitreyī, who asks whether wealth will make her immortal (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.4), and with the court of King Janaka. They state paradoxes: ‘it moves, it does not move; it is far, it is near’ (Īśā 5). And they negate: the self is described as ‘not this, not this’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.3.6).

Each form does something an argument cannot. A story shows a teaching at work in a life; a dialogue lets the student’s objections be heard; a paradox stops the mind from settling on a single picture of what cannot be pictured; negation removes descriptions without putting another in their place. The Īśā uses almost all of these in eighteen mantras: an instruction (1), a paradox (4–5), a vision (6–7), a description by negation (8: bodiless, without wound, without sinews), a received teaching from ‘the wise’ (10, 13) and, at the end, the prayers of a dying person.

  • Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 2.3.6: नेति नेति neti neti

Ritual, meditation, knowledge and liberation

Comparative Vedānta

The Upaniṣads grew out of a ritual culture and never simply reject it. They reinterpret it, relocate it within the person, and set beside it — or above it — knowledge of the self. How the three are related is one of the great questions of Vedānta, and the Īśā is one of the texts on which it is argued.

Śaṅkara holds that knowledge of the Self alone liberates, and that ritual action, which presupposes a doer who wants a result, cannot be combined with it; ritual and meditation on deities purify the mind and bring other results. In his closing remarks on the Īśā he says that its mantras teach a combination with meditation, not with knowledge of the supreme Self: उपासनया समुच्चयो न परमात्मविज्ञानेनेति. The theistic schools hold that knowledge of the Lord is itself a meditative devotion, and that action done as worship supports it. The Īśā’s second mantra (‘doing works here, one should wish to live a hundred years’) and its mantras 9–11 are read in each of these ways; the studies on this site set the readings out side by side.

How this collection reads the texts

Contemporary Reflection

This collection begins with the Īśā because it is short, complete and central: eighteen mantras that every school comments on, that raise most of the questions the longer Upaniṣads explore, and whose text survives in two recensions that can be compared word by word. Each mantra is presented with its verified Sanskrit, a word-by-word analysis, a close and a readable translation, a plain explanation, the traditional commentaries in their own words, and a clearly marked modern reflection. The aim is that a reader can always tell the ancient text from the modern voice explaining it.
