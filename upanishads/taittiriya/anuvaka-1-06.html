Taittirīya Upaniṣad · Śīkṣāvallī · Anuvāka 6 of 12

The person in the heart, and the way out through the crown

The Brahman of the four calls is located within the heart as a person made of mind, and the path of one who meditates on it is traced upward and out through the crown into fire, wind, sun and Brahman.

5 Faithful English translation

1.6.1 That space which [is] within the heart — in it [is] this person made of mind, immortal, golden. Between the two [sides of the] palate, that which hangs down like a teat — that [is] the passage of Indra, where that root of the hair parts; having parted the two skull-bones, with ‘bhūḥ’ he is established in fire, with ‘bhuvaḥ’ in wind,

1.6.2 with ‘suvar’ in the sun, with ‘mahaḥ’ in Brahman. He attains self-rule; he attains the lord of mind. [He becomes] lord of speech, lord of the eye, lord of the ear, lord of understanding. This then he becomes: Brahman whose body is space, whose self is truth, whose delight is in the breaths, whose mind [is] joy, rich in peace, immortal. Thus, O Prācīnayogya, meditate.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

1.6.1 There is a space within the heart. In it is this person, made of mind, immortal, made of light. Between the two sides of the palate hangs a piece of flesh like a teat; beyond it is the passage of Indra, which goes up to where the hair parts on the crown and passes out through the skull. Through it, with the call bhūḥ, a person comes to rest in fire; with bhuvaḥ, in wind;

1.6.2 with suvar, in the sun; with mahaḥ, in Brahman. Such a person attains self-rule and reaches the lord of the mind, becoming lord of speech, of sight, of hearing, of understanding. Then this follows: Brahman, whose body is space, whose nature is truth, whose delight is in life’s breaths, whose mind is joy, full of peace, deathless. Meditate on it so, Prācīnayogya.

7 Explain it to a beginner

The previous passage described the greatest reality as the centre of everything. This one says where to find it: in a small space inside the heart, where there lives a ‘person made of mind’, deathless and shining. Then it describes a route through the body. From the heart a channel rises, passes the soft flesh that hangs at the back of the throat, and goes up to the crown of the head, where the hair parts. Someone who has meditated in the way taught here is said to leave the body by this route and to rest, step by step, in fire, wind, the sun and finally in the greatest reality itself. There such a person becomes master of their own mind, speech, sight, hearing and understanding. The passage ends with a teacher telling a student, by name: meditate on it this way.

An analogy Like a lighthouse keeper who climbs from the room at the base of the tower, up the narrow stair, and out onto the lamp gallery at the top: the climb is inside the building, and the view at the top is of everything outside it.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the passage actually say?

It says that in the space within the heart there is a person made of mind, immortal and golden. It describes a passage, ‘the passage of Indra’, between the sides of the palate where something hangs like a teat, reaching to where the hair parts, through the skull. By the four calls of the previous anuvāka someone is established in fire, wind, sun and Brahman; that person attains self-rule and the lord of mind, and becomes lord of speech, eye, ear and understanding. It then lists the qualities of Brahman — body of space, self of truth, delight in the breaths, mind of joy, full of peace, immortal — and tells Prācīnayogya to meditate so.

Traditional Interpretation

Why is the heart named as the place of meditation?

Śaṅkara: the heart is given as the place where the Brahman of the calls can be directly met, as Viṣṇu is met in a sacred stone — हृदयाकाशः स्थानमुच्यते, सालग्राम इव विष्णोः — and the path is given for the one who would become the self of all. He describes the heart as the lotus-shaped organ पुण्डरीकाकारो मांसपिण्डः, with the space inside it करकाकाशवत्, like the space within a pot.

Traditional Interpretation

What is the path through the crown?

Śaṅkara identifies the passage with the channel called suṣumnā, known in the yoga texts, rising from the heart: सुषुम्ना नाम नाडी योगशास्त्रेषु प्रसिद्धा. The knower leaves through the crown and becomes established in fire, wind and sun, which are the limbs of the great Brahman, and then in Brahman, their whole. Kūranārāyaṇa reads it as the path of the gods: one who meditates on the calls reaches, in turn, the worlds of fire, wind and sun and the world of Brahman, the highest heaven — मह इति ब्रह्मणि - ब्रह्मलोकं, परमव्योम गच्छति.

Comparative Vedānta

What does ‘self-rule’ mean here?

For Śaṅkara svārājya is becoming a sovereign over the deities who are limbs, as Brahman is: स्वयमेव राजा अधिपतिर्भवति अङ्गभूतानां देवतानां यथा ब्रह्म; the knower becomes lord of every mind, every speech and every eye because Brahman is the self of all. For Kūranārāyaṇa it is freedom from the power of karma, service of the Lord, the end of every obstacle: अकर्मवश्यत्वं भगवत्कैङ्कर्यं प्रतिबन्धकनिवृत्तिरूपं.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

How are the attributes of Brahman built?

Five of the six are bahuvrīhi compounds in the neuter, agreeing with brahma: ‘whose body is space’, ‘whose self is truth’, ‘whose delight is in the breaths’. Śaṅkara gives two analyses of several, for instance आकाशः शरीरमस्य, आकाशवद्वा सूक्ष्मं शरीरमस्येत्याकाशशरीरम्. The pair manaḥ ānandam is written as two words, not a compound; Śaṅkara still reads it as one description, ‘whose mind is joy’. The anuvāka ends with a vocative and an imperative, the form of a teacher’s instruction, which Śaṅkara calls a sign of earnestness: आचार्यवचनोक्तिरादरार्था.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Śīkṣāvallī 6

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

The person in the heart is the Brahman of the calls, Hiraṇyagarbha, here meditated on with qualities (manomaya and the rest); the other deities are its limbs: हिरण्यगर्भस्य व्याहृत्यात्मनो ब्रह्मणोऽङ्गान्यन्या देवता. The meditation brings the knower, after death, to identity with that Brahman and its sovereignty. It is meditation (upāsana) on Brahman with qualities, which Śaṅkara keeps distinct from the knowledge of Brahman without qualities taught in the second vallī.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Kūranārāyaṇa; Gopālānanda (modern), commentaries on Śīkṣāvallī 6 (six-commentary edition)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Kūranārāyaṇa reads the anuvāka as the way the one devoted to the meditation on the calls reaches the Lord by the path of the gods: देवयानमार्गेण भगवत्प्राप्तिप्रकार उच्यते. On reaching him the released self becomes master of its powers and comes to have Brahman as its self; the attributes show that the Lord and the released have divine form and unbounded joy. The modern commentator Gopālānanda calls this anuvāka the higher knowledge to which the calls were a limb: अथ तदङ्गिभूतां परविद्यामाह.

Dvaita · Madhva, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, on the sixth anuvāka

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Madhva cites verses he attributes to a work he calls Brahmasāra (इति ब्रह्मसारे). In them the forms of Viṣṇu are placed along the path: Aniruddha in the space of the heart, Pradyumna in the palate, and so on — य एष हृदयाकाशस्त्वनिरुद्धस्तु तद्गतः. The closing instruction is Viṣṇu’s words to Brahmā: इत्युपास्व प्राथमिको योग्यस्त्वं मदुपासने. These are verses cited by Madhva; this edition cannot verify them as quotations of an independent work.

Where they disagree

All read a meditation and a path out of the body. They differ on who the person in the heart is (Brahman with qualities for Śaṅkara; the individual self for Kūranārāyaṇa; the supreme self for Gopālānanda; Aniruddha for Madhva), on what the destination is (identity with Hiraṇyagarbha, or the service of the Lord in his world), and on whether the closing list describes Brahman or the released self.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • 1.6.1 स य एषोऽन्तर्हृदय आकाशः sa ya eṣo'ntarhṛdaya ākāśaḥ → सः + यः + एषः + अन्तर्हृदये + आकाशः

    visarga of saḥ; aḥ before a vowel; aḥ + a; e before a vowel

    saḥ drops its visarga before a consonant: P. 6.1.132 (एतत्तदोः सुलोपोऽकोरनञ्समासे हलि). yaḥ before e loses the visarga with hiatus. eṣaḥ before a becomes eṣo and the a is elided (avagraha): P. 6.1.113 (अतो रोरप्लुतादप्लुते), then P. 6.1.109 (एङः पदान्तादति). hṛdaye before ā becomes hṛdaya with hiatus: e → ay, P. 6.1.78 (एचोऽयवायावः), and the y dropped, P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य).

  • 1.6.1 तस्मिन्नयं tasminnayaṃ → तस्मिन् + अयम्

    doubling of n

    A final n after a short vowel is doubled before a vowel: P. 8.3.32 (ङमो ह्रस्वादचि ङमुण्नित्यम्).

  • 1.6.1 इवावलम्बते ivāvalambate → इव + अवलम्बते

    vowel sandhi (savarṇa-dīrgha)

    a + a → ā: P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः).

  • 1.6.1 सेन्द्रयोनिः sendrayoniḥ → सा + इन्द्रयोनिः

    vowel sandhi (guṇa)

    ā + i → e: P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः).

Morphology

1.6.1 मनोमयः
manas with the suffix -maya, ‘consisting of, made of’: ‘made of mind’. Śaṅkara offers ‘known through the mind’ and ‘identified with the mind’ (तदुपलभ्यत्वात्, तदभिमानी तन्मयः).
1.6.1 हिरण्मयः
hiraṇya, ‘gold’, with -maya; the second a of hiraṇya is dropped (hiraṇmaya). Śaṅkara glosses ‘made of light’.
1.6.1 तालुके
Accusative dual of tāluka, ‘palate’, governed by antareṇa: ‘between the two [sides of the] palate’.
1.6.1 व्यपोह्य
Absolutive of ūh with the preverbs vi and apa (vy-apa-ūhya, the ū shortened by the absolutive suffix -ya): ‘having pushed apart’. The subject, the channel, is understood: it passes out having parted the skull.
1.6.1 प्रतितिष्ठति
Present of sthā (reduplicated tiṣṭha-) with prati: ‘stands firm in, is established in’. Its subject, the one who meditates, is not named until the next unit (āpnoti).
1.6.2 स्वाराज्यम्
An abstract noun from svarāj, ‘self-ruler’, with vṛddhi of the first syllable: ‘the state of ruling oneself’, sovereignty.
1.6.2 सत्यात्म
The neuter nominative of the bahuvrīhi satyātman, agreeing with brahma (an n-stem neuter loses its n in the nominative).
1.6.2 उपास्स्व
Imperative 2nd singular middle of ās with upa (upāsva). The doubled s is a Taittirīya spelling; Madhva’s text writes upāsva (corrections.json, orthographic).
1.6.2 प्राचीनयोग्य
Vocative. Read as a name (a patronymic of Prācīnayoga) or, by Kūranārāyaṇa and Madhva, as a description of the student: ‘you who have been made fit by the preceding’ (see ambiguities).

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

The first sentence is a correlative: saḥ … yaḥ eṣa antarhṛdaya ākāśaḥ, tasmin … ‘that space which is within the heart — in it’, with saḥ taken up by ayaṃ puruṣaḥ (Śaṅkara). A string of attributes follows. A second correlative (yaḥ eṣa stana ivāvalambate — sā indrayoniḥ) locates the passage, and an absolutive (vyapohya) carries the movement through the skull to four parallel clauses with prati-tiṣṭhati understood after the first. The subject of the verbs āpnoti … bhavati is the knower, never named. The last two units are a list of neuter attributes of brahma, closed by the teacher’s imperative to a named student.

Ambiguities

Who is the person made of mind?
Śaṅkara: Brahman itself, met in the heart as an image is met in a sacred stone — सालग्राम इव विष्णोः — and realised there पाणाविवामलकम्. Kūranārāyaṇa takes ‘this person’ as the individual self, known by a mind purified in yoga (अयं पुरुषः जीवात्मा); Gopālānanda as the supreme self named by mahaḥ (परमात्मा).
Whose ‘passage’ is the indrayoni?
Indra may be Brahman (Śaṅkara, इन्द्रस्य ब्रह्मणः; Gopālānanda, इन्द्रस्य परमेश्वरस्य), or the individual self, lord of the body’s powers (Kūranārāyaṇa, इन्द्रस्य - जीवात्मनः, शरीरेश्वरस्य वा). yoni may be ‘the way of reaching’ or ‘the place of going out’.
Does ‘ākāśaśarīraṃ brahma …’ describe what is meditated on, or what the knower becomes?
After ‘this then he becomes’, the neuter list can be read as the knower’s new state (Kūranārāyaṇa: ब्रह्मात्मको भवति; Gopālānanda: the released one attains likeness to Brahman), or as the description of the Brahman to be meditated on, which the closing imperative takes up (Śaṅkara: एवं मनोमयत्वादिधर्मैर्विशिष्टं यथोक्तं ब्रह्म हे प्राचीनयोग्य, उपास्स्व; he reads etat tato bhavati as ‘more than that, this [he becomes]’).
Is Prācīnayogya a name?
It has the form of a patronymic, and Kūranārāyaṇa names the rule for one (P. 4.1.92 (तस्यापत्यम्)); he also offers ‘you who are made fit by the preceding limbs of knowledge’ (प्राचीनैः पूर्वोक्तैः, विद्याङ्गैः भगवदुपासनयोग्यतामापन्न हे शिष्य). Madhva reads ‘you who are first fit for my worship’, spoken by Viṣṇu to Brahmā.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Technology and digital distractions

The reward named here is not a possession but a kind of mastery: to be ‘lord of speech, of the eye, of the ear, of understanding’. Much of modern life works the other way round: the eye and the ear are directed by whatever is designed to capture them. The passage locates the beginning of mastery in a quiet place within, and only then speaks of command over the senses. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin What would it mean to be ‘in charge of your own eyes and ears’ for one day?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Why does the text describe Brahman with qualities like ‘whose mind is joy’ and ‘full of peace’ at the end of a path, rather than at its beginning?

A contemplation exercise Sit for two minutes with attention on the centre of the chest. When attention is pulled elsewhere, notice by what — sight, sound, thought — and return.
