Taittirīya Upaniṣad · Śīkṣāvallī · Anuvāka 12 of 12

The closing prayer: ‘I have spoken the truth; it has protected me’

The opening prayer returns at the end of the Śīkṣāvallī with its verbs in the past: what was asked for has been given.

5 Faithful English translation

[May] Mitra [be] well-being for us, [may] Varuṇa [be] well-being; may Aryaman be well-being for us; [may] Indra [and] Bṛhaspati [be] well-being for us; [may] wide-striding Viṣṇu [be] well-being for us. 7 Explain it to a beginner

At the very end of this first part of the Upaniṣad, the opening prayer comes back, almost word for word. Only two things change. At the start the student said, ‘I will speak what is right and true,’ and asked, ‘May this protect me and my teacher.’ Now he says, ‘I have spoken what is right and true,’ and, ‘It has protected me and my teacher.’ The promise has been kept, and the help that was asked for has been received. The prayer for kindness from the powers of nature remains, because learning never really ends. It is a quiet way of closing a chapter: by saying thank you, and by noticing that the hope you began with came true.

An analogy Like a diary that opens a journey with ‘I hope we arrive safely’ and closes it with ‘We arrived safely’: the same words, one tense later, carrying the whole trip between them.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the passage actually say?

It repeats the prayer of 1.1 — the wishes for well-being from Mitra, Varuṇa, Aryaman, Indra, Bṛhaspati and Viṣṇu, the homage to Brahman and to Vāyu as ‘the perceptible Brahman’ — but now says ‘I have called you the perceptible Brahman; I have spoken the right; I have spoken the true; that has protected me, has protected the speaker’. In the base text the anuvāka has no om at its beginning and no śānti at its end.

Traditional Interpretation

Why is it here?

Śaṅkara says only that this śānti is recited to calm the obstacles to the knowledge that has been obtained: अतीतविद्याप्राप्त्युपसर्गशमनार्थां शान्तिं पठति, and that the words have been explained before. Rāmānanda calls it the śānti-pāṭha to be read at the end of the chapter (अध्यायसमाप्तौ पठनीयः शान्तिपाठः). Kūranārāyaṇa notes that the Sāṃhitī upaniṣads are framed by this anuvāka at their beginning and end.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What is the force of the past tenses?

The only grammatical difference from 1.1 is tense. Kūranārāyaṇa: अवादिषम् इति भूतकालप्रत्ययो विशेषः — the special point is the suffix of past time. Gopālānanda explains both changes: because the sense is of what has happened, avādiṣam stands in place of vadiṣyāmi and āvīt in place of avatu (भूतार्थत्वात् वदिष्यामि इति स्थाने अवादिषम् इति). The form is the aorist, which in Vedic prose typically reports what has just been done.

Traditional Interpretation

Why does Madhva single out Vāyu at the close?

Madhva’s only comment, after the end of the vallī, ties the opening and closing prayers to the earlier anuvākas: the abhimāna over the five topics of the saṃhitā (1.3) and the fivefold nature (1.7) belong also to Vāyu in the form of prāṇa, संहितायाः पञ्चाधिकरणाभिमानित्वं प्राणादिरूपस्य वायोरपि भवति, and that is why the beginning and end say ‘you indeed are the perceptible Brahman’ (अत उपक्रमोपसंहारयोस् त्वमेव प्रत्यक्षं ब्रह्म वदिष्यामि).

Contemporary Reflection

What is it like to end where one began?

The prayer that began the study now ends it, almost unchanged. The difference is small and large: the student is no longer promising but reporting. Many traditions of learning close with gratitude rather than with a summary; this one does it with a change of tense. This is a modern reflection.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Śīkṣāvallī 12

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Śaṅkara treats the anuvāka as the closing śānti, recited to remove obstacles to the knowledge already gained, and refers to his explanation of 1.1 (शं नो मित्र इत्यादि). There, Vāyu is the perceptible Brahman as the all-pervading breath.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Kūranārāyaṇa; Rāmānanda; Gopālānanda (modern), commentaries on Śīkṣāvallī 12 (six-commentary edition)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Kūranārāyaṇa: the prayer has been answered — ‘by your favour I have spoken without hindrance’ — and what was studied has protected the student from the faults of wrong study. Rāmānanda and Gopālānanda read it as the śānti for the end of study, explained as before (व्याख्यातोऽयं मन्त्रः, Gopālānanda).

Dvaita · Madhva, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, end of the Śīkṣāvallī

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Madhva explains why the opening and closing prayers call Vāyu ‘the perceptible Brahman’: the presiding over the five topics of the saṃhitā belongs also to Vāyu in the form of prāṇa (संहितायाः पञ्चाधिकरणाभिमानित्वं प्राणादिरूपस्य वायोरपि भवति). In his opening verse (on 1.1) the Brahman of the Upaniṣad is Viṣṇu.

Where they disagree

The schools agree that this is the closing śānti and that its past tenses report the prayer of 1.1 as fulfilled. They carry over their differences from 1.1 on whom ‘brahman’ names.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

    vowel sandhi (yaṇ)

    A final u before a dissimilar vowel becomes v: P. 6.1.77 (इको यणचि).

  • ब्रह्मावादिषम् brahmāvādiṣam → ब्रह्म + अवादिषम्

    vowel sandhi (savarṇa-dīrgha)

  • तन्मामावीत् tanmāmāvīt → तत् + माम् + आवीत्

Morphology

अवादिषम्
Aorist of vad with the connecting i (iṣ-aorist), 1st sg.: a-vād-iṣ-am, with vṛddhi of the root vowel, P. 7.2.3 (वदव्रजहलन्तस्याचः). It replaces the future vadiṣyāmi of 1.1: ‘I have spoken’ for ‘I shall speak’. Gopālānanda: भूतार्थत्वात् वदिष्यामि इति स्थाने अवादिषम् इति.
आवीत्
Aorist 3rd sg. of av, ‘to protect’: the augment a fused with the root’s a (ā-v-īt), the ending -īt as in other iṭ-aorists, P. 7.3.96 (अस्तिसिचोऽपृक्ते). It replaces the imperative avatu of 1.1: ‘it has protected’ for ‘may it protect’. Madhva’s text reads avīt (corrections.json).
वायो
Vocative of vāyu, as in 1.1.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

The same three movements as 1.1 — five wishes, two salutations and a statement, then the petition — with two changes of tense. The future vadiṣyāmi (‘I shall speak’) becomes the aorist avādiṣam (‘I have spoken’), and the imperative avatu (‘may it protect’) becomes the aorist āvīt (‘it has protected’). The opening wishes keep their optative-imperative sense: the prayer for well-being continues.

Ambiguities

Why repeat the prayer at all?
Śaṅkara: it is recited to calm the obstacles to the knowledge already gained (अतीतविद्याप्राप्त्युपसर्गशमनार्थां शान्तिं पठति), and he does not explain it again (व्याख्यातमेतत्पूर्वम्). Kūranārāyaṇa: the śānti-anuvāka stands at the beginning and the end of the Sāṃhitī upaniṣads (तासामादावन्ते च शान्त्यनुवाकः उक्तः). Gopālānanda calls it the closing śānti (इयमुत्तरशान्तिः).
What has ‘protected’ the speaker?
Kūranārāyaṇa: what was studied has protected him from the faults of wrong study (तदधीतम् आवीन्मां मिथ्याधीतत्वादि दोषप्रसङ्गेन), and he has spoken without hindrance by the deity’s favour (एवं त्वत्प्रसादेन अविघ्न अवादिषम्). Grammatically tat refers back, as in 1.1, to brahma.
Is there a final ‘om śāntiḥ’?
The base text of Śaṅkara does not print it here; the Sāyaṇa-pāṭha and Madhva’s text close the vallī with ‘om śāntiḥ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ’ (corrections.json).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Corporate life

Projects usually close with a report on deliverables. This passage closes a course of learning by restating its opening wish and confirming that it was met: the promises were kept, the protection was received. A short retrospective that asks simply ‘did we do what we said, and were we looked after?’ — and says thank you — is an underrated way to end a piece of work together. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Think of something you began with a hope. Did the hope come true, and did you notice?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Why might a tradition end a course of study by repeating its first prayer, instead of summarising what was learnt?

A contemplation exercise At the end of something you finished this week, write the sentence you would have written at its start, and change only the tense.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
