Taittirīya Upaniṣad · Śīkṣāvallī · Anuvāka 1 of 12

A prayer before learning: may the powers be kind, may truth be spoken

The Upaniṣad opens not with a doctrine but with a request: that nothing hinder the teaching, and that teacher and student both be protected.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Om. May Mitra be kind to us, and Varuṇa; may Aryaman be kind to us; may Indra and Bṛhaspati be kind to us, and Viṣṇu of the wide strides. Homage to Brahman. 7 Explain it to a beginner

Before a lesson begins, the student and the teacher ask for help. This passage is that request. First it asks a series of powers — named after the forces of nature and of the body — to be kind, so that nothing gets in the way of learning. Then it bows to the wind, the breath, calling it the one great reality you can actually feel. Then the student makes three promises: to name the truth, to speak what is right, and to speak what is true. Last comes the heart of it: may this protect me, and may it protect my teacher — said twice, because it matters. Learning, the passage suggests, is something two people do together, and both of them need care. It ends with the word ‘peace’ said three times.

An analogy Like a climbing pair checking each other’s ropes before the climb: the wish is not only ‘keep me safe’ but ‘keep us both safe’, because neither gets up the mountain without the other.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the passage actually say?

It is a prayer in three parts. It asks five deities — Mitra, Varuṇa, Aryaman, Indra with Bṛhaspati, and Viṣṇu — to be ‘śam’, well-being, for ‘us’. It salutes Brahman and Vāyu, the wind, and declares Vāyu to be ‘the perceptible Brahman’. The speaker then promises to call Vāyu so, and to speak ṛta and satya. It closes by asking ‘that’ to protect ‘me’ and ‘the speaker’, twice, and with a threefold ‘peace’. The text does not say why these deities are invoked, why Vāyu is singled out, or what the obstacles are; the commentators supply all of that.

Traditional Interpretation

Why does an Upaniṣad of knowledge begin with a prayer for protection?

Śaṅkara reads the whole anuvāka as removing obstacles to learning: the deities who preside over the student’s faculties are asked to be kind, because only then will hearing and retaining the knowledge proceed without hindrance — तासु हि सुखकृत्सु विद्याश्रवणधारणोपयोगाः अप्रतिबन्धेन भविष्यन्तीति. For him the deities are those of the person (adhyātma): Mitra of the out-breath and day, Varuṇa of the in-breath and night, Aryaman of the eye, Indra of strength, Bṛhaspati of speech and intellect, Viṣṇu of the feet (एवमाद्या अध्यात्मदेवताः शं नः). Govindarāja similarly calls it the student’s prayer for the removal of obstacles before studying (ब्रह्माध्येष्यमाणो ब्रह्मचारी तद्विघ्घ्रशान्तिं प्रार्थयते).

Traditional Interpretation

In what sense is the wind ‘the perceptible Brahman’?

Śaṅkara explains that compared with the eye and the other senses the wind is outside, near and unmediated: त्वमेव चक्षुराद्यपेक्ष्य बाह्यं सन्निकृष्टमव्यवहितं प्रत्यक्षं ब्रह्मासि. The breath on which every faculty depends is the most immediate form in which the student can meet what is greater than any faculty. Raṅgarāmānuja gives a different reason for addressing the wind: it is the breath that makes the syllables of the Veda audible (वेदाक्षराभिव्यञ्जकवायुं प्रार्थयते).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Why say both ‘ṛta’ and ‘satya’?

The two words overlap; the commentators make the pair do work. For Śaṅkara ṛta is the meaning rightly settled in the mind according to scripture, and satya the same meaning carried out by speech and body (ऋतं यथाशास्त्रं यथाकर्तव्यं बुद्धौ सुपरिनिश्चितमर्थं, स एव वाक्कायाभ्यां सम्पाद्यमानः). For Raṅgarāmānuja ṛta is the correctness of the words, free of corruption, and satya the truth of what they report: ऋतत्वम् अपभ्रंशराहित्यलक्षणं शब्दसत्यत्वम्, सत्यत्वञ्च यथावस्थितार्थकथनरूपमर्थसत्यत्वम्. Both readings fit a passage spoken at the start of study, where correct recitation and faithful understanding are both at stake.

Traditional Interpretation

Why is ‘peace’ said three times?

Śaṅkara: to quiet the three kinds of obstacle to obtaining knowledge — those arising from oneself, from other beings, and from the powers of nature: आध्यात्मिकाधिभौतिकाधिदैविकानां विद्याप्राप्त्युपसर्गाणां प्रशमनार्थम्. The repetition of ‘may it protect me, may it protect the speaker’ he explains as a sign of earnestness: पुनर्वचनमादरार्थम्.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Śīkṣāvallī 1

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Śaṅkara reads the anuvāka as a preparation for the knowledge of Brahman that the Upaniṣad will teach. The deities are the presiding powers of the student’s own faculties; ‘brahman’ in namo brahmaṇe is Vāyu, the all-pervading breath, तत् सर्वात्मकं वाय्वाख्यं ब्रह्म; the prayer asks for the conditions under which knowledge can arise. His long introduction argues that liberation is not produced by action but is the Self’s abiding in itself once ignorance is removed: तस्मादविद्याकामकर्मोपादानहेतुनिवृत्तौ स्वात्मन्यवस्थानं मोक्ष इति.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Raṅgarāmānuja; Kauśika Govindarāja, commentaries on Śīkṣāvallī 1 (six-commentary edition)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Raṅgarāmānuja reads ‘brahman’ in namo brahmaṇe as the Veda, and the wind as invoked because it voices the Veda’s syllables. Govindarāja reads namo brahmaṇe as homage to the supreme, ‘the root of all bliss’ (नमो ब्रह्मणे परस्मै सर्वानन्दकन्दाय). Kūranārāyaṇa’s introduction reads these Upaniṣads as having Nārāyaṇa for their purport (नारायणस्वरूपरूपगुणविभूत्यादिषु तात्पर्यातिशयात्); Govindarāja names the twelve anuvākas from ‘śaṃ no mitraḥ’ as the Sāṃhitī upaniṣad (शं नो मित्र इत्यारभ्य द्वादशानुवाकाः).

Dvaita · Madhva, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, opening

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Madhva’s commentary says nothing on the words of this anuvāka beyond its opening verse, which names the Brahman of the whole Upaniṣad as Viṣṇu: सत्यं ज्ञानमनन्तमानन्दं ब्रह्म सर्वशक्त्येकम् । सर्वैर्देवैरीड्यं विष्ण्वाख्यं. For the deities of the prayer no Dvaita reading is given here.

Where they disagree

The schools agree that the anuvāka is a prayer for unhindered study. They differ on what ‘brahman’ refers to in namo brahmaṇe (Vāyu for Śaṅkara, the Veda for Raṅgarāmānuja, the supreme for Govindarāja), and on how ṛta differs from satya.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • भवत्वर्यमा bhavatvaryamā → भवतु + अर्यमा

    vowel sandhi (yaṇ)

    A final u before a dissimilar vowel becomes v: P. 6.1.77 (इको यणचि). bhavatu + aryamā → bhavatv aryamā.

  • शं न इन्द्रो śaṃ na indro → शम् + नः + इन्द्रः

    visarga sandhi (aḥ before a vowel other than a; aḥ before a voiced consonant)

    naḥ before the vowel i loses its visarga and the hiatus stays (na indraḥ). indraḥ before the voiced b becomes indro: the visarga (ru) becomes u before a voiced consonant, P. 6.1.114 (हशि च), and a + u gives o.

  • विष्णुरुरुक्रमः viṣṇururukramaḥ → विष्णुः + उरुक्रमः

    visarga sandhi (r-visarga)

    A visarga after a vowel other than a or ā becomes r before a vowel or voiced sound: viṣṇuḥ + urukramaḥ → viṣṇur urukramaḥ.

  • नमस्ते namaste → नमः + ते

    visarga sandhi

    A visarga before t becomes s: P. 8.3.34 (विसर्जनीयस्य सः).

  • ब्रह्मासि brahmāsi → ब्रह्म + असि

    vowel sandhi (savarṇa-dīrgha)

    a + a → ā: P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः).

  • तन्मामवतु tanmāmavatu → तत् + माम् + अवतु

    consonant sandhi (t before a nasal); m before a vowel

    A final t before a nasal becomes the nasal n; mām before the vowel of avatu is written together without change.

  • तद्वक्तारमवतु tadvaktāramavatu → तत् + वक्तारम् + अवतु

    consonant sandhi (voicing)

    A final t before the voiced v becomes d: P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते).

Morphology

भवतु
Imperative of bhū. Stated once, it is understood with every ‘śaṃ naḥ’ (Śaṅkara: भवतु इति सर्वत्रानुषङ्गः): ‘may Mitra be well-being for us, may Varuṇa …’.
वायो
Vocative of vāyu, ‘wind’: a final u takes guṇa in the vocative singular (vāyo).
वदिष्यामि
The future of vad with the connecting vowel i (vad-i-ṣyā-mi), ‘I shall speak’. With tvām … brahma it takes two accusatives: ‘I shall call you Brahman’.
अवतु
Imperative of av, ‘to help, favour, protect’. The subject is tat, ‘that’ — the Brahman just addressed.
वक्तारम्
Accusative of the agent noun vaktṛ (vac + tṛ), ‘speaker’; here the teacher who will speak the teaching.
बृहस्पतिः
A name, ‘lord of prayer’; its first member bṛhas is not analysed further here.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

Three movements. First, five short wishes on one pattern, ‘śaṃ naḥ X’, with the verb bhavatu stated once and understood throughout (lines 1–4: the Ṛgveda verse 1.90.9). Second, two salutations with the verb understood (‘homage to Brahman’, ‘homage to you, Vāyu’), followed by a statement and three promises in the future tense (‘I shall call you … I shall speak the right … the true’). Third, a petition with the imperative avatu, repeated with the order of verb and object reversed, and the threefold śānti.

Ambiguities

Whom does ‘namo brahmaṇe’ salute?
Śaṅkara takes brahman here as Vāyu, named indirectly before he is addressed directly (परोक्षप्रत्यक्षाभ्यां वायुरेवाभिधीयते). Raṅgarāmānuja takes it as the Veda (नमो ब्रह्मणे वेदाय नम इत्यर्थः), whose syllables the breath carries; Govindarāja as the supreme Brahman, ‘without whom not a blade of grass moves’. The word allows all three.
ṛtam and satyam
Both can mean ‘truth’. Śaṅkara distinguishes what is rightly decided in the mind (ṛta) from the same carried out in speech and action (satya); Raṅgarāmānuja distinguishes correctness of the words (ṛta) from truth of what they say (satya). Neither distinction is stated in the text, which simply says both.
Who is ‘that’ (tat) which protects?
Grammatically the neuter tat points back to brahma: the Brahman just praised. For Śaṅkara that is Vāyu as the visible Brahman.

Metre

mixed · syllables counted as written: 9 + 7 + 8 + 8 + 5 + 5 + 9 + 12 + 6 + 6 + 5 + 7 + 4 + 13

Lines 1–4 are an anuṣṭubh verse (Ṛgveda 1.90.9, 8 + 8 + 8 + 8 syllables); the rest is prose (yajus) in short clauses.

Textual variations between witnesses

  • Variant reading Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 7–9, Sāyaṇa-pāṭha (svara): त्वमेव प्रत्यक्षं ब्रह्म वदिष्यामि — S reads tvam eva … vadiṣyāmi (‘you alone I shall call …’) where the base and Madhva’s text read tvām eva, the accusative the verb requires; S’s tvam may be a keying slip, since its own closing anuvāka (1.12) reads त्वामे॒व.
  • Variant reading Madhva’s mūla: शिक्षां — Madhva’s text reads śikṣām, the ordinary spelling. Śaṅkara explains the base’s long ī as Vedic: शिक्षैव शीक्षा । दैर्घ्यं छान्दसम्.
11 Connections with other scriptures

  • Ṛgveda 1.90.9 Direct textual parallel

    शं नो मित्रः शं वरुणः शं नो भवत्वर्यमा

    śaṃ no mitraḥ śaṃ varuṇaḥ śaṃ no bhavatvaryamā

    The first four lines are Ṛgveda 1.90.9, word for word: the Upaniṣad opens by reciting a verse of the Ṛgveda.

  • Bhagavad Gītā 4.34 Modern thematic association

    तद्विद्धि प्रणिपातेन परिप्रश्नेन सेवया

    tadviddhi praṇipātena paripraśnena sevayā

    ‘Learn it by bowing down, by questioning, by service’: the Gītā’s account of how knowledge is received from a teacher. A thematic association with the anuvāka’s homage and its care for both teacher and student.

  • Bhagavad Gītā 17.15 Modern thematic association

    सत्यं प्रियहितं च यत्

    satyaṃ priyahitaṃ ca yat

    The Gītā’s austerity of speech — words that are true, kind and beneficial, and the practice of recitation. A thematic association with the promise ‘I shall speak the true’.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Corporate life

Many workplaces begin a mentoring relationship with a contract about deliverables. This passage begins a teaching relationship differently: with a wish that both parties be protected, and a promise from the learner to speak what is right and true. The order is instructive. Before anything is taught, the conditions for teaching — goodwill, honesty, attention to the teacher’s welfare as well as one’s own — are named and asked for. Teams that take this seriously tend to find that knowledge moves more freely when people are not afraid. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Before you learn something hard, what do you need — and what does the person teaching you need?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Why might a text about the ultimate reality begin by asking that nothing go wrong, rather than by stating its doctrine?

A contemplation exercise Before your next conversation where you will learn from someone, take a breath and silently wish them well. Notice afterwards whether you listened differently.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
