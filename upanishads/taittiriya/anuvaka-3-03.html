Taittirīya Upaniṣad · Bhṛguvallī · Anuvāka 3 of 10

The second answer: breath is Brahman

The formula repeats with breath in place of food: beings are born of breath, live by it and go into it. Bhṛgu again returns, and is again told only to practise austerity.

5 Faithful English translation

[Having practised austerity,] he understood: ‘Breath is Brahman.’ For from breath, indeed, surely, these beings are born; by breath, born, they live; into breath they go forth [and] enter. Having understood that, he approached [his] father Varuṇa once more: ‘Teach [me] Brahman, venerable one.’ He said to him: ‘By austerity seek to know Brahman. 6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Having practised austerity, he understood that breath is Brahman: for it is from breath that these beings are born, by breath that they live once born, and into breath that they pass and merge at the end. Having understood this, he went to his father Varuṇa again and said, ‘Sir, teach me Brahman.’ Varuṇa told him: ‘Seek to know Brahman by austerity. Austerity is Brahman.’ He practised austerity, and when he had practised it —

7 Explain it to a beginner

The story repeats itself with one word changed. After more effort, Bhṛgu decides that breath is Brahman. It also fits the test: living things begin to breathe when they are born, they live as long as they breathe, and at death their breath goes out. But Bhṛgu still goes back to his father, and Varuṇa gives exactly the same reply: seek Brahman by austerity. The repetition is the point. Each answer goes a little deeper than the one before — from the body to the breath that keeps it alive — and each time the student is sent back to look again rather than being told what to think.

An analogy Like peeling an onion, each layer Bhṛgu finds is real and has a shape, but there is another inside it.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the passage say?

Bhṛgu concludes that breath is Brahman, because beings are born of breath, live by it and go into it. He returns to his father, asks again, and is again told to seek Brahman by austerity. He practises austerity.

Traditional Interpretation

Why is the reply always the same?

Kūranārāyaṇa: there was no other means to give, so austerity is taught again and again (तद्व्यतिरेकोपायाभावेन च पुनः पुनः तपसः एव च उपदेशः). Rāmānanda: the repetition shows that austerity is the highest of the means to knowledge (तपसः पौनःपुन्येन उपदेशः).

Traditional Interpretation

Is this the self of breath of the second vallī?

Rāmānanda says so outright (प्राणपदेन अत्र प्राणमयकोशः विवक्षितः), and Śaṅkara, summing up the series at 3.6, describes it as moving step by step inward to the innermost bliss (शनैः शनैः अन्तरनुप्रविश्य अन्तरतममानन्दं ब्रह्म विज्ञातवान्).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What do the forms tell us?

Only the four case forms of prāṇa change from 3.2. The base file keys adhīhi with a long ī in this anuvāka, corrected to adhīhi (corrections.json). Madhva’s text has the accusative prāṇaṃ in the first line.

Comparative Vedānta

Śaṅkara adds no comment here: his note on 3.2 covers the pattern and the rest is plain (ऋज्वन्यत्). Breath, like food, has part of the mark of Brahman but not all of it; the series leads inward until bliss is reached.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Kūranārāyaṇa; Rāmānanda, commentaries on Bhṛguvallī 3 (six-commentary edition)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Breath, the self made of breath, is again a real part of Brahman’s body, and Bhṛgu is right to find the mark in it; he is not content because breath too is produced and perishes (प्राणस्य उत्पत्तिविनाशवत्त्वदर्शनात्).

Dvaita · Madhva, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, one comment on Bhṛguvallī 2–7

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Breath is the next of the forms of Viṣṇu that Bhṛgu came to know in order (क्रमाद् भृगुर्व्यजानात् तमन्नादिबहुरूपिणम्).

Where they disagree

As in 3.2: a partial answer left behind (Śaṅkara; Viśiṣṭādvaita), or a true knowing of Viṣṇu under another name (Madhva).

  • प्राणो ब्रह्मेति prāṇo brahmeti → प्राणः + ब्रह्म + इति

    vowel sandhi (guṇa)

    As bhagavo brahmeti: P. 6.1.114 (हशि च) and P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः).

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this anuvāka.

Morphology

व्यजानात्
Imperfect of vi-√jñā (ninth class, stem jānā-), with the augment a after the preverb: vi + a-jānāt → vyajānāt. It supplies the main verb for the absolutive taptvā that ended the previous anuvāka.
प्राणः
Nominative inside the quoted equation (prāṇo brahmeti). Madhva’s text reads the accusative prāṇaṃ brahmeti, ‘he knew breath as Brahman’ (corrections.json); the sense is the same.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

The same frame as 3.2, word for word, with prāṇa (nominative, ablative, instrumental, accusative) in place of anna. The open ending sa tapas taptvā again runs on into the next anuvāka.

Ambiguities

What ‘breath’ is
Rāmānanda takes it as the self made of breath of the second vallī (प्राणपदेन अत्र प्राणमयकोशः विवक्षितः). Kūranārāyaṇa supports ‘beings go into breath’ with the Chāndogya’s account of dying, in which speech goes into mind and mind into breath (वाङ्मनसि सम्पद्यते मनः प्राणे).
Why go back again?
The text gives no reason. Rāmānanda: breath too is seen to arise and perish, so Bhṛgu was not content (प्राणस्य उत्पत्तिविनाशवत्त्वदर्शनात्).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Aging and mortality

Breath is the simplest measure of being alive, and it is the first thing a body loses at death. The passage treats it as a serious candidate for what we depend on, and then as not enough. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin How often do you notice your own breathing?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry If breath sustains life, what sustains breath?

A contemplation exercise Sit and follow ten breaths without changing them, noticing only that each comes and goes.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
