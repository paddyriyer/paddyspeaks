Taittirīya Upaniṣad · Brahmānandavallī · Anuvāka 6 of 9

He wished to be many, and entered what he made

The verse on knowing that Brahman is, the pupil’s questions about who reaches it, and the answer begun in a creation account: the self desired, brooded, created all this and entered it, becoming both sides of every pair.

5 Faithful English translation

‘Non-existent indeed he becomes, if he knows Brahman [as] non-existent. If he knows “Brahman is”, therefore they know him [as] existent.’ Of that [previous one] this very [self] is the embodied self, [the self] which [is the self] of the previous [one]. Now, hence, the follow-up questions. Does anyone who does not know, having departed, go to that world? Or does anyone who knows, having departed, attain that world? He desired: ‘May I be many, may I be born forth.’ He practised tapas. Having practised tapas he created all this, whatever there is. Having created it, he entered into that very [thing]. Having entered it, he became the sat and the tyat, the defined and the undefined, what has a resting-place and what has none, the conscious and the unconscious, the true and the untrue; [as] the true he became whatever there is. That they call ‘the true’. On that also there is this verse.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

‘One who thinks Brahman does not exist becomes as if he did not exist himself. One who knows that Brahman is — him they know as truly being.’ This is the embodied self of the one before. Now the questions that follow: Does one who does not know go to that world after death? Or does one who knows reach it? He wished: ‘Let me be many; let me be born.’ He brooded in heat, and from that brooding he brought forth all this, everything there is. Having brought it forth, he entered into it. Having entered it, he became both the formed and the formless, the defined and the undefined, what rests on something and what rests on nothing, the conscious and the unconscious, the true and the false. As the true he became all that is; and so they call it ‘the true’. There is a verse about this too.

7 Explain it to a beginner

This passage moves from the five selves to a story. It starts with a short verse: if you think Brahman does not exist, you yourself become as good as non-existent; if you know that Brahman is, people recognise you as truly real. Then a student asks the obvious follow-up questions: after death, does someone who does not know this reach that world? Does someone who knows it reach it? The teacher answers by telling how the world began. The one reality wished, ‘Let me be many.’ It brooded, and brought forth everything. Then it entered into what it had made, and became every pair of opposites — the formed and the formless, the defined and the undefined, the conscious and the unconscious, the true and the false. And so, the passage ends, it is called ‘the true’.

An analogy Like a composer who writes a symphony and then plays in it: the music is something she made, and yet in every bar it is she who is sounding.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the passage say?

A verse: one who knows Brahman as non-existent becomes non-existent; one who knows that Brahman is is known as existent. After the formula of the embodied self come follow-up questions: does one who does not know reach ‘that world’ after death, or does one who knows? The answer begins with creation: he desired to be many, practised tapas, created all this, entered it, and became the formed and the formless and every other pair — as the real, he became all, and so it is called ‘the real’.

Traditional Interpretation

Why does the verse speak of Brahman’s non-existence?

Because Brahman is beyond everyday dealings, and people take what is not an object of them to be non-existent: व्यवहारातीतत्वं ब्रह्मण इति ब्रूमः. The verse warns against that conclusion. Śaṅkara gives a second reading: one who denies Brahman denies the whole way of life that leads to it, and so is called asat, ‘not good’; one who affirms it walks that path and is called sat. His conclusion: तस्मादस्तीत्येव ब्रह्म प्रतिपत्तव्यमिति वाक्यार्थः.

Traditional Interpretation

How many questions are there, and why?

The text says ‘questions’, plural. Śaṅkara counts three: whether Brahman exists, whether the ignorant reach it, and whether the knower reaches it, the first implied by the verse — एतेषां प्रतिवचनार्थ उत्तरो ग्रन्थ आरभ्यते. Kūranārāyaṇa counts two spoken questions about the knower and two implied, एवं वाचनिकं प्रश्नद्वयं हार्दद्वयमिति अनुप्रश्नाः इति बहुवचनोपपत्तिः, and calls the question one that holds alternatives: विकल्पगर्भः प्रश्नः.

Traditional Interpretation

What is the creator’s ‘desire’ and ‘tapas’?

Not the wanting of one who lacks something. Śaṅkara: Brahman’s desires are its own nature, real and knowing, and pure: सत्यज्ञानलक्षणाः स्वात्मभूतत्वाद्विशुद्धाः; tapas here is knowledge, reflection on the world to be made: तप इति ज्ञानमुच्यते. Becoming many is the unfolding of name and form: तदेतन्नामरूपव्याकरणं ब्रह्मणो बहुभवनम्. Rāmānanda agrees on tapas: स्रष्टव्यस्य सृष्टिरीतिपर्यालोचनमेव भगवतः तपः.

Traditional Interpretation

How does what has become ‘many’ remain one?

Śaṅkara: names and forms exist only through Brahman, never Brahman through them, so the many is Brahman’s unfolding and not a second thing. Gopālānanda (modern) presses the other way: the becoming many is real, not appearance — एतेन बहुभवनं न बौद्धम् आभासमात्र — and yet Brahman is not changed, because the coming into being is only the division of name and form in what is its body: नामरूपविभागापत्तिमात्रं हि प्रजननम्.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Brahmānandavallī 6

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

The anuvāka proves that Brahman exists: what is the cause of space and the rest, what desires and reflects, and what is found within the intellect as ‘seer, hearer, thinker, knower’ must be. Its ‘entry’ is just that being found: स एव तस्य प्रवेशः. The pairs it became are name and form, real only relatively; the real (satya) in the full sense is one: एकमेव हि परमार्थसत्यं ब्रह्म.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Kūranārāyaṇa; Rāmānanda; Gopālānanda (modern), commentaries on Brahmānandavallī 6 (six-commentary edition)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

The questions are about the knower’s attainment (utā vidvān). The creation shows the supreme self as both material and efficient cause, with conscious and non-conscious beings as its body: सत् इत्यनेन चेतनवर्गः उच्यते (Rāmānanda). Becoming many is real, and Brahman remains unchanged in its own nature, as Gopālānanda argues. Kūranārāyaṇa reads tapas as the Lord’s considered resolve to create: प्राचीनजगत्संस्थानपर्यालोचनपूर्वकसङ्कल्पः अत्र तपः.

Dvaita · Madhva, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, on the sixth anuvāka

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Those who know Brahman as no other than the individual are ‘non-existent’; those who know it as other than the individual are the ‘existent’, fit for release: जीवादन्यत् परं ब्रह्म पञ्चरूपं च ये विदुः. Madhva frames the questions as asked of Brahmā (Caturmukha) and answers: अज्ञा न प्राप्नुयुर्ब्रह्म प्राप्नुयुर्ज्ञानिनोऽखिलाः. Viṣṇu’s only tapas is his thought ‘let it be’: स्यादित्यालोचनान्नान्यत् तपो विष्णोः कदाचन; he entered the world by another form.

Where they disagree

All three read the creation account as an answer to the questions, but they hear different questions. Śaṅkara’s are about Brahman’s existence and about knower and ignorant, and the answer shows Brahman as the one real behind name and form. The Viśiṣṭādvaita commentators’ questions are about what the knower attains, and the answer shows a Brahman with real qualities and a real world for its body. Madhva makes the knowledge that saves the knowledge that Brahman is other than the individual.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

A four-line verse is followed by the usual identifying sentence; then a heading (athāto ’nupraśnāḥ) and two questions, each a participial clause (avidvān … pretya, vidvān … pretya) with a finite verb whose final vowel is prolated (gacchatī3, samaśnutā3). The answer begins as narrative: a chain of imperfects with the same subject saḥ (akāmayata, atapyata, asṛjata, anuprāviśat, abhavat), linked by absolutives (taptvā, sṛṣṭvā, anupraviśya), with the wish quoted by iti. The predicates of abhavat are six pairs joined by ca … ca, and a final clause names the result (tat satyam ity ācakṣate).

Ambiguities

uta avidvān or utā vidvān?
The letters उताविद्वान् divide two ways. Śaṅkara reads uta avidvān, ‘does one who does not know…?’, and finds three questions: does Brahman exist, does the ignorant reach it, does the knower reach it. Kūranārāyaṇa, Rāmānanda and Gopālānanda read utā vidvān, with a Vedic lengthening of uta — उता इति निपातस्य छान्दसो दीर्घः (Kūranārāyaṇa) — so both questions concern the knower: does he go to that world after death, and does he attain it there? Madhva’s verses follow Śaṅkara’s division (अविद्वानपि यः कश्चिद् ब्रह्माप्नोति कथञ्चन). The padaccheda follows Śaṅkara.
How is the creation an answer?
Śaṅkara: the narrative answers first the implied question whether Brahman exists — what desires, creates and is found entered into the intellect as seer and hearer must exist: स एव तस्य प्रवेशः ; तस्मादस्ति तत्कारणं ब्रह्म. Gopālānanda: it shows Brahman’s qualities, and so what the knower attains: जगत्स्रष्टृत्वोपपादनमुखेन तु कल्याणगुणयोगो निरूपणीयः.
What is ‘entering’?
Śaṅkara rejects a literal entry into what is already pervaded by the cause, and reads it as Brahman’s being found in the cave of the intellect as the knower. Kūranārāyaṇa and Gopālānanda take it as a real mode of the all-pervading Brahman’s presence in each thing; Gopālānanda cites the Upaniṣatprakāśikā for it (पुष्कलाभिव्यक्तयर्हः स्थितिविशेषः अयम्). Madhva: रूपान्तरेणाविशच्च जगत्सर्वं जनार्दनः — he entered by another form.
sat and tyat
Śaṅkara: the formed and the unformed (mūrta and amūrta) — सच्च मूर्तं त्यच्च अमूर्तम् अभवत्. Rāmānanda and Gopālānanda: the conscious and the non-conscious, सत् इत्यनेन चेतनवर्गः उच्यते, because the self does not change while matter does.
Two senses of satya
The pair ‘satyaṃ cānṛtaṃ ca’ uses satya of what is relatively true (water against a mirage, Śaṅkara says); the following ‘satyam abhavat’ and ‘tat satyam ity ācakṣate’ use it of the real itself, which Śaṅkara identifies with the Brahman of ‘satyaṃ jñānam anantam’ (2.1): एकमेव हि परमार्थसत्यं ब्रह्म.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Ego and self-image

The verse says that what we hold to be real shapes what we become: deny that anything deeper exists and you become, in its words, as good as non-existent. We often build a self-image from what can be measured and shown. The passage suggests the self that people recognise as truly ‘being’ is the one oriented to something it cannot measure. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin If you made something and then lived inside it, would you be the maker, the thing made, or both?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Śaṅkara says the follow-up questions begin with ‘does Brahman exist?’; the Viśiṣṭādvaita commentators say they begin with ‘what does the knower reach?’. Which question would you ask first, and why?

A contemplation exercise Look at one ordinary object today and name in it a ‘pair’ the passage lists: what in it is defined and what is not, what rests on something and what does not.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.

Corrections to the base witness

  • सन्तमेन न्ततो → सन्तमेनं ततो (orthographic) — The base file breaks the word space inside the anusvāra’s class nasal (सन्तमेन न्ततो for santam enaṃ tato). The letters are unchanged; S and Madhva’s text read सन्तमेनं ततो.

Further notes

  • Śaṅkara’s comment on this anuvāka includes a long argument on how Brahman could ‘enter’ what it made (rejecting the clay-and-pot and reflection models); it is summarised, not quoted in full.
  • Gopālānanda cites the Upaniṣatprakāśikā on ‘entering’; it is reported as cited by him and was not checked. Kūranārāyaṇa cites many other works (Manu, the Subāla, the Gītā and others), likewise reported as cited.
  • The base text’s ‘सन्तमेनन्ततो’ (spacing) is corrected to ‘सन्तमेनं ततो’ in corrections.json; the letters are unchanged.

Uncertainties recorded

  • interpretation The division उत अविद्वान् (Śaṅkara, Madhva) or उता विद्वान् (Kūranārāyaṇa, Rāmānanda, Gopālānanda) changes the first question from the ignorant to the knower. The padaccheda and translation follow Śaṅkara.
  • uncertain The prolated forms गच्छती३ and समश्नुता३ are given as written; the analysis of samaśnutā3 u from samaśnute u is Śaṅkara’s.
  • interpretation Madhva sets the questions as asked of Brahmā by Varuṇa (इत्युक्ते ब्रह्मणा पूर्वं पप्रच्छवरुणो विभुम्); this framing is his and is not in the text.
