Taittirīya Upaniṣad · Brahmānandavallī · Anuvāka 1 of 9

Brahman is truth, knowledge, the infinite — and the first sheath, made of food

The second vallī opens with its whole teaching in one sentence, explains it with a verse, and then begins the descent from the Self through the elements to the human body, the first of five ‘selves’.

5 Faithful English translation

The knower of Brahman attains the highest. On that, this [verse] has been declared: ‘Brahman [is] truth, knowledge, infinite. Who knows [it] placed in the cave, in the highest space, he attains all desires together, with Brahman, the wise.’ From that, indeed, from this Self, space arose; from space, air; from air, fire; from fire, water; from water, earth; from earth, plants; from plants, food; from food, the person. That, indeed, this person [is] made of the essence of food. Of him this indeed [is] the head; this the right wing; this the left wing; this the trunk; this the tail, the support. On that too there is this verse.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Whoever knows Brahman reaches the highest. A verse says: Brahman is the real, is knowing, is without limit. One who knows it as hidden in the cave of the heart, in the highest space, has all that is desired, all at once, as or with Brahman the all-knowing. From this Self came space; from space, air; from air, fire; from fire, water; from water, earth; from earth, plants; from plants, food; from food, the human being. This human being is made of the essence of food. Its head is this head, its right side this right arm, its left side this left arm, its body this trunk, its tail and support this lower part. There is a verse on this too.

7 Explain it to a beginner

This passage begins with a promise and a puzzle. The promise: whoever really knows Brahman, the deepest reality, reaches what is highest. The puzzle: what is Brahman? A short verse answers in three words — it is what is real, it is knowing itself, and it has no limits. It is found, the verse says, hidden in the cave of the heart. Then the text tells a story of how things come to be: from the Self comes space, then air, fire, water, earth, plants, food, and finally the human body, made of what we eat. And it describes that body like a bird: head, two wings, a trunk and a tail. This is the first of five layers the text will peel away, one by one, to show the Self within.

An analogy A guide who wants to show you a faint star first points to the branch of a tree it lies beside; Śaṅkara uses exactly this image (शाखाचन्द्रनिदर्शनवदन्तः प्रवेशयन्) for beginning with the body.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the passage say?

A sentence states the fruit of knowing Brahman (the highest). A verse gives three marks of Brahman (satya, jñāna, ananta) and says it is to be known in the cave, in the highest space, and that the knower attains all desires together with Brahman the wise. Prose then traces the arising of the elements from ‘this Self’, down to the human being, and describes the body as a bird with head, wings, trunk and tail. The anuvāka ends by announcing another verse.

Traditional Interpretation

Why does the vallī begin with one sentence?

For Śaṅkara the first sentence is the sūtra of the whole vallī: ब्रह्मविदाप्नोति परमिति वाक्यं सूत्रभूतं सर्वस्य वल्ल्यर्थस्य. The verse explains it briefly; the rest of the vallī explains it at length. Knowing and attaining are not two events: the highest is Brahman itself, and one does not reach one thing by knowing another (न ह्यन्यस्य विज्ञानादन्यस्य प्राप्तिः). Brahman is not reached as one reaches a place; it seems unreached only through ignorance — एवमविद्यया आत्मभूतमपि ब्रह्म अनाप्तं स्यात् — like the tenth man who counts the others and forgets himself.

Traditional Interpretation

How do the three words define Brahman?

Śaṅkara takes each word separately with brahma. satya is what does not change from the form in which it is known (सत्यमिति यद्रूपेण यन्निश्चितं तद्रूपं न व्यभिचरति, तत्सत्यम्); that excludes every effect, which changes. But a cause could be inert like clay, so jñāna is added, meaning knowing itself and not a knower. And knowing in the world is limited, so ananta is added: unlimited by place, time and thing (त्रिविधं ह्यानन्त्यम्: देशतः कालतो वस्तुतश्चेति). The words do not name Brahman directly; they point to it by excluding what it is not.

Traditional Interpretation

How do the Viśiṣṭādvaita commentators read the same words?

Kūranārāyaṇa reads the three words as a definition that sets Brahman apart from all else: satya is unconditioned being (तत्र सत्यपदं निरुपाधिकसत्तायोगि ब्रह्म आह), excluding matter and bound souls; jñāna is knowledge never contracted (ज्ञानपदं नित्यासङ्कुचितज्ञानैकाकारम् आह), excluding the freed, whose knowledge was once contracted; ananta is free of the limits of place, time and thing (अनन्तपदं देशकालवस्तुपरिच्छेदरहितम् आह), and infinite in qualities too. Gopālānanda (a modern commentator) follows the same lines. Rāmānanda’s Ānandabhāṣya (printed in the six-commentary edition as notes marked ‘आ.भा.’) explains why the vallī is called both Brahmavallī and Ānandavallī (इयं ब्रह्मवल्ली इत्युच्यते) and takes ‘knowing’ as meditation (वेदनञ्च उपासना पर्यायं).

Traditional Interpretation

Why does the creation sequence end in a bird-shaped body?

Śaṅkara: the human being is singled out because only a human is fit for ritual and knowledge (कर्मज्ञानाधिकारः). The aim is to lead that person inward to the innermost Brahman, and the mind attached to the body cannot be turned there at once; so the text starts from the familiar body and its shape (शाखाचन्द्रनिदर्शनवदन्तः प्रवेशयन्). The bird-shape of the body is the mould in which the later selves are imagined, like molten copper poured into a mould (मूषानिषिक्तद्रुतताम्रप्रतिमावत्).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What do the forms tell us?

vyoman is a Vedic locative without ending (Śaṅkara: व्योमन् व्योम्नि). veda is a perfect with present meaning. sambhūtaḥ is stated once and understood after each later ablative. annarasamaya has the suffix maya of ‘made of’; Śaṅkara glosses अन्नरसविकारः. The tail is a ‘support’ because one stands on it (प्रतितिष्ठत्यनयेति प्रतिष्ठा).

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Brahmavallī 1

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Brahman is the knower’s own Self: the text says ‘from this Self’ of Brahman, so the Self of the knower is Brahman. The three words are marks, not attributes; Brahman cannot be named by them, only pointed to. To know Brahman is to be it, and the knower ‘enjoys all desires at once’ as the all-knowing Brahman (ब्रह्मभूतो विद्वान् ब्रह्मस्वरूपेणैव सर्वान्कामान् सह अश्नुते), not one after another as an embodied person does. The selves made of food and the rest are imagined through ignorance and will be set aside one by one.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Kūranārāyaṇa; Gopālānanda (modern), commentaries on Ānandavallī 1 (six-commentary edition)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Brahman is the supreme person, distinct from the soul, the cause of the world, possessed of infinite auspicious qualities. ‘The knower of the supreme attains the supreme’ (परब्रह्मवित् परब्रह्म प्राप्नोति इत्यर्थः), and the released soul experiences Brahman with all its qualities (सर्वगुणान्वितं ब्रह्मानुभवति इत्यर्थः): the ‘desires’ are those qualities. Brahman is not mere knowledge; jñāna and vipaścit together show a knowing knower. Gopālānanda reads saha as ‘with’, as rice is eaten with curd.

Dvaita · Madhva, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, on Ānandavallī 1–2

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Madhva’s comment on the verse is one couplet: the knower of Brahman enjoys what is fit for him, together with Viriñca (Brahmā) and the supreme, being under the supreme’s control — स्वयोग्यं ब्रह्मविद् भुङ्क्ते विरिञ्चेन परेण च. There is no identity: the freed soul’s enjoyment is graded by its capacity. In his comment on the next anuvāka the person made of food is Viṣṇu, manifest in the body for the discerning (अन्नात्मनि शरीरेऽसौ व्यज्यते सुविवेकिभिः), and the tail is Aniruddha (पुच्छमित्येष भगवाननिरुद्धः स्वयं हरिः).

Where they disagree

All three take the verse as the key to the vallī and read the elements as arising from Brahman. They differ on the three words (marks excluding opposites, for Śaṅkara; a definition stating Brahman’s nature and qualities, for Kūranārāyaṇa); on the knower’s ‘enjoyment’ (as Brahman, at once; with Brahman, of its qualities; according to capacity, under the Lord’s control); and on the ‘person made of food’ (an imagined self to be set aside, for Śaṅkara; Viṣṇu present in the body, for Madhva).

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • ब्रह्मविदाप्नोति brahmavidāpnoti → ब्रह्मवित् + आप्नोति

    consonant sandhi

    A final t becomes d before a voiced sound, here the vowel ā: P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते).

  • तदेषाभ्युक्ता tadeṣābhyuktā → तत् + एषा + अभ्युक्ता

    consonant and vowel sandhi

    tat → tad before the vowel (P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते)); eṣā + abhyuktā: ā + a → ā, P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः).

  • सत्यं ज्ञानमनन्तं ब्रह्म satyaṃ jñānamanantaṃ brahma → सत्यम् + ज्ञानम् + अनन्तम् + ब्रह्म

    final m

    A final m becomes anusvāra before a consonant (P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः)) but stays m before a vowel: jñānam anantam.

  • सोऽश्नुते so'śnute → सः + अश्नुते

    visarga before a

    saḥ before a: the visarga (from s, P. 8.2.66 (ससजुषो रुँः)) becomes u (P. 6.1.113 (अतो रोरप्लुतादप्लुते)), a + u → o (P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः)), and the following a is absorbed, written with avagraha (P. 6.1.109 (एङः पदान्तादति)).

  • विपश्चितेति vipaściteti → विपश्चिता + इति

    vowel sandhi (guṇa)

    ā + i → e: P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः).

  • तस्माद्वा एतस्मादात्मन आकाशः tasmādvā etasmādātmana ākāśaḥ → तस्मात् + वै + एतस्मात् + आत्मनः + आकाशः

    several junctions

    tasmāt → tasmād before v and etasmāt → etasmād before ā (P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते)); vai before e becomes vāy and the y is dropped, leaving vā e (P. 6.1.78 (एचोऽयवायावः), P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य)); ātmanaḥ before ā loses its visarga and the hiatus stays (P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि), P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य)).

  • वायोरग्निः vāyoragniḥ → वायोः + अग्निः

    visarga as r

    After a vowel other than a or ā, the visarga (from s) is pronounced r before a voiced sound: vāyor agniḥ, agner āpaḥ.

  • पृथिव्या ओषधयः pṛthivyā oṣadhayaḥ → पृथिव्याः + ओषधयः

    āḥ before a vowel

    The visarga after ā is lost before a vowel and the hiatus stays: P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि), P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य).

  • स वा एष पुरुषोऽन्नरसमयः sa vā eṣa puruṣo'nnarasamayaḥ → सः + वै + एषः + पुरुषः + अन्नरसमयः

    saḥ and eṣaḥ before consonants

    saḥ and eṣaḥ drop their ending before a consonant: P. 6.1.132 (एतत्तदोः सुलोपोऽकोरनञ्समासे हलि). vai + e as above. puruṣaḥ + anna- as in so ’śnute.

  • तस्येदमेव tasyedameva → तस्य + इदम् + एव

    vowel sandhi (guṇa)

    a + i → e: P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः); idam keeps its m before the vowel of eva.

  • तदप्येष श्लोको भवति tadapyeṣa śloko bhavati → तत् + अपि + एषः + श्लोकः + भवति

    several junctions

    tad api (P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते)); api + e → apy e: P. 6.1.77 (इको यणचि); eṣaḥ → eṣa before a consonant (P. 6.1.132 (एतत्तदोः सुलोपोऽकोरनञ्समासे हलि)); ślokaḥ before the voiced bh becomes śloko: P. 6.1.114 (हशि च), then a + u → o.

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • ब्रह्मवित् brahmavit

    upapada-tatpuruṣa · ब्रह्मन् + विद्

    Vigraha ब्रह्म वेत्तीति ब्रह्मवित्

    one who knows Brahman. Śaṅkara analyses it तद्वेत्ति विजानातीति ब्रह्मवित्.

  • अनन्तम् anantam

    bahuvrīhi (nañ) · अ (न) + अन्त

    Vigraha नास्ति अन्तो यस्य तत् अनन्तम्

    that which has no end. Śaṅkara and Kūranārāyaṇa both take the end as one of place, time or thing.

  • अन्नरसमयः annarasamayaḥ

    ṣaṣṭhī-tatpuruṣa with the taddhita suffix maya · अन्न + रस + मय (taddhita)

    Vigraha अन्नस्य रसः अन्नरसः, तद्विकारः अन्नरसमयः

    made of, a transformation of, the essence of food. Śaṅkara: अन्नरसविकारः.

Morphology

वेद
A perfect form used with present meaning, as vid allows: P. 3.4.83 (विदो लटो वा). ‘knows’, not ‘knew’.
व्योमन्
Locative singular of vyoman without its ending, a Vedic form (P. 7.1.39 (सुपां सुलुक्पूर्वसवर्णाऽऽच्छेयाडाड्यायाजालः) covers such losses of case endings). Śaṅkara glosses व्योमन् व्योम्नि.
अश्नुते
From aś (fifth class, ātmanepada), ‘to reach, obtain’; Śaṅkara takes it as ‘enjoys’ (भुङ्क्ते). Not to be confused with aśnāti, ‘eats’, from the ninth-class aś.
अभ्युक्ता
Past passive participle of vac with abhi; va becomes u before the participle’s t (P. 6.1.15 (वचिस्वपियजादीनां किति)): ukta, abhyukta.
अद्भ्यः
Ablative plural of ap, ‘water’ (used only in the plural). Before -bhyas the p becomes t (P. 7.4.48 (अपो भि)), and the t then becomes d before the voiced bh.
विपश्चिता
Instrumental of vipaścit, ‘wise, inspired’. Kūranārāyaṇa explains it as ‘seeing in many ways’, with the y of paśyat dropped by the pṛṣodara class of irregular forms (पृषोदरादित्वात् पश्यच्छब्दावयवस्य यकारलोपेन व्युत्पादितः).
प्रतिष्ठा
From prati-sthā; Śaṅkara: प्रतितिष्ठत्यनयेति प्रतिष्ठा, ‘that by which one stands firm’.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

Three parts. First a prose sentence (brāhmaṇa): subject brahmavit, verb āpnoti, object param. Then a verse (ṛc), introduced by tad eṣābhyuktā: a nominal sentence with brahma as subject and three predicate adjectives in the same case (satyam, jñānam, anantam); then a relative clause yo veda … with nihitam (brahma understood) as object and two locatives, and its correlative so ’śnute with the object sarvān kāmān and saha governing brahmaṇā vipaścitā. Third, prose again: a chain of ablative-nominative pairs with sambhūtaḥ stated once and understood after each (from X, Y [arose]), ending in the person; then a nominal identification (sa vā eṣa puruṣo ’nnarasamayaḥ) and five deictic sentences (this … is the head, …), each with ayam or idam pointing to a part of the body, as a teacher would point.

Ambiguities

satyaṃ jñānam anantaṃ brahma: definition or description?
Śaṅkara takes the three words as marks (lakṣaṇa) that set Brahman apart from everything, not as qualities that distinguish one Brahman from others: सत्यं ज्ञानमनन्तं ब्रह्म इति ब्रह्मणो लक्षणार्थं वाक्यम्; each word excludes its opposite and none is related to the others (सत्यादिशब्दा न परस्परं सम्बध्यन्ते). Kūranārāyaṇa takes them as stating the nature of Brahman that sets it apart from matter, from bound and freed souls and from the eternal ones, and as pointing to its auspicious qualities: अतः सत्यादीनि पदानि कल्याणगुणप्रतिपादनपराण्येव निखिलहेयव्यावर्तकानि. The grammar (three neuter nominatives in apposition to brahma) allows both.
jñānam: knowing, or a knower?
Śaṅkara insists the word is an action noun, knowing itself, not an agent who knows: ज्ञानं ज्ञप्तिः अवबोधः … न तु ज्ञानकर्तृ. Kūranārāyaṇa rejects ‘mere knowledge’ (ज्ञानमात्रं ब्रह्मेति किमिति नाभ्युपेयते) and reads knowledge whose essence is knowing and which is also a knower, the all-knowing (vipaścit) of the same verse.
sa ’śnute sarvān kāmān saha brahmaṇā vipaścitā
Three readings of saha. Śaṅkara: ‘at once’ (सह युगपत्), and ‘as Brahman’ — the knower enjoys everything as the all-knowing Brahman, not one thing after another. Kūranārāyaṇa and Gopālānanda: ‘together with Brahman’ the knower enjoys its qualities, which are what kāma means here; Gopālānanda compares ‘he eats rice with curd’ (दध्ना सह ओदनं भुङ्क्ते इतिवत्). Madhva: the knower enjoys according to his capacity, with Brahmā and with the supreme, under his control.
guhā and parama vyoman
For Śaṅkara the cave is the intellect, and the highest space is best taken as the space in the heart (हार्दमेव तु परमं व्योमेति न्याय्यम्); he also records the reading of it as the unmanifest. Kūranārāyaṇa keeps them apart: Brahman abides in the supreme heaven and, out of compassion, also in the cave of the heart — न परमव्योम्नि गुहा ; न च गुहायां परमव्योम.
uttaraḥ pakṣaḥ
uttara means both ‘northern’ and ‘upper, later’. Śaṅkara takes the image as a person facing east, so the right arm is the southern wing and the left arm the northern: अयं दक्षिणो बाहुः पूर्वाभिमुखस्य दक्षिणः पक्षः.

Metre

mixed · syllables counted as written: 8 + 5 + 9 + 14 + 9 + 8 + 15 + 5 + 4 + 4 + 5 + 7 + 5 + 5 + 12 + 7 + 7 + 7 + 4 + 7 + 9

Prose (brāhmaṇa) enclosing one ṛc, which Śaṅkara calls a mantra (lines 3–6: satyaṃ … vipaściteti). The printed lines divide the verse by sense, not by pāda; Gopālānanda notes that its pādas are fixed by sense or by metre (अर्थवशेन वृत्तवशेन वा पादव्यवस्था).     Śaṅkara’s proof that satya excludes every effect: a modification is a name grounded in speech; only the clay is real.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Ego and self-image

Most of us, asked who we are, start with the body: a face, a height, a state of health. The passage starts there too, and takes it seriously — it names the head, the arms, the trunk — but it calls this only the first self, ‘made of food’. It suggests a practical question: how much of the way I see myself is about the body I feed and dress, and what would remain if that were set aside for a moment? This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin If you described yourself without mentioning your body, what would you say first?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Can something be ‘knowing’ without being a knower? Why might Śaṅkara insist on this, and why might Kūranārāyaṇa resist it?

A contemplation exercise Sit for a few minutes and notice the body as the text describes it: head, two sides, trunk, base. Then notice what is noticing.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.

Further notes

  • Madhva’s digital commentary has only one couplet on 2.1; his comment on the person made of food is printed under 2.2 and is cited here with that location.
  • The Sāyaṇa-pāṭha and Madhva’s text insert a t between a final n and s (…न्त्स…), an optional junction of recitation; Madhva’s text prefixes ॐ (corrections.json; spelling only).

Uncertainties recorded

  • interpretation saha brahmaṇā vipaścitā: ‘at once, as Brahman’ (Śaṅkara), ‘with Brahman’ (Kūranārāyaṇa, Gopālānanda) and ‘with Brahmā and the supreme’ (Madhva) are all given; the translation keeps both ‘together’ and ‘with’.
  • uncertain In the six-commentary edition this passage of Rāmānanda’s Ānandabhāṣya is printed as a running note marked ‘आ.भा.’ inside Kūranārāyaṇa’s section; the siglum is identified by the edition’s own colophons, and the importer files it under Rāmānanda.
