Taittirīya Upaniṣad · Śīkṣāvallī · Anuvāka 10 of 12

Triśaṅku’s declaration: ‘I am the mover of the tree’

A seer speaks in the first person as one who has reached the goal; the verse is handed on as a text to be recited by the student.

5 Faithful English translation

I [am] the mover of the tree. [My] fame [is high] like the summit of a mountain. Having the purifier on high, I am the excellent immortal, as in the one possessed of vigour [the sun]. [I am] radiant wealth. [I am] of good understanding, sprinkled with the immortal. Thus [is] Triśaṅku’s utterance after knowledge.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

I am the one who shakes the tree. My name stands high as a mountain’s ridge. Made pure from on high, I am the pure immortal, as it shines in the sun. I am treasure, full of light. I am wise, immortal, untouched by decay. — So spoke Triśaṅku when he had come to know.

Key concepts ऋषि seer अमृत immortality, the deathless आत्मन् self, Self ब्रह्मन् Brahman वेद Veda

7 Explain it to a beginner

Here a sage named Triśaṅku speaks as someone who has reached the end of the search. He says: I am the one who shakes the tree — the great tree of a life caught in endless coming and going. My name stands as high as a mountain ridge. I am pure, I am deathless like the light in the sun, I am a treasure full of brightness, I am wise and never wasting away. It sounds like boasting, but the tradition hears it differently: it is the voice of someone describing what he discovered about himself, not about his achievements. The passage was kept so that students could recite it, as a reminder of where study is meant to lead.

An analogy Like a climber’s note left in the summit register: brief, a little exultant, and written less for himself than for those still on the slope, to say that the top is real.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the passage actually say?

Five short sentences in the first person: I am the rerivā of the tree; my fame is like a mountain’s summit; with my purifier on high, I am the good immortal, as in the vājin; radiant wealth; well-minded, amṛtokṣita. Then a label: ‘thus Triśaṅku’s vedānuvacana’. The text does not say what the tree is, what the speaker had realised, or why the verse is placed here.

Traditional Interpretation

Why is this verse placed after the list of duties?

Śaṅkara: it is a text for recitation (स्वाध्यायार्थो मन्त्राम्नायः), and the recitation serves the arising of knowledge (अस्य च जपो विद्योत्पत्त्यर्थोऽधिगम्यते). Its place, right after the duties of 1.9, shows that to one who performs the obligatory rites without desire and wishes to know Brahman, seers’ insights about the self appear (एवं श्रौतस्मार्तेषु नित्येषु कर्मसु युक्तस्य निष्कामस्य परं ब्रह्म विविदिषोरार्षाणि दर्शनानि प्रादुर्भवन्त्यात्मादिविषयाणीति). Kūranārāyaṇa and Gopālānanda agree that it is a mantra to be recited for the success of knowledge (विद्यासमृद्ध्यर्थं जप्तव्यो मन्त्रः).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What is ‘vedānuvacana’?

veda here is taken not as the scripture but as ‘knowing’: वेदः वेदनम् आत्मैकत्वविज्ञानम्, and anuvacana as speech that follows (anu) its attainment. Śaṅkara compares Triśaṅku to Vāmadeva, who declared his identity with all things: वामदेववत्त्रिशङ्कुना आर्षेण दर्शनेन दृष्टो मन्त्राम्नाय आत्मविद्याप्रकाशक इत्यर्थः. Gopālānanda follows him on the word (वेदो वेदनमात्मविज्ञानं तदनुवचनम्). Kūranārāyaṇa reads vedānuvacana as the speaking of a mantra in meditation, and says the seer is named to show the mantra’s greatness (ऋषिनिर्देशो मन्त्रस्य माहात्म्य सूचनार्थः).

Traditional Interpretation

Who was Triśaṅku?

The text gives only the name. For Śaṅkara he is a seer who knew Brahman (त्रिशङ्कोः ऋषेः ब्रह्मभूतस्य ब्रह्मविदः). Kūranārāyaṇa calls him a kṣatriya seer (क्षत्रियमहर्षेः). The verse Madhva cites from the Yajurviveka calls him a king, a descendant of Manu, and a seer of mantras (इत्याह मन्त्रदृक् पूर्वं त्रिशङ्कुर्मानवो नृपः). Whether this is the Triśaṅku of the epic stories is not said by any of them.

Comparative Vedānta

How can a person say ‘I am the immortal’?

Here the schools part. For Śaṅkara the speaker has realised that the self is the one pure reality; ‘I am the good immortal’ is literally so (एवं सु अमृतं शोभनं विशुद्धमात्मतत्त्वम् अस्मि भवामि). Rāmānanda reads the verse as the freed self whose true nature has become manifest: free of all change, not part of saṃsāra (सकलभावविकाररहितोऽसंसारीत्यर्थः). Gopālānanda says the self is like that immortal, not that immortal itself (न तु तदेवामृतमहम्). The verse Madhva cites makes the purity and immortality gifts of Hari, by whom the speaker is purified and pervaded (अत्युत्कृष्टेन हरिणा पावितोऽस्मि यतः स्फुटम्).

Contemporary Reflection

What does the verse say to a reader now?

Whatever its doctrine, the verse records a voice that is no longer anxious. It does not ask for anything; it says what is so. Placed after a list of duties and before the teacher’s parting instructions, it reads like a glimpse of the destination given in the middle of the road — a reason to keep going. This is a modern reflection.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Śīkṣāvallī 10

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

The speaker speaks as Brahman, as Vāmadeva did. He moves the tree of saṃsāra as its inner self (अहं वृक्षस्य उच्छेद्यात्मकस्य संसारवृक्षस्य रेरिवा प्रेरयिता अन्तर्याम्यात्मना); his omniscience is shown in the creation, maintenance and dissolution of the world. The recitation helps knowledge to arise in one who performs the rites without desire.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Kūranārāyaṇa; Rāmānanda; Gopālānanda (modern), commentaries on Śīkṣāvallī 10 (six-commentary edition)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Kūranārāyaṇa: having cut down the tree of ego, the speaker has reached supreme devotion to Nārāyaṇa (परस्मिन् ब्रह्मणि नारायणे परां भक्तिं प्राप्तः) and enjoys the immortal like the eternally free (नित्यमुक्तवत्परमामृतभोक्तास्मि). Rāmānanda reads the verse as the freed self’s nature become manifest. Gopālānanda: the self resembles the Supreme by its own pure nature (नैसर्गिकशुद्धस्वभावतस्तत्साधर्म्यात् तत्सदृशोऽहमस्मि).

Dvaita · Madhva, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Śīkṣāvallī 10, citing the Yajurviveka

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Madhva comments only through verses he cites from a work he calls the Yajurviveka: the speaker is the cutter of the tree, purified by Hari, and immortal because pervaded by Viṣṇu who dwells in the sun, called vājinīvasu (तेनामृतेन सिक्तोहं तेन व्याप्तो यतस्सदा). The verses are reported as cited by Madhva.

Where they disagree

All read the verse as the utterance of one who has reached the goal, and all treat it as a text for recitation. They differ on the ‘I’: identical with Brahman (Śaṅkara), the freed self resembling the Supreme and devoted to Nārāyaṇa (Kūranārāyaṇa, Rāmānanda, Gopālānanda), or a self purified and pervaded by Viṣṇu (Madhva’s cited verses).

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • गिरेरिव gireriva → गिरेः + इव

    visarga sandhi (r-visarga)

    A visarga after a vowel other than a or ā becomes r before a vowel: gireḥ iva → girer iva.

  • ऊर्ध्वपवित्रो वाजिनीव ūrdhvapavitro vājinīva → ऊर्ध्वपवित्रः + वाजिनि + इव

    visarga sandhi; vowel sandhi (savarṇa-dīrgha)

    aḥ before the voiced v becomes o (P. 6.1.114 (हशि च), then a + u → o). vājini + iva: i + i → ī, P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः).

  • स्वमृतमस्मि svamṛtamasmi → सु + अमृतम् + अस्मि

    vowel sandhi (yaṇ)

    u before a dissimilar vowel becomes v: su + amṛtam → svamṛtam, P. 6.1.77 (इको यणचि).

  • सुमेधा अमृतोक्षितः sumedhā amṛtokṣitaḥ → सुमेधाः + अमृतोक्षितः

    visarga sandhi (āḥ before a vowel)

    After ā a visarga is dropped before a vowel, and the hiatus stays.

  • अमृतोक्षितः amṛtokṣitaḥ → अमृत + उक्षितः

    vowel sandhi within the compound (guṇa)

    a + u → o: P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः). (On the other reading, amṛtaḥ akṣitaḥ, aḥ + a → o with the a elided, written without avagraha.)

  • त्रिशङ्कोर्वेदानुवचनम् triśaṅkorvedānuvacanam → त्रिशङ्कोः + वेदानुवचनम्

    visarga sandhi (r-visarga)

    oḥ before a voiced consonant: the visarga becomes r.

Morphology

रेरिवा
A rare form with a reduplicated root syllable. Rāmānanda calls it an intensive with the intensive suffix dropped (रेरिवा इति यङ्लुगन्तम्) from a root rī ‘to wear away’ (रीङ्घातुः क्षयार्थः), hence ‘destroyer’; Śaṅkara glosses ‘mover, impeller’ (prerayitā). The nominative in -ā suggests a stem in -van. The derivation is uncertain and is marked so.
वाजिनि
Locative singular of vājin, ‘possessing vāja (food, vigour)’; with iva it fuses to vājinīva. Śaṅkara and Rāmānanda take vājin as the sun, who brings food through rain.
सुमेधाः
A bahuvrīhi with medhā takes the stem sumedhas, P. 5.4.122 (नित्यमसिच् प्रजामेधयोः); the nominative singular is sumedhāḥ.
त्रिशङ्कोः
Genitive singular of the u-stem triśaṅku: guṇa of the final u + s (triśaṅko-ḥ).
अस्मि
Stated once in the third line and understood with what follows: ‘[I am] radiant wealth, [I am] wise, …’ (Śaṅkara: अस्मीत्यनुवर्तते).

Anvaya

अहम् वृक्षस्य रेरिवा [अस्मि] । [मम] कीर्तिः गिरेः पृष्ठम् इव [उच्छ्रिता] । ऊर्ध्वपवित्रः [अहम्] वाजिनि इव सु अमृतम् अस्मि । द्रविणम् सवर्चसम् [अस्मि] । सुमेधाः अमृतोक्षितः [अस्मि] । इति त्रिशङ्कोः वेदानुवचनम् ।

aham vṛkṣasya rerivā asmi | mama kīrtiḥ gireḥ pṛṣṭham iva ucchritā | ūrdhvapavitraḥ aham vājini iva su amṛtam asmi | draviṇam savarcasam asmi | sumedhāḥ amṛtokṣitaḥ asmi | iti triśaṅkoḥ vedānuvacanam |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

Five short first-person statements, mostly without a verb; asmi (‘I am’) appears once, in the third, and is carried to the rest. Two of them are comparisons with iva. The last line, in the third person, labels the whole as a quotation: ‘thus [is] Triśaṅku’s veda-anuvacana’.

Ambiguities

What is the ‘tree’, and what does rerivā do to it?
Śaṅkara: the tree of saṃsāra, which is to be felled, and rerivā is its mover — the speaker, having become Brahman, moves it as its inner controller (अन्तर्याम्यात्मना). Rāmānanda and Kūranārāyaṇa: its destroyer (भृशं क्षपयिता, Kūranārāyaṇa); the verse Madhva cites says ‘cutter of the tree of saṃsāra’ (संसारवृक्षच्छेत्ताऽहं). Gopālānanda also allows the body as the tree and the self as its mover. Kūranārāyaṇa reads ahaṃvṛkṣasya as one word: ‘of the great tree of I-making’ (अहङ्कारमहावृक्षस्य).
vājinīva svamṛtam
Śaṅkara, Rāmānanda and Gopālānanda divide vājini iva su amṛtam: ‘like (the immortal) in the sun, I am the excellent immortal’. The verse Madhva cites reads a name of Viṣṇu in the sun, vājinīvasu (तत्रस्थो भगवान् विष्णुस्सम्प्रोक्तो वाजिनीवसुः), which divides the same letters differently. Kūranārāyaṇa takes vājinī as the eternal realm (वाजिनी - नित्यविभूतिः in his gloss).
amṛtokṣitaḥ
One compound, ‘sprinkled with the immortal’, or two words, amṛtaḥ akṣitaḥ, ‘immortal, undecaying’. Śaṅkara gives both, the second first (अमृतः अमरणधर्मा अक्षितः अक्षीणः), and supports the compound with a Brāhmaṇa phrase (अमृतोक्षितोऽहम्). The base prints no avagraha, so the letters allow either.
Is ‘I’ the person, or Brahman?
For Śaṅkara the speaker speaks as one who has realised identity with Brahman, like Vāmadeva; hence he can call himself the mover of the world-tree. Gopālānanda (modern) insists that the self is like the immortal, not identical with it (न तु तदेवामृतमहम्).

Metre

irregular · syllables counted as written: 8 + 8 + 14 + 7 + 8 + 11

Kūranārāyaṇa calls the verse a six-pāda jagatī (इयं षट्पदा जगति). As printed the five verse lines count 8, 8, 14, 7 and 8 syllables (45); the third line divides into 9 + 5 at vājinīva, or 8 + 6 if su amṛtam is read uncontracted. A jagatī of six pādas would have 48; the remaining difference is not reconciled here.

The witnesses differ here only in spelling (listed in section 14).

11 Connections with other scriptures

  • Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.4.10 Doctrinal comparison

    ऋषिर्वामदेवः प्रतिपेदेऽहं मनुरभवं सूर्यश्चेति

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Ego and self-image

First-person declarations today are usually about achievement: what I built, what I won. Triśaṅku’s ‘I am’ is of a different kind. Read with the commentators, it is not a claim about status but a report of what the self turned out to be when it was no longer defined by its possessions — so that ‘my fame is high as a mountain’ becomes a statement of clarity, not of reputation. The contrast can be a useful test for one’s own self-descriptions. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin If you described yourself without mentioning any job, possession or achievement, what would you say?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Why might a tradition hand on a seer’s first-person words to be recited by students who have not yet seen what he saw?

A contemplation exercise Write down three sentences beginning ‘I am’ that do not depend on anything you own or have done. Read them again in a week.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
