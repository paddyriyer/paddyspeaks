Taittirīya Upaniṣad · Śīkṣāvallī · Anuvāka 9 of 12

Truth, discipline, household — and always study and teaching

Twelve duties of a life, each paired with the study and teaching of the Veda; three teachers then argue over which matters most.

5 Faithful English translation

And the right, and recitation and teaching. And truth, and recitation and teaching. And austerity, and recitation and teaching. And restraint, and recitation and teaching. And calm, and recitation and teaching. And the fires, and recitation and teaching. And the agnihotra, and recitation and teaching. And guests, and recitation and teaching. And the human [dealings], and recitation and teaching. And offspring, and recitation and teaching. And begetting, and recitation and teaching. And progeny, and recitation and teaching. ‘Truth’ — thus [holds] Rāthītara, whose speech is truth. ‘Austerity’ — thus [holds] Pauruśiṣṭi, constant in austerity. ‘Recitation and teaching alone’ — thus [holds] Nāka Maudgalya; for that is austerity, for that is austerity.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Practise what is right — and study and teach. Practise truth — and study and teach. Austerity, self-restraint, calm of mind — and with each, study and teach. Keep the sacred fires and the daily offering, honour guests, attend to the dealings of human life, raise children and grandchildren — and with all of these, study and teach. Rāthītara, called Truthful, says truth is what matters. Pauruśiṣṭi, ever austere, says austerity. Nāka Maudgalya says study and teaching alone: for that is austerity — that is austerity.

Key concepts सत्य truth, the real धर्म dharma कर्मन् action, ritual work वेद Veda ऋषि seer ऋत the right; cosmic and moral order तपस् austerity; disciplined effort स्वाध्याय recitation; study of one’s own Veda

7 Explain it to a beginner

This passage is a list of the things a full life asks of a person: be right and truthful, live with discipline and self-control, keep the household rites, welcome guests, deal fairly with people, and raise a family. The surprise is the refrain. After every single item comes the same phrase — and keep studying, and keep teaching. Whatever else you are doing, learning and passing it on are never set aside. At the end three old teachers give their verdicts. One says truth is what matters most. Another says discipline. The third says studying and teaching are themselves the real discipline. The passage does not settle the argument; it lets each voice stand, and it has already shown, by its refrain, how much it values study.

An analogy Like a musician’s daily routine: whatever the day holds — work, family, errands — the scales are practised every day. The list of duties changes; the practice runs underneath all of them.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the passage actually say?

It names twelve things — ṛta, satya, tapas, dama, śama, the fires, the agnihotra, guests, ‘the human’, offspring, begetting and progeny — and joins each to the same pair, svādhyāya and pravacana, recitation and teaching. It states no verb. It then reports three teachers: Rāthītara holds for truth, Pauruśiṣṭi for austerity, Nāka Maudgalya for recitation and teaching alone, ‘for that is austerity’. The text does not say why this list comes here or which teacher is right.

Traditional Interpretation

Why a list of duties in an Upaniṣad of knowledge?

Śaṅkara places the anuvāka in an argument. The previous anuvāka ended by promising sovereignty through knowledge alone; that might suggest that the rites of the Veda are pointless. This list is set down to prevent that conclusion and to show that actions too serve the human goal: विज्ञानादेवाप्नोति स्वाराज्यमित्युक्तत्वात् श्रौतस्मार्तानां कर्मणामानर्थक्यं प्राप्तमित्येतन्मा प्रापदिति. Kūranārāyaṇa says the same in his terms: these are taught as to be practised as auxiliaries of knowledge (विद्याङ्गत्वेनानुष्ठेयानि प्रतिपाद्यते). Gopālānanda (a modern commentator in the same edition) adds that action is necessary even for one who desires to know (विविदिषोरपि कर्मणामावश्यकतां ज्ञापयन्).

Traditional Interpretation

Why is ‘recitation and teaching’ attached to every item?

Śaṅkara: so that even one engaged in all these duties should practise study and teaching with effort, because understanding depends on study and the highest good depends on understanding — स्वाध्यायाधीनं ह्यर्थज्ञानम्, अर्थज्ञानाधीनं च परं श्रेयः. Teaching keeps what was learnt from being forgotten and increases merit: प्रवचनं च तदविस्मरणार्थं धर्मवृद्ध्यर्थं च. Madhva gives a different reason: study and exposition are to be done at the time of every act, so they are understood with every item (सर्वकर्मकृतिकालेष्वपि स्वाध्यायप्रवचनयोः कर्तव्यत्वात् तयोः सर्वत्र अनुषङ्गः).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

How do the commentators define ṛta and satya here?

Śaṅkara refers ṛta back to his explanation in the first anuvāka and takes satya as truthful speech or the carrying out of what was understood (सत्यं सत्यवचनं यथाव्याख्यातार्थं वा). Madhva defines ṛta as right knowledge and satya as the speech and action that follow from it — यथार्थज्ञानम् ऋतम्, तत्पूर्वकं यथार्थवचनं करणं च सत्यम् — and supports it with a verse he cites from a work called the Śabdanirṇaya. Kūranārāyaṇa cites the Bhāgavata for ṛta as ‘pleasant, true speech’ (ऋतं च सूनृता वाणी), and Gopālānanda cites the same verse for satya as ‘seeing all equally’ (ऋतं च सूनृता वाणी सत्यं तु समदर्शनम्). The verses are reported here as cited by them.

Traditional Interpretation

Who are the three teachers, and does the Upaniṣad choose between them?

The text gives only names and patronymics. Śaṅkara explains them: Rāthītara of the Rathītara lineage, Pauruśiṣṭi son of Puruśiṣṭa, Nāka son of Mudgala. He reads Nāka’s ‘for that is austerity’ as the ground of Nāka’s view — यस्मात्स्वाध्यायप्रवचने एव तपः — and the whole report as emphasis on truth, austerity and study, all of which were already in the list. Kūranārāyaṇa: there is no austerity higher than study and teaching (स्वाध्यायप्रवचनाभ्यां परं तपो नास्तीति), adding that some take the repetition as marking pre-eminence (प्राधान्यख्यापनार्थेति केचित्).

Traditional Interpretation

What is the point of naming begetting and grandchildren?

Śaṅkara takes prajā, prajana and prajāti as three distinct duties: to have offspring, to approach one’s wife in season, and to see a son married so that grandchildren follow (प्रजातिः पौत्रोत्पत्तिः). He returns to the triad in the next anuvāka but one, arguing that it shows that a man must try to continue his line. These are duties of the householder; the commentators do not present them as conditions of knowledge itself.

Contemporary Reflection

Is study really ‘austerity’?

Nāka’s claim reverses an ordinary expectation. Austerity suggests fasting or hardship; Nāka says the steady, daily work of learning and passing on what one has learnt is the real discipline. The Gītā’s list of austerities includes recitation as ‘austerity of speech’. Whatever the Upaniṣad intended, the claim invites a question about one’s own practice: which kind of effort is harder to sustain, the dramatic or the daily? This is a modern reflection.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Śīkṣāvallī 9

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

For Śaṅkara the list answers an objection: if knowledge alone gives sovereignty, are rites useless? They are not: the anuvāka is placed here to show that actions are means to the human goal (कर्मणां पुरुषार्थं प्रति साधनत्वप्रदर्शनार्थ इहोपन्यासः). In his larger argument (on 1.11) actions purify the mind so that knowledge can arise, but liberation itself comes from knowledge alone.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Kūranārāyaṇa; Gopālānanda (modern), commentaries on Śīkṣāvallī 9 (six-commentary edition)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Kūranārāyaṇa reads the duties as auxiliaries of knowledge (vidyāṅga) that must be practised (विद्याङ्गत्वेनानुष्ठेयानि प्रतिपाद्यते), citing the Bṛhadāraṇyaka’s ‘by sacrifice, by gift, by austerity’ and the Brahmasūtra on the need of sacrifices. Gopālānanda: all this is to be done according to one’s eligibility, together with study and teaching (सर्वमिदम् आश्रमाङ्गं कर्म यथाधिकारं प्रवचनाभ्यां सहैव अनुष्ठेयम्). For both, action is part of the discipline in which knowledge grows, not merely preparation that is later left behind.

Dvaita · Madhva, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Śīkṣāvallī 9

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Madhva defines ṛta as right knowledge and satya as speech and action grounded in it (यथार्थज्ञानम् ऋतम्), and holds that study and exposition belong to every act. He closes with verses he cites from the Mahābhārata and from a work he calls the Karmatattva: that one who has rightly known Viṣṇu and expounds and recites has nothing left undone (सम्यग्ज्ञात्वा तु यो विष्णुं व्याख्यायीत जपेत च).

Where they disagree

All agree that the duties are to be practised and that study and teaching run through them. They differ on how action relates to knowledge: for Śaṅkara a preparation that purifies, after which knowledge alone liberates; for Kūranārāyaṇa and Gopālānanda an auxiliary of knowledge; for Madhva knowledge of Viṣṇu with recitation and exposition fulfils every act.

10 Advanced Sanskrit analysis
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • ऋतं च ṛtaṃ ca → ऋतम् + च

    m before a consonant

    A final m before a consonant becomes anusvāra: ṛtam ca → ṛtaṃ ca.

  • तपश्च tapaśca → तपः + च

    visarga sandhi

    A visarga before c becomes ś: tapaḥ ca → tapaś ca. The same in damaś ca, śamaś ca, agnayaś ca, atithayaś ca, prajanaś ca, prajātiś ca.

  • सत्यवचा राथीतरः satyavacā rāthītaraḥ → सत्यवचाः + राथीतरः

    visarga sandhi (āḥ before a voiced sound)

    After ā a visarga is dropped before a voiced sound: satyavacāḥ rāthītaraḥ → satyavacā rāthītaraḥ.

  • तप इति tapa iti → तपः + इति

    visarga sandhi (aḥ before a vowel other than a)

    aḥ before a vowel other than a loses its visarga and the hiatus stays: tapaḥ iti → tapa iti.

  • एवेति eveti → एव + इति

    vowel sandhi (guṇa)

    a + i → e: P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः).

  • नाको मौद्गल्यः nāko maudgalyaḥ → नाकः + मौद्गल्यः

    visarga sandhi (aḥ before a voiced consonant)

    The visarga becomes u before a voiced consonant, P. 6.1.114 (हशि च), and a + u gives o: nākaḥ maudgalyaḥ → nāko maudgalyaḥ.

  • तद्धि taddhi → तत् + हि

    consonant sandhi

    t before h becomes d, and the h is written as the aspiration of that d (dh): tat hi → taddhi.

Junctions outside the general rules

  • स्वाध्यायप्रवचने · एव — pragṛhya: svādhyāyapravacane eva: a dual ending in e is pragṛhya and does not combine with a following vowel, P. 1.1.11 (ईदूदेद्द्विवचनं प्रगृह्यम्).

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • स्वाध्यायप्रवचने svādhyāyapravacane

    itaretara-dvandva (dual) · स्वाध्याय + प्रवचन

    Vigraha स्वाध्यायः च प्रवचनं च

    recitation and teaching, the two together

  • अग्निहोत्रम् agnihotram

    tatpuruṣa (the name of a rite) · अग्नि + होत्र

    Vigraha अग्नये होत्रम्

    the offering into (or to) the fire; the daily agnihotra

  • सत्यवचाः satyavacāḥ

    bahuvrīhi · सत्य + वचस्

    Vigraha सत्यमेव वचो यस्य सः

    he whose speech is truth alone (Śaṅkara also allows that it is his name)

  • तपोनित्यः taponityaḥ

    saptamī-tatpuruṣa · तपस् + नित्य

    Vigraha तपसि नित्यः

    constant in austerity (or a name)

Morphology

स्वाध्यायप्रवचने
Nominative dual of a dvandva whose last member, pravacana, is neuter: ‘study and teaching’. Each line names one duty and adds ‘ca svādhyāyapravacane ca’ — ‘and also study and teaching’. No verb is stated; Śaṅkara supplies ‘are to be practised’ (अनुष्ठेयानि इति वाक्यशेषः).
राथीतरः
A patronymic with vṛddhi of the first syllable: ‘of the family of Rathītara’ (Śaṅkara: रथीतरसगोत्रः).
पौरुशिष्टिः
Patronymic in -i from Puruśiṣṭa, with vṛddhi of the first syllable: P. 4.1.95 (अत इञ्) forms ‘son of’ names in -i from stems in a.
मौद्गल्यः
Patronymic with vṛddhi: ‘descendant of Mudgala’.
मानुषम्
Neuter of the adjective mānuṣa, ‘human’, used as a noun: ‘what belongs to human life’ — for Śaṅkara the ordinary dealings of the world.
प्रजातिः
pra + jāti, ‘further birth’. Because prajā (offspring) and prajana (begetting) are already named, Śaṅkara takes prajāti as the birth of grandchildren: प्रजातिः पौत्रोत्पत्तिः.

Anvaya

ऋतम् च स्वाध्यायप्रवचने च [अनुष्ठेयानि] । … सत्यम् इति सत्यवचाः राथीतरः [मन्यते] । तपः इति तपोनित्यः पौरुशिष्टिः [मन्यते] । स्वाध्यायप्रवचने एव इति नाकः मौद्गल्यः [मन्यते] । हि तत् तपः ।

ṛtam ca svādhyāyapravacane ca anuṣṭheyāni | … satyam iti satyavacāḥ rāthītaraḥ manyate | tapaḥ iti taponityaḥ pauruśiṣṭiḥ manyate | svādhyāyapravacane eva iti nākaḥ maudgalyaḥ manyate | hi tat tapaḥ |

Prose order; words in [brackets] are supplied.

Syntax

A list without a verb. Twelve lines repeat one frame — ‘X ca svādhyāyapravacane ca’ — so that each named duty is joined to the same pair, recitation and teaching. The verb (‘are to be practised’) is understood, as Śaṅkara says. Then three nominal sentences report three teachers’ views, each in the form ‘[word] iti [teacher]’, the verb of thinking or saying understood. The last, Nāka’s, is given a reason with hi (‘for that is austerity’), repeated.

Ambiguities

Who says ‘tad dhi tapaḥ’?
Grammatically the reason clause can be Nāka’s own justification or the Upaniṣad’s comment on his view. Śaṅkara, Kūranārāyaṇa and Gopālānanda all give it to Nāka; none of them takes the anuvāka as deciding between the three teachers, and Śaṅkara says the repetition of truth, austerity and study is for emphasis (उक्तानामपि सत्यतपःस्वाध्यायप्रवचनानां पुनर्ग्रहणमादरार्थम्).
svādhyāya and pravacana
svādhyāya is recitation (or study) of one’s own Veda; pravacana is teaching it, or, Śaṅkara adds, the daily brahmayajña (the ritual recitation of the Veda): प्रवचनमध्यापनं ब्रह्मयज्ञो वा. Madhva takes pravacana as exposition (प्रवचनं व्याख्यानम्).
Names or epithets?
Satyavacas (‘truth-speaking’) and taponitya (‘always in austerity’) fit each teacher’s view so well that Śaṅkara allows both readings: an epithet earned by the view, or simply the man’s name (नाम वा तस्य; तपोनित्य इति वा नाम).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Corporate life

Professionals are often told that learning is something for quiet periods — a course between projects, a book on holiday. This passage makes study a refrain that runs under every other duty, and makes teaching part of the same refrain: what one learns is to be passed on, not hoarded. Teams that build teaching into ordinary work — reviews that explain, seniors who show their reasoning — keep knowledge alive in the way Śaṅkara says teaching does, by keeping it from being forgotten. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Of all the things you do in a week, which one could carry a little learning or teaching alongside it?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The three teachers disagree about what matters most, and the text lets them. What does a text gain by recording a disagreement instead of resolving it?

A contemplation exercise For one week, end each day by explaining one thing you learnt that day to someone else, or in writing.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.

  • Kūranārāyaṇa cites the Bṛhadāraṇyaka (‘yajñena dānena tapasā’) and Brahmasūtra 3.4.26 for the place of action; Kūranārāyaṇa and Gopālānanda cite the Bhāgavata (11.19) for the definitions of ṛta, satya, tapas and śama. Madhva cites the Śabdanirṇaya, the Karmatattva and the Mahābhārata. These are reported as cited by them.

Uncertainties recorded

  • variant The base text (Advaita Sharada) reads अग्नयश्य in the sixth line, a keying error for अग्नयश्च; corrected (corrections.json). Śaṅkara’s gloss अग्नयश्च आधातव्याः and the Sāyaṇa-pāṭha read च.
  • variant Madhva’s text doubles च after tapas, damas and śamas (‘tapaś ca ca svādhyāyapravacane ca’); the base and the Sāyaṇa-pāṭha have one (corrections.json, near 1.9).
  • review The six-commentary edition prints Rāmānanda’s Ānandabhāṣya on this anuvāka as a running note marked ‘आ.भा.’ inside Kūranārāyaṇa’s section; the importer files it under Rāmānanda, and nothing after the mark is quoted as Kūranārāyaṇa’s.
