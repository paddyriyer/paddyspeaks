Taittirīya Upaniṣad · Brahmānandavallī · Anuvāka 9 of 9

Where words turn back: the knower who fears nothing

The vallī closes with its verse: words and mind turn back from Brahman’s bliss, and one who knows it fears nothing; neither the good left undone nor the wrong done torments him.

5 Faithful English translation

‘From which words turn back, together with the mind, not having reached [it] — knowing the bliss of Brahman, one fears nothing at all.’ This [thought] indeed does not torment him: ‘Why did I not do the good? Why did I do the evil?’ He who knows thus [makes] these two the self (spṛṇute); for he [makes] both of these the self — he who knows thus. Thus the secret teaching (upaniṣad).

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

‘Words turn back from it, and the mind with them, without reaching it. Whoever knows that bliss of Brahman fears nothing.’ Such a person is not tormented by the thoughts, ‘Why did I not do the good? Why did I do wrong?’ Knowing this, he holds both the good and the bad within the Self and is strengthened by it — whoever knows thus. This is the secret teaching.

7 Explain it to a beginner

The vallī ends with a short verse and a comment on it. The verse says that words and thoughts cannot reach Brahman’s bliss: they set out and come back without arriving. But whoever knows that bliss is afraid of nothing. The prose then names the fear that is hardest to escape — looking back on one’s life and asking, ‘Why did I not do the good I could have done? Why did I do that wrong?’ The knower is not tormented by such questions. Śaṅkara says this is because he sees both the good and the bad as no longer his own burden; others say he is protected from both. Either way, the regret that haunts people at the end of life does not touch him. The last words, ‘this is the Upaniṣad’, mark this as the secret at the heart of the teaching.

An analogy Like trying to measure the sky with a ladder: however long the ladder, it comes back down; yet the person who has simply looked up is not troubled by the ladder’s failure.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the passage say?

A verse: ‘From which words turn back with the mind, not having reached — knowing the bliss of Brahman, one fears nothing at all.’ Then prose: this does not torment him — ‘Why did I not do the good? Why did I do evil?’ He who knows thus ‘spṛṇute’ the self with these two; for he does so with both — he who knows thus. ‘Thus the Upaniṣad.’

Comparative Vedānta

What is it that words cannot reach?

Śaṅkara: words and ideas apply to things with distinctions; used of the distinctionless Brahman they turn back without illumining it, and the mind with them, since speech goes only where an idea goes (तस्मात् सहैव वाङ्मनसयोः अभिधानप्रत्ययोः प्रवृत्तिः सर्वत्र). Kūranārāyaṇa argues against this: if words could not reach Brahman, the whole vallī that describes it would be meaningless, and the verse itself names it ‘bliss’ and ‘of Brahman’ (तदसङ्गतम्); what words cannot reach is the measure of a bliss that has no measure. Rāmānanda and Gopālānanda gloss alike: the far shore, its extent, is not reached (अप्राप्य इयत्तालक्षणं पारम् अलब्ध्वा).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Why is the verse repeated from 2.4, with one word changed?

At 2.4 the verse ends ‘na bibheti kadācana’, ‘never fears’; here ‘kutaścana’, ‘from anywhere’. Śaṅkara notices the difference: at the self made of mind the verse praised the mind by ascribing Brahman to it and denied fear only; here, in the non-dual, the very cause of fear is denied (इह अद्वैतविषये न बिभेति कुतश्चनेति भयनिमित्तमेव प्रतिषिध्यते), since for the knower there is no second thing to fear (न हि तस्माद्विदुषः अन्यद्वस्त्वन्तरमस्ति भिन्नं यतो बिभेति).

Traditional Interpretation

Why does neither the good undone nor the evil done torment him?

Śaṅkara places the torment at the approach of death: regret for good not done and fear of hell for evil done (पश्चात्सन्तापो भवति आसन्ने मरणकाले); they torment the ignorant, not the knower. Rāmānanda, Bhāradvāja and Gopālānanda give two reasons: the knower does not desire heaven, and his sins are burnt by knowledge (स्वर्गादिफलेच्छाभावात्, ब्रह्मज्ञानदग्धसर्वपाप्मत्वाच). Kūranārāyaṇa compares the Chāndogya: sin does not cling to the knower, as water to a lotus leaf (पुष्करपलाश आपः).

Comparative Vedānta

What does ‘he spṛṇute the self with these two’ mean?

For Śaṅkara the knower sees merit and demerit as the Self, emptied of their separate form, and so they become powerless to start another birth (तस्य आत्मभावेन दृष्टे पुण्यपापे निर्वीर्ये अतापके जन्मान्तरारम्भके न भवतः). For Kūranārāyaṇa he protects himself from the experience of their fruits (पुण्यपापफलानुभवभयात् रक्ष्यति इत्युक्तेः फलानुभवः नास्ति इत्युक्तं भवति). Both read the repetition as emphasis; Kūranārāyaṇa and Bhāradvāja add that it marks the end of the anuvāka.

Traditional Interpretation

Why ‘thus the Upaniṣad’?

Śaṅkara: the knowledge of Brahman taught in this vallī is the upaniṣad, the highest secret of all knowledge, because the highest good rests in it (सर्वाभ्यः विद्याभ्यः परमरहस्यं दर्शितमित्यर्थः - परं श्रेयः अस्यां निषण्णमिति). Kūranārāyaṇa: such a secret is not to be told to just anyone, and he cites the Gītā’s warning not to teach it to one without austerity or devotion (इदं ते नातपस्काय नाभक्ताय कदाचन, as cited by him).

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Brahmānandavallī 9

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Brahman is beyond words because it is without distinctions; the knower fears nothing because nothing exists apart from him to be feared, ignorance having been removed like the second moon seen by a defective eye (तैमिरिकदृष्टद्वितीयचन्द्रवत्). Merit and demerit, seen as the Self, lose the power to cause another birth.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Kūranārāyaṇa; Bhāradvāja-Rāmānuja; Gopālānanda (modern), commentaries on Brahmānandavallī 9 (six-commentary edition)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Words do not fail because Brahman lacks qualities; they fail because its bliss has no limit (तस्य इयत्तायाः अभावादेव वाङ्मनसोः तत्र निवृत्तिः उच्यते). The meditator who knows that boundless bliss is freed from all fear; he does not desire heaven, his sins are burnt, and he is protected from the fruits of merit and demerit.

Dvaita · Madhva (his verses at 2.7), Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, at the end of his section on 2.7; no comment on 2.9 in the digital text

A securely sourced doctrine of the school

Madhva has no comment on this anuvāka in the imported text. On fear, his verses at 2.7 say that Brahman causes fear to the ignorant and some fear to the knower until he is released (विदुषोऽल्पभयं कुर्याद् यावन्मुक्तिं व्रजत्यसौ), and for the released Brahman is always fearless (अथाभयं भवेद् ब्रह्म तस्य मुक्तस्य सर्वदा). How he read ‘yato vāco nivartante’ here is not recorded.

Where they disagree

All agree that the knower fears nothing and is not tormented by past deeds. They differ on why words turn back — for Śaṅkara because Brahman is without distinctions, for the Viśiṣṭādvaita commentators because its bliss has no measure — and on what freedom from merit and demerit means: seeing them as the Self (Śaṅkara), or being protected from their fruits.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • यतो वाचो yato vāco → यतः + वाचः

    aḥ before a voiced consonant

    The visarga (ru) becomes u before a voiced consonant, P. 6.1.114 (हशि च), and a + u → o.

  • आनन्दं ब्रह्मणो विद्वान् ānandaṃ brahmaṇo vidvān → आनन्दम् + ब्रह्मणः + विद्वान्

    final m before a consonant; aḥ before a voiced consonant

    ānandam → ānandaṃ, P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः); brahmaṇaḥ before v → brahmaṇo, P. 6.1.114 (हशि च).

  • कुतश्चनेति kutaścaneti → कुतश्चन + इति

    vowel sandhi (guṇa)

    a + i → e, P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः). kutaścana is itself kutaḥ + cana with ś for the visarga before c.

  • ना करवम् nā karavam → न + अकरवम्

    vowel sandhi

    na + akaravam: a + a → ā, P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः). The base file prints a space after nā; Śaṅkara’s gloss writes the words together (नाकरवं न कृतवानस्मि).

  • पापमकरवमिति pāpamakaravamiti → पापम् + अकरवम् + इति

    final m before a vowel

    A final m before a vowel is written together with it.

  • विद्वानेते आत्मानम् vidvānete ātmānam → विद्वान् + एते + आत्मानम्

    pragṛhya: no sandhi

    ete is a dual ending in e and so pragṛhya, P. 1.1.11 (ईदूदेद्द्विवचनं प्रगृह्यम्); it stands unchanged before a vowel, P. 6.1.125 (प्लुतप्रगृह्या अचि नित्यम्).

  • ह्येवैष hyevaiṣa → हि + एव + एषः

    vowel sandhi (semivowel; vṛddhi)

    hi + eva → hy eva, P. 6.1.77 (इको यणचि); eva + eṣaḥ → evaiṣa, P. 6.1.88 (वृद्धिरेचि); eṣaḥ before the vowel of ete loses its visarga.

  • इत्युपनिषत् ityupaniṣat → इति + उपनिषत्

    vowel sandhi (semivowel)

    i + u → yu, P. 6.1.77 (इको यणचि).

Junctions outside the general rules

  • एते · आत्मानम् — pragṛhya: ete ātmānam: the dual ete is pragṛhya, P. 1.1.11 (ईदूदेद्द्विवचनं प्रगृह्यम्), and does not combine with the following vowel, P. 6.1.125 (प्लुतप्रगृह्या अचि नित्यम्).
  • एते · आत्मानम् — pragṛhya: ete ātmānam again: pragṛhya dual, as in the line before.

Morphology

अकरवम्
Imperfect 1st singular of kṛ, ‘to do’ (a-kar-a-vam): ‘I did’. With na and kim: ‘why did I not do …?’.
बिभेति
Present of bhī, ‘to fear’, a reduplicating root (third class): bi-bhe-ti. In the same verse at 2.4 the text has kadācana, ‘ever’; here kutaścana, ‘from anywhere’.
स्पृणुते
Present middle of spṛ, a root of the fifth (nu) class: spṛ-ṇu-te. The commentators gloss it ‘pleases, strengthens’ (Śaṅkara: प्रीणाति बलयति वा) or ‘protects’ (Kūranārāyaṇa: रक्षति).
वेद
Perfect of vid with present meaning, ‘knows’ (the old perfect-present).

Syntax

The verse is one sentence: a relative clause (yato vāco nivartante aprāpya manasā saha) whose antecedent is the bliss of Brahman, then the main clause ‘ānandaṃ brahmaṇo vidvān na bibheti kutaścana’, closed by iti. The prose comments on ‘fears nothing’: a negative main clause (etaṃ ha vāva na tapati) whose subject is the quoted double question (kim ahaṃ … iti). Then a relative–correlative sentence (sa ya evaṃ vidvān … spṛṇute), repeated with hi (‘for’) and a final relative clause (ya evaṃ veda), and the closing formula iti upaniṣat.

Ambiguities

Why do words and mind turn back?
Śaṅkara: because Brahman, being without distinctions, is not the object of any word or idea; words used to reveal it return having failed to illumine it (अप्राप्य अप्रकाश्यैव निवर्तन्ते). Kūranārāyaṇa rejects reading this as proof that Brahman is without attributes (कश्चिदाह वाङ्मनसोः निवृत्तिवचनेन निर्विशेषत्वं प्रतिपाद्यते इति । तदसङ्गतम्): words turn back because the bliss has no limit to reach (तस्य इयत्तायाः अभावादेव वाङ्मनसोः तत्र निवृत्तिः उच्यते). Bhāradvāja and Gopālānanda gloss the same: what is not reached is the far shore, the limit (Bhāradvāja: आनन्दस्य इयत्तालक्षणं पारम् अवगन्तुं प्रवृत्ते वाङ्मनसी तदप्राप्य निवृत्ते भवतः).
ete ātmānaṃ spṛṇute
Śaṅkara: the knower sees the two, merit and demerit, as the Self, and so ‘strengthens’ the Self (and they lose their power to bind) (परमात्मभावेन उभे पश्यतीत्यर्थः). Kūranārāyaṇa construes ete as ‘from these two’ and spṛṇute as ‘protects’: he protects himself from both (एते एताभ्याम् आत्मानं स्पृणुते रक्षति). Gopālānanda: knowing that doing or not doing them brings him neither gain nor loss, he protects himself. The grammar allows both: ete is accusative dual, and the second construction supplies a sense the case does not state.
kim: ‘why?’ or ‘what?’
Śaṅkara takes kim as ‘why’ (किं कस्मात्): the remorse ‘why did I not do good?’ at the approach of death. Bhāradvāja’s gloss can also be read as ‘what good did I fail to do?’ (साधु - पुण्यकार्यं किमपि नाकरवम्).

Metre

mixed · syllables counted as written: 8 + 8 + 8 + 9 + 9 + 9 + 11 + 14 + 13 + 5 + 5

Lines 1–4 are the anuṣṭubh verse announced at the end of 2.8 (8 + 8 + 8 syllables, and 9 in the fourth line as written, because the quotation mark iti is joined to kutaścana: without it, 8). The rest is prose. The same verse, with kadācana for kutaścana, is quoted at 2.4.

11 Connections with other scriptures

  • Taittirīya Upaniṣad 2.4.1 Direct textual parallel

    यतो वाचो निवर्तन्ते । अप्राप्य मनसा सह

    yato vāco nivartante | aprāpya manasā saha

    The same verse at the self made of mind, ending ‘na bibheti kadācana’; Śaṅkara explains the change.

  • Kena Upaniṣad 1.3 Doctrinal comparison

    न तत्र चक्षुर्गच्छति न वाग्गच्छति नो मनः

    na tatra cakṣurgacchati na vāggacchati no manaḥ

    The Kena’s statement that neither eye, speech nor mind goes there.

  • Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.4.22 Direct textual parallel

    एतमु हैवैते न तरत इत्यतः पापमकरवमित्यतः कल्याणमकरवमित्युभे उ हैवैष एते तरति नैनं कृताकृते तपतः

    etamu haivaite na tarata ityataḥ pāpamakaravamityataḥ kalyāṇamakaravamityubhe u haivaiṣa ete tarati nainaṃ kṛtākṛte tapataḥ

    The Bṛhadāraṇyaka has the same thought and the same words (ete, tapataḥ): the knower crosses both ‘I did evil’ and ‘I did good’; what was done and not done does not torment him.

  • Chāndogya Upaniṣad 4.14.3 Doctrinal comparison

    यथा पुष्करपलाश आपो न श्लिष्यन्त एवमेवंविदि पापं कर्म न श्लिष्यत इति

    yathā puṣkarapalāśa āpo na śliṣyanta evamevaṃvidi pāpaṃ karma na śliṣyata iti

    Cited by Kūranārāyaṇa: evil action does not cling to the knower, as water does not cling to a lotus leaf.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Aging and mortality

People who care for the dying often say that the hardest pain at the end is not physical but regret: the things not done, the things done wrongly. The Upaniṣad names exactly these two questions and says that for the knower they lose their sting. It does not say that conduct does not matter — the Śīkṣāvallī ended with a code of conduct — but that what one is matters more than the ledger of what one did. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Is there a ‘why did I not …?’ or ‘why did I …?’ that you carry with you?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Words turn back from Brahman, the text says, yet the whole vallī has been describing it. What can words do with something they cannot reach?

A contemplation exercise Sit quietly and let one regret come to mind. Notice it without arguing with it, and notice what in you is aware of it.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
