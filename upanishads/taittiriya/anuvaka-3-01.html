Taittirīya Upaniṣad · Bhṛguvallī · Anuvāka 1 of 10

Bhṛgu asks his father: the mark of Brahman, and the way of austerity

The third vallī opens with a story: a son asks his father to teach him Brahman, is given a list of doors and a definition — that from which beings are born, by which they live, into which they return — and is left to find it by austerity.

Key concepts ब्रह्मन् Brahman तपस् austerity; disciplined effort आचार्य ācārya, teacher उपनिषद् Upaniṣad लक्षण defining mark, definition

7 Explain it to a beginner

A young man named Bhṛgu goes to his father, the god Varuṇa, and asks, “Teach me what Brahman is.” His father does not give him a neat answer. First he names some things to look at: food, breath, the eye, the ear, the mind and speech. Then he gives him a test that anything claiming to be Brahman must pass: it must be the source from which all beings are born, the support by which they live, and the place into which they return at the end. “Find out what that is,” he says. Bhṛgu does not ask for more. He goes away and practises austerity — disciplined effort and focused reflection — to discover the answer for himself.

An analogy A teacher who gives a student the properties a solution must have, and then leaves the student to find it, teaches more than one who hands over the answer.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the passage say?

Bhṛgu, son of Varuṇa, approaches his father and asks to be taught Brahman. Varuṇa names food, breath, eye, ear, mind and speech, and then defines Brahman as that from which beings are born, by which they live and into which they return; he tells his son to seek to know it. Bhṛgu practises austerity.

Traditional Interpretation

Why does the third vallī begin with a story?

Śaṅkara: the teaching of Brahman was completed in the second vallī (परिसमाप्ता च ब्रह्मविद्या । अतः परं ब्रह्मविद्यासाधनं तपो वक्तव्यम्); what remains is the means, austerity, and the meditations on food and the rest. The story praises the knowledge, because a father taught it to his beloved son (आख्यायिका विद्यास्तुतये). Rāmānanda says the same and adds that it is therefore not to be taught to the unfit (आख्यायिका विद्यास्तुतिद्योतनार्था).

Traditional Interpretation

What is the definition?

Śaṅkara: Brahman is that whose nature beings do not leave at their origin, life or dissolution — उत्पत्तिस्थितिलयकालेषु यदात्मतां न जहति भूतानि, तदेतद्ब्रह्मणो लक्षणम्. Raṅgarāmānuja, Rāmānanda and Gopālānanda (modern) all insist that the three together are the definition, not each alone (अतः समुदितमेव लक्षणम्; समुदितमेव लक्षणं ब्रह्मणः न तु प्रत्येकम्; त्रितयं समुदितं लक्षणम्): otherwise one might suppose a second cause of the world’s life or end. Raṅgarāmānuja adds that ‘from which, by which, into which’ restates a cause already known, and ‘that is Brahman’ predicates Brahmanhood of it (यतः येन यत् इति यद्वृत्तयोगात् कारणम् अनूद्यते । तद्ब्रह्मेति कारणस्य ब्रह्मत्वं विधीयते).

Traditional Interpretation

Why does Varuṇa not simply say what Brahman is?

Śaṅkara: Varuṇa left the teaching incomplete on purpose, since he did not name Brahman directly (साक्षाद्ब्रह्मणोऽनिर्देशात्); from this Bhṛgu understood that another means was needed, and took up austerity, the most effective means of all. That austerity is the concentration of the outer and inner organs (तच्च तपो बाह्यान्तःकरणसमाधानम्), and Śaṅkara supports it with a smṛti verse he cites: मनसश्चेन्द्रियाणां च ह्यैकाग्र्यं परमं तपः.

Comparative Vedānta

What is the austerity?

Govindarāja gives two readings: Bhṛgu performed fasts such as the cāndrāyaṇa, or he reflected (चान्द्रायणादिकम् अकरोत् । यद्वा आलोचनम् अकरोत्), deriving tapas also from tap ‘to consider’. Bhāradvāja-Rāmānuja makes it the restraint of the senses (इन्द्रियनियमनलक्षणरूपं तपः). Kūranārāyaṇa: once the thing to be known and its mark were taught, the pupil practised austerity to see it directly (एवं लक्ष्ये लक्षणे च उपदिष्टे शिष्यः स तपोऽतप्यत तत्साक्षात्कारार्थम्).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What do the forms tell us?

Govindarāja: yataḥ is an ablative of cause, which shows Brahman to be both the efficient and the material cause (यतः इति हेतौ पञ्चमी । तेन अभिन्ननिमित्तोपादानसिद्धिः); Raṅgarāmānuja too reads the ablative as causal. The vocative bhagavaḥ is a Vedic form (see morphology). The anuvāka ends on an absolutive, taptvā, so that the sentence runs on into the next.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Bhṛguvallī 1

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

The definition points to Brahman as that whose nature beings never leave at birth, life or death; the list names the doors through which it is approached. The third vallī adds nothing new to the knowledge, which is complete; it teaches its means, austerity, understood as concentration of mind and senses (तच्च तपो बाह्यान्तःकरणसमाधानम्).

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Kūranārāyaṇa; Raṅgarāmānuja; Kauśika Govindarāja, commentaries on Bhṛguvallī 1 (six-commentary edition)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Brahman is defined as the one cause, efficient and material, of the world’s origin, life and dissolution, the three taken together. Kūranārāyaṇa draws a further point from the words: because beings are said to be born and to perish, they are real — जन्मविनाशादिवचनात् सत्यत्वमपि भूतानाम् उक्तम् — for no one speaks of the birth and death of a snake that is only a garland seen wrongly (न हि मिथ्याभूतस्य स्रक्सर्पस्य तत्र जन्मविनाशौ). Raṅgarāmānuja distinguishes two kinds of definition: causality marks Brahman as qualified by the world, while satyaṃ jñānam marks Brahman in itself (जगत्कारणत्वलक्षणं विशिष्टनिष्ठम्; सत्यज्ञानत्वलक्षणं तु विशेष्यनिष्ठम्).

Dvaita · Madhva, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, one comment on Bhṛguvallī 2–7

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Madhva has no separate comment on this anuvāka; his single comment on the following ones reads the list of 3.1 as names of Viṣṇu — he is ‘eye’ because he sees (चक्षुः स चष्टे यद् विष्णुः) — and austerity as contemplation of knowledge, by which Bhṛgu came to know Janārdana step by step (ज्ञानानुसन्धानरूपतपसा तं जनार्दनम्).

Where they disagree

All agree that Brahman is the cause of the world and that austerity is the means to know it. Śaṅkara reads the definition as pointing beyond the world to the Self whose nature beings never leave; Kūranārāyaṇa and Raṅgarāmānuja read it as establishing Brahman as the real cause of a real world, and Kūranārāyaṇa argues explicitly against the illusion reading; Madhva reads every name as a name of Viṣṇu. They also differ on the austerity: concentration (Śaṅkara), sense-restraint or fasting and reflection (Bhāradvāja, Govindarāja), contemplation of knowledge (Madhva).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • भृगुर्वै bhṛgurvai → भृगुः + वै

    visarga before a voiced consonant

    The final s of bhṛgus becomes ru, P. 8.2.66 (ससजुषो रुँः), and appears as r before the voiced v.

  • भगवो ब्रह्मेति bhagavo brahmeti → भगवः + ब्रह्म + इति

    visarga before a voiced consonant; guṇa

    bhagavaḥ before the voiced b: the visarga becomes u, P. 6.1.114 (हशि च), and a + u gives o, P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः). brahma + iti → brahmeti, also by P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः).

  • तस्मा एतत् tasmā etat → तस्मै + एतत्

    ai before a vowel

    ai before a vowel becomes āy, P. 6.1.78 (एचोऽयवायावः), and the y is dropped, P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य); the hiatus that results stays.

  • होवाच hovāca → ह + उवाच

    vowel sandhi (guṇa)

    a + u → o: P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः).

  • यतो वा इमानि yato vā imāni → यतः + वै + इमानि

    visarga before a voiced consonant; ai before a vowel

    yataḥ before v: P. 6.1.114 (हशि च) and P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः). vai before i: āy by P. 6.1.78 (एचोऽयवायावः), y dropped by P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य), so vai imāni is written vā imāni.

  • यत्प्रयन्त्यभिसंविशन्ति yatprayantyabhisaṃviśanti → यत् + प्रयन्ति + अभिसंविशन्ति

    yaṇ

    i before a dissimilar vowel becomes y: P. 6.1.77 (इको यणचि).

  • तद्विजिज्ञासस्व tadvijijñāsasva → तत् + विजिज्ञासस्व

    consonant sandhi

    A final t becomes d before a voiced consonant: P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते).

  • स तपोऽतप्यत sa tapo'tapyata → सः + तपः + अतप्यत

    saḥ before a consonant; aḥ before a

    saḥ loses its ending before a consonant, P. 6.1.132 (एतत्तदोः सुलोपोऽकोरनञ्समासे हलि). tapaḥ before a: the visarga becomes u, P. 6.1.113 (अतो रोरप्लुतादप्लुते), a + u → o, and the following a is absorbed, P. 6.1.109 (एङः पदान्तादति), written with avagraha.

  • स तपस्तप्त्वा sa tapastaptvā → सः + तपः + तप्त्वा

    visarga before t

    The visarga becomes s before t: P. 8.3.34 (विसर्जनीयस्य सः).

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this anuvāka.

Morphology

वारुणिः
A patronymic, ‘son of Varuṇa’, formed with the taddhita -i; Govindarāja cites P. 4.1.95 (अत इञ्) (वरुणस्य ऋषेः अपत्यं वारुणिः).
भगवः
The Vedic vocative of bhagavat (classical bhagavan): Govindarāja cites P. 8.3.1 (मतुवसो रु सम्बुद्धौ छन्दसि), by which the final of a stem in -vat becomes ru in the vocative in the Veda.
उपससार
Perfect of upa-√sṛ, ‘go near’; used of a pupil approaching a teacher in due form.
विजिज्ञासस्व
Desiderative of vi-√jñā (‘wish to know distinctly’), imperative, ātmanepada; the desiderative of √jñā takes ātmanepada by P. 1.3.57 (ज्ञाश्रुस्मृदृशां सनः).
अतप्यत
Imperfect of √tap in the middle (ātmanepada) with tapas as its object, ‘practised austerity’; P. 3.1.88 (तपस्तपःकर्मकस्यैव) gives √tap this construction only when its object is tapas.
तप्त्वा
The sentence is left open: ‘having practised austerity, …’; its main verb is vyajānāt at the start of 3.2. The same open ending closes 3.2–3.5.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

A narrative frame in the perfect (upasasāra, provāca, uvāca) around two speeches. The first speech is a bare list in the accusative (annam … vācam iti), the object of provāca. The second is a single sentence with three relative clauses (yataḥ … jāyante; yena … jīvanti; yat prayanti abhisaṃviśanti), resumed by tat, the object of the imperative vijijñāsasva, and closed by a predication, tad brahma. The narrative resumes in the imperfect (atapyata) and breaks off on an absolutive (taptvā) whose main verb begins the next anuvāka.

Ambiguities

vai or vā
The text is written yato vā imāni. Sandhi allows vā (‘or’) or vai (‘indeed’, ai becoming ā before a vowel). Govindarāja reads vai (वै इति योगिप्रसिद्धिः); the padaccheda follows him, since ‘or’ gives no sense here. Śaṅkara and Bhāradvāja only repeat the words.
What the list is
Śaṅkara: the list names the doors through which Brahman is apprehended, the body, breath and the organs (ब्रह्मोपलब्धौ द्वाराण्युक्तवान्). Kūranārāyaṇa reads it as naming the twenty-four principles (एवं चतुर्विंशतितत्त्वसङ्ग्रहणम्); Rāmānanda and Bhāradvāja take Varuṇa to be saying ‘food is Brahman, breath is Brahman’, which leaves the son confused and prompts the definition.
prayanti
‘They go forth’: Śaṅkara takes it of death and dissolution, with abhisaṃviśanti as becoming one with Brahman. Raṅgarāmānuja and Bhāradvāja allow that prayanti may mean going to Brahman at release and abhisaṃviśanti dissolution at the end of a world-age.
vijijñāsasva — an injunction?
Kūranārāyaṇa: it enjoins meditation (अतः तद्विजिज्ञासस्व उपासनं कुरु). Raṅgarāmānuja: it enjoins neither inquiry nor meditation, but asks for attention to the definition being given (तद्विजिज्ञासस्व इति न विचारस्य उपासनस्य वा विधिः).

11 Connections with other scriptures

  • Brahmasūtra 1.1.2 Doctrinal comparison

    जन्माद्यस्य यतः

    janmādyasya yataḥ

    The second sūtra of the Brahmasūtra defines Brahman as that from which the origin, maintenance and dissolution of this world proceed. Raṅgarāmānuja discusses this anuvāka with it; the sūtra is generally taken to rest on this sentence.

  • Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.4.18 Doctrinal comparison

    प्राणस्य प्राणमुत चक्षुषश्चक्षुरुत श्रोत्रस्य श्रोत्रं

    prāṇasya prāṇamuta cakṣuṣaścakṣuruta śrotrasya śrotraṃ

    Śaṅkara cites this verse to show that breath, eye, ear and mind are doors to Brahman, ‘the breath of breath, the eye of the eye’.

  • Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad 1.2.12 Modern thematic association

    स गुरुमेवाभिगच्छेत्समित्पाणिः

    sa gurumevābhigacchetsamitpāṇiḥ

    The Muṇḍaka’s rule that one seeking knowledge should go to a teacher with fuel in hand; Govindarāja and Kūranārāyaṇa cite it for Bhṛgu’s approach.

  • Bhagavad Gītā 10.25 Modern thematic association

    महर्षीणां भृगुरहं

    maharṣīṇāṃ bhṛgurahaṃ

    Kūranārāyaṇa identifies the Bhṛgu of this story with the great seer named in the Gītā.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Ethical leadership

Varuṇa could have given his son an answer. Instead he gives criteria and sends him away to work. Good mentors still do this: they say what a good solution must achieve, let the learner try candidates, and stay available when the learner comes back unsatisfied. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin When did someone help you most by not giving you the answer?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry What would have to be true of something for it to be the source, the support and the end of everything? Can anything you know meet all three conditions?

A contemplation exercise For a few minutes, sit still and let the senses quiet down, as the commentators describe austerity; notice what remains when the eye, ear and speech are at rest.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
