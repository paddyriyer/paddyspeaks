Taittirīya Upaniṣad · Bhṛguvallī · Anuvāka 9 of 10

The third vow: make much of food

The third rule — make food abundant, or hold it in high esteem — with earth and space as food and eater, each resting in the other.

5 Faithful English translation

One should make food abundant. That [is] the vow. The earth indeed [is] food; space [is] the eater of food. In the earth space is established; in space the earth is established. So this food is established in food. He who knows this food established in food is established. He becomes possessed of food, an eater of food. He becomes great — by offspring, by cattle, by the lustre of sacred learning; great by fame.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Make much of food: that is the vow. The earth is food, and space eats it; space rests in the earth, and the earth rests in space. So food rests in food. Whoever knows that food rests in food stands firm, has food and enjoys it, and grows great in family, wealth, learning and good name.

Key concepts अन्न food लोक world, realm उपासना worship, meditation जगत् world, ‘the moving’ व्रत vow, observance

7 Explain it to a beginner

The third rule could mean two things, and the commentators divide. Taken literally it says: make food plentiful — grow it, store it, have enough to share. Most of the commentators take it more inwardly: make much of food, value it, be glad of even a little. Either way the attitude is the opposite of blame and refusal. The text then widens its view to the whole world: the earth is food, and space, which holds it, is the eater; space rests in the earth, and the earth rests in space. So, once more, food rests in food. The three rules together move outward, the commentators note, from the body to the elements to the worlds, showing everything held up by something else.

An analogy Like a host who sets a small dish on the table as if it were a feast, and so makes it one.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

Make food abundant; that is the vow. The earth is food and space is the eater; space rests in the earth and the earth rests in space; so food rests in food. Whoever knows this is established, has food, eats it, and becomes great in offspring, cattle, learning and fame.

Traditional Interpretation

What does the vow ask?

Śaṅkara reads it as abundance: the one who meditates on water and light as food and eater takes the vow of making food plentiful — अप्सु ज्योतिः इत्यब्ज्योतिषोरन्नान्नादगुणत्वेनोपासकस्य अन्नस्य बहुकरणं व्रतम्.

Traditional Interpretation

How do the Viśiṣṭādvaita commentators read ‘much’?

Kūranārāyaṇa: ‘making much’ is honouring — बहुकरणं सम्माननम् — receiving it with words like ‘how fortunate I am to have it’, अहो मया दिष्ट्या तल्लब्धम्. Earth and space, on which all life depends, are themselves to be honoured: किञ्च पृथिव्याकाशयोः नित्योपजीव्यत्वात् तयोः बहुमानः कर्तव्यः. Rāmānanda: regarding even a little as enough. Gopālānanda (modern): ‘make much’ means ‘think much of’.

Traditional Interpretation

What does Madhva add?

Madhva’s verses name the Lord ‘earth’ for his vastness and ‘space’ for his knowing all — स विष्णुः पृथिवीनामा पृथुत्वात्, आकाशः सर्ववेत्तृतः — and give the two vows of 3.7 and 3.9 together: अन्नाख्यं तं नैव निन्द्याद् बहु मन्येत तं सदा. One should think of Janārdana as ‘much’ in every way: बह्वित्येव जनार्दनम्.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Kūranārāyaṇa; Rāmānanda; Gopālānanda (modern), commentaries on Bhṛguvallī 9 (six-commentary edition)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

To make much of food is to honour it, and with it the earth and sky on which life depends; Kūranārāyaṇa ends that the worshipper of the supreme Self must hold food in high regard — सर्वस्य परमात्मोपासकस्य अन्नं बहुमानं कर्तव्यम्.

Dvaita · Madhva, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, verses filed under 3.8–3.9

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Earth and space are names of Viṣṇu; the vow is to hold him, named ‘food’, in high esteem — अन्नाख्यं तं नैव निन्द्याद् बहु मन्येत तं सदा.

Where they disagree

Śaṅkara alone reads bahu kurvīta literally, ‘make abundant’; the Viśiṣṭādvaita commentators and Madhva read ‘esteem highly’. They differ again on what is esteemed: food as a means, the world as the Lord’s body, or the Lord himself.

Morphology

कुर्वीत
Optative middle of kṛ, ‘one should make’; with bahu, ‘make abundant’ (Śaṅkara) or ‘make much of’, esteem highly (Kūranārāyaṇa, Gopālānanda, Madhva: bahu manyeta).
अन्नादः
Masculine here, agreeing with ākāśaḥ (in 3.7 and 3.8 neuter, with śarīram and jyotiḥ).

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

The frame of 3.7–3.8 again: an optative injunction (annaṃ bahu kurvīta), named a vow; the identifications (pṛthivī vā annam, ākāśo ’nnādaḥ); the two sentences of mutual support; the summary; the result and the refrain.

Ambiguities

‘Make much food’ or ‘make much of food’?
Śaṅkara: the vow is to produce food in abundance, for one who meditates on water and light as food and eater — अप्सु ज्योतिः इत्यब्ज्योतिषोरन्नान्नादगुणत्वेनोपासकस्य अन्नस्य बहुकरणं व्रतम्. Kūranārāyaṇa: making much of it means honouring it — बहुकरणं सम्माननम् — receiving it gladly (अहो मया दिष्ट्या तल्लब्धम्). Rāmānanda: treating even a little as enough — पर्याप्तमिति बुद्ध्या सत्कुर्यात्; Gopālānanda (modern): अन्नं बहु कुर्वीत बहु मन्वीत इति. Madhva’s verse has बहु मन्येत.
How do earth and space rest in each other?
Rāmānanda: space is the cause of earth through wind and the rest, and a cause is present in its effect — आकाशश्च कारणं पृथिव्याः वाय्वाद्यनुक्रमेण — while the effect rests subtly in its cause. Kūranārāyaṇa reads it of the worlds: the oblation offered on earth reaches the beings of the sky, and rain from the sky sustains the earth.
Body, cosmos, worlds
Kūranārāyaṇa sees an order in the three vows: the person (breath and body), the lights (water and fire), and now the worlds — एवमध्यात्मम् अधिज्योतिषं चान्नं प्रतिष्ठितम् इत्युक्तम्, अथाधिलोकं वक्तुमाह.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Wealth and consumption

Two readings, two lessons: produce enough to share, and value what you have. Both resist the habit of treating food as cheap and disposable. The second reading, ‘think even a little enough’, is a quiet answer to the sense that there is never enough. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin What would change if you treated what you have as enough?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Śaṅkara reads ‘make much food’; the others read ‘make much of food’. Which is harder, and which comes first?

A contemplation exercise Share a meal, or part of one, with someone this week, and notice how it changes the meal.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
