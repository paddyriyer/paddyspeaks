Śīkṣā: the science of sound and the saṃhitā meditations

Anuvākas 1.2–1.3: why an Upaniṣad about Brahman begins with a lesson in pronunciation, and how the joining of two sounds becomes a way of looking at the world

Anuvākas 1.2, 1.3

The second anuvāka names the six things a reciter of the Veda must learn and stops; the third takes the last of them, the joining of sounds (saṃhitā), and turns it into five meditations — on the worlds, the lights, learning, offspring and the body. This study sets out what the words say, how Śaṅkara, Kūranārāyaṇa and Madhva explain them, and why all three treat the passage as a meditation rather than a manual of phonetics.

Six words and a closing formula

Direct Textual Meaning

The whole of the second anuvāka is an announcement, a list and a close: शीक्षां व्याख्यास्यामः — ‘we shall explain śīkṣā’ — then वर्णः स्वरः, मात्रा बलम्, साम सन्तानः, and इत्युक्तः शीक्षाध्यायः, ‘thus the lesson on śīkṣā has been told’. Nothing is explained. The anuvāka names a discipline and its six headings and declares the lesson finished.

The six words are the vocabulary of Vedic recitation: varṇa, the sound or letter; svara, the pitch-accent; mātrā, the length of a vowel; bala, the effort of articulation; sāman, evenness; santāna, continuity — the running together of words in connected recitation. They stand at the beginning of an Upaniṣad that its school handed on by recitation, so that almost the first thing its students hear is a reminder of how it must be said.

What the six words name

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara glosses each word briefly: वर्णः अकारादिः (the sounds beginning with a), स्वर उदात्तादिः (the accents, udātta and the rest), मात्रा ह्रस्वाद्याः (short and the other quantities), बलं प्रयत्नविशेषः (a particular effort), साम वर्णानां मध्यमवृत्त्योच्चारणं समता (pronouncing the sounds at a middle pace, evenly), and सन्तानः सन्ततिः, संहितेत्यर्थः — santāna means continuity, that is, saṃhitā. The last gloss is the hinge to the next anuvāka, which is about saṃhitā.

Gopālānanda, the modern commentator in the six-commentary edition, gives the same six glosses almost word for word (बलम् - प्रयत्नविशेषः, साम - समता सा चोच्चारणे), and adds that the lesson is recited first so that no carelessness in accent creeps into the reading of the Upaniṣads: स्वरादिप्रमादो माभूदिति.

Why a lesson in sound comes first

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara’s one-sentence introduction gives a reason that is easy to miss. The Upaniṣad’s chief concern is understanding its meaning, and a reader so concerned may grow careless about the words themselves; the lesson is placed here so that this does not happen: अर्थज्ञानप्रधानत्वादुपनिषदः ग्रन्थपाठे यत्नोपरमो मा भूदिति शीक्षाध्याय आरभ्यते. The meaning does not excuse the reciter from the sound.

Kūranārāyaṇa gives a stronger reason, in a verse he cites with a reference to the Pāṇinīya Śikṣā: मन्त्रो हीनस्स्वरतो वर्णतो वा मिथ्याप्रयुक्तो न तमर्थमाह — a mantra deficient in accent or sound, wrongly used, does not convey its meaning. He then quotes at length from the śikṣā literature and the Taittirīya Prātiśākhya on the sounds, accents, quantities and pauses (these quotations are reported here as cited by him; they have not been checked against editions of those works). His conclusion is that the Upaniṣad does not teach the discipline but only recalls it, the details belonging to the śikṣā texts themselves: तस्य सर्वस्य प्रसङ्गादवसरे स्मरणमात्रं कृतमस्माभिरिति भावः.

Śīkṣā or śikṣā: the word itself

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The base text writes śīkṣā with a long ī; Madhva’s text has the ordinary śikṣā (the difference is listed on the Sources page). The commentators agree that the long vowel is a Vedic licence and the word is the ordinary one: Śaṅkara, शिक्षैव शीक्षा । दैर्घ्यं छान्दसम्; Kūranārāyaṇa, छान्दसो दीर्घः.

Both derive it from √śikṣ, ‘to learn’. Śaṅkara offers two analyses — the means by which the sounds are learnt, or the things learnt in it: शिक्ष्यते अनयेति वर्णाद्युच्चारणलक्षणम्. Kūranārāyaṇa names the suffix: the feminine noun in -ā is formed by P. 3.3.103 (गुरोश्च हलः), which gives the suffix a after a root ending in a consonant and containing a heavy syllable — इत्यकारप्रत्ययः, in his words. The verb vyākhyāsyāmaḥ, ‘we shall explain’, is a future of vi-ā-√khyā; Śaṅkara notes that the form belongs to √cakṣ, for which khyā is substituted (चक्षिङः ख्याञादिष्टस्य).

From the joining of sounds to the joining of worlds

Direct Textual Meaning

The third anuvāka begins with a short prayer that teacher and pupil may share fame and the radiance of sacred learning — सह नौ यशः । सह नौ ब्रह्मवर्चसम् — and then announces संहिताया उपनिषदं व्याख्यास्यामः, ‘we shall explain the hidden teaching (upaniṣad) of saṃhitā’, in five domains (adhikaraṇa): अधिलोकमधिज्यौतिषमधिविद्यमधिप्रजमध्यात्मम् — the worlds, the lights, learning, offspring and the self (the body). These, it says, are called the great saṃhitās.

Each domain is set out with the same four terms. When two sounds are joined in recitation there is a former sound (पूर्वरूपम्), a latter sound (उत्तररूपम्), the junction between them (सन्धिः) and what joins them (सन्धानम्). For the worlds: earth, sky, space, wind. For the lights: fire, sun, the waters, lightning. For learning: teacher, pupil, knowledge, instruction (आचार्यः पूर्वरूपम्, अन्तेवास्युत्तररूपम्, विद्या सन्धिः, प्रवचनं सन्धानम्). For offspring: mother, father, child, procreation. For the self: lower jaw, upper jaw, speech, tongue.

The anuvāka ends with a promise to the one who knows the great saṃhitās so explained: सन्धीयते प्रजया पशुभिः — such a person is joined with offspring, cattle, radiance, food and the heavenly world. The verb is the same as the subject: the one who meditates on joining is joined.

Śaṅkara: a meditation close to the text

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara explains why the Upaniṣad, having just insisted on correct recitation, now gives a meditation that stays with the sounds. A mind long formed by the text cannot be brought all at once to its meaning — यतोऽत्यर्थं ग्रन्थभाविता बुद्धिर्न शक्यते सहसार्थज्ञानविषयेऽवतारयितुमित्यतः — so the Upaniṣad begins with a way of seeing that is ‘close to the text’ (ग्रन्थसन्निकृष्टामेव). The method is superimposition: on the former sound one is to see the earth — संहितायाः पूर्वे वर्णे पृथिवीदृष्टिः कर्तव्येत्युक्तं भवति — on the latter the sky, and so on.

He reads the opening prayer as the pupil’s, not the teacher’s: शिष्यस्य हि अकृतार्थत्वात्प्रार्थनोपपद्यते ; नाचार्यस्य, कृतार्थत्वात्. And he takes ‘knows’ (veda) in the closing promise to mean ‘meditates’, giving one of his clearest definitions of upāsanā: a continuous stream of the same idea, according to scripture, not mixed with other ideas — उपासनं च यथाशास्त्रं तुल्यप्रत्ययसन्ततिरसङ्कीर्णा च अतत्प्रत्ययैः. The everyday use of the word confirms it: one ‘attends upon’ a teacher or a king (‘गुरुमुपास्ते’ ‘राजानमुपास्ते’), serving continually, and receives the fruit of that service. The fruit here is worldly — offspring and the rest up to heaven: प्रजादिफलं प्राप्नोतीत्यर्थः.

Kūranārāyaṇa: one saṃhitā in five places

Traditional Interpretation

Kūranārāyaṇa reads the five domains as five places where the one saṃhitā is to be contemplated: it becomes five by the difference of its seat — अधिकरणभेदेन पञ्चधाभवतीति. He analyses adhilokam as an indeclinable compound in the sense of a case-ending (‘in the world’), and mahāsaṃhitā as a compound of praise by P. 2.1.61 (सन्महत्परमोत्तमोत्कृष्टाः पूज्यमानैः): they are ‘great’ because they are honoured — ताः प्रशस्तत्वान्महासंहिता इत्युच्यन्ते.

His glosses on the four terms are concrete. Space is the interval between the two sounds — तयोर्वर्णयोः अन्तराळ आकाशो वेदितव्यः — and wind is the effort that pronounces them together: पूर्वोत्तरयोः सहोच्चारणार्थो यो यत्नः स वायुरिति वेदितव्यः. For the lights he cites a Manusmṛti verse on the offering that rises to the sun and returns as rain, to show why fire is ‘former’ and sun ‘latter’. Like Śaṅkara he concludes that the passage is for meditation, since knowing the saṃhitā has no visible fruit of its own: संहिताव्याख्यानमिदम् उपासनार्थम्.

Madhva: names of the Lord in the sounds

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva’s comment on these two anuvākas is a few lines long. He reads each of the six words of 1.2 as describing the Lord: वरणीयो वर्णः — varṇa because he is to be chosen; स्वरतेस्तु स्वरः; मानात् त्राता मात्रा — mātrā as the one who measures and protects. On 1.3 he says only that Nārāyaṇa and his other forms are to be meditated on in the five domains: नारायणादिरूपाणि लोकादिषु च पञ्चसु.

That is the whole of his digital commentary here. It shows the direction of his reading — every word of the Veda ultimately names Viṣṇu — but not how he would construe each line, and it should not be filled out by guesswork.

Where the readings meet and divide

Comparative Vedānta

On the main point the three commentators agree: neither anuvāka is a manual of phonetics. 1.2 recalls a discipline taught elsewhere; 1.3 uses the vocabulary of that discipline for an upāsanā, and its promised fruits are of this world and the next. Śaṅkara and Kūranārāyaṇa even agree in many glosses.

They divide on what the meditation is for and on what it is about. For Śaṅkara it is a preparatory discipline for a mind still bound to the text. Looking back from the second vallī, he calls these meditations ‘not opposed to ritual’ — संहितादिविषयाणि कर्मभिरविरुद्धान्युपासनान्युक्तानि — and says that they do not uproot the seed of saṃsāra: न चैतावता अशेषतः संसारबीजस्य उपमर्दनमस्ति. That is why the second vallī begins. Kūranārāyaṇa, opening 1.5, counts what precedes among the auxiliaries of the knowledge taught later (एवं विद्याङ्गानि उपदिश्य), and attends to its grammar and its sources in the śikṣā literature. For Madhva the objects of the meditation are forms of Nārāyaṇa present in the five domains. The Sanskrit itself names no deity in 1.3 at all; each reading supplies what the bare correspondences leave open.

Notes on the text

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The base file splits the word saṃhitā in four places (सं हिता) and doubles a vowel sign in अथाधिलोकम्; these are corrected and logged. A real variant falls in the last line: the base text and Śaṅkara read suvargeṇa lokena, ‘with the heavenly world’, while the Sāyaṇa-pāṭha and Madhva’s text read suvargyeṇa, ‘with the world that leads to heaven’. Śaṅkara’s gloss प्रजादिभिः स्वर्गान्तैः (‘with offspring and the rest, ending with heaven’) fits his reading.

Note also that 1.3 uses saṃhitā in two senses at once: the technical sense of 1.2 (the joining of sounds in continuous recitation) and the extended sense of a ‘joining’ that can be found in the worlds, the lights and the rest. The commentators’ glosses keep both in view; a translation has to choose a word, and ‘joining’ or ‘combination’ is closer to the Sanskrit than ‘text’ or ‘collection’.

Reading the two anuvākas today

Contemporary Reflection

A modern reader may find it striking that a text remembered for its teaching on Brahman begins by insisting on how its words are to be pronounced, and then asks its students to see the whole world in the gap between two sounds. Read as a reflection — not as what the commentators say — the passage holds two attitudes together: precision about the form in which knowledge is handed on, and attention to what connects things rather than to the things alone. Teacher and pupil are joined by knowledge and held together by instruction; mother and father by the child; the jaws by speech.

That is a modern way of noticing a pattern in the text. The tradition’s own reading is narrower and more exact: these are meditations, performed according to scripture, with stated results. A careful reader keeps the two apart.

Open questions

  • Do the six items of 1.2 summarise a particular śikṣā text, or the discipline in general? The commentators cite the Pāṇinīya Śikṣā and the Taittirīya Prātiśākhya; the anuvāka names neither. Kūranārāyaṇa’s quotations from those works are reported as cited by him and have not been checked against their editions.
  • suvargeṇa or suvargyeṇa in the last line of 1.3? Śaṅkara’s text and gloss support the first; the Sāyaṇa-pāṭha and Madhva’s text read the second. A printed Taittirīya Āraṇyaka with Sāyaṇa’s commentary should be consulted.
  • Madhva’s comment on 1.2–1.3 in the digital text is very brief. Whether a fuller construction exists in his bhāṣya or its sub-commentaries is not known from the materials of this edition.
