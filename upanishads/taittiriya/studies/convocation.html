Study III · Taittirīya Upaniṣad

The teacher’s last instruction: satyaṃ vada, dharmaṃ cara

Anuvākas 1.9–1.11: the duties that go with study, a seer’s declaration, and the address of a teacher to the pupil who is leaving

Anuvākas 1.9, 1.10, 1.11

The Śīkṣāvallī ends its teaching with conduct. 1.9 lists the duties of a householder, each to be kept ‘together with study and teaching’, and records three teachers’ views of what matters most. 1.10 is a seer’s declaration, Triśaṅku’s, which the commentators read three different ways. 1.11 is the teacher’s address to the departing student — speak the truth, practise dharma, honour mother, father, teacher and guest, give with faith, and in doubt follow the thoughtful. This study sets out the text, the commentators’ readings, and Śaṅkara’s long argument, attached to this anuvāka, about whether works or knowledge lead to the highest good.

Duties, each with study and teaching (1.9)

Direct Textual Meaning

The ninth anuvāka is a list with a refrain. Each item is followed by ‘and study and teaching’: ऋतं च स्वाध्यायप्रवचने च, सत्यं च स्वाध्यायप्रवचने च, and so through austerity, self-control of the senses and of the mind, the sacred fires, the agnihotra, guests, human affairs, offspring, begetting and grandchildren. The refrain makes the point by repetition: whatever else a person does, svādhyāya (study, recitation of one’s own Veda) and pravacana (teaching it) go with it.

Three named teachers then give their view of what is chief. सत्यमिति सत्यवचा राथीतरः — truth, says Satyavacas Rāthītara; तप इति तपोनित्यः पौरुशिष्टिः — austerity, says Taponitya Pauruśiṣṭi; स्वाध्यायप्रवचने एवेति नाको मौद्गल्यः — study and teaching alone, says Nāka Maudgalya, तद्धि तपस्तद्धि तपः, ‘for that is austerity, that is austerity’. The anuvāka records the three views without deciding between them; the last word, and the repetition, falls to Nāka.

Why duties are listed after a teaching on knowledge

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara begins with a problem. 1.6 said that self-rule is gained by knowledge; that might seem to make the rites of scripture and tradition useless, and the anuvāka is placed here to prevent that conclusion: विज्ञानादेवाप्नोति स्वाराज्यमित्युक्तत्वात् श्रौतस्मार्तानां कर्मणामानर्थक्यं प्राप्तमित्येतन्मा प्रापदिति. The refrain is there because study and teaching must be kept up with special care even by someone busy with every other duty: स्वाध्यायाधीनं ह्यर्थज्ञानम् । अर्थज्ञानाधीनं च परं श्रेयः.

Kūranārāyaṇa states the purpose more directly: the duties are now taught as auxiliaries of knowledge, इदानीं विद्याङ्गत्वेनानुष्ठेयानि प्रतिपाद्यते. Madhva makes the refrain general — study and teaching are to accompany every act, सर्वकर्मकृतिकालेष्वपि स्वाध्यायप्रवचनयोः कर्तव्यत्वात् तयोः सर्वत्र अनुषङ्गः — and takes pravacana as exposition (प्रवचनं व्याख्यानम्).

The commentators also separate ṛta and satya, which the list keeps apart. Madhva cites a definition: ऋतं यथार्थविज्ञानं सत्यं तत्पूर्विका कृतिः — ṛta is knowing things as they are, satya the action that follows from it. Kūranārāyaṇa cites a Bhāgavata verse for ṛta as pleasant, true speech: ऋतं च सूनृता वाणी कविभिः परिकीर्तिता. Śaṅkara takes satya here as truthful speech, सत्यं सत्यवचनं यथाव्याख्यातार्थं वा. The verses Madhva and Kūranārāyaṇa cite are reported as cited by them.

Triśaṅku’s declaration (1.10)

Direct Textual Meaning

The tenth anuvāka is a short declaration in the first person: अहं वृक्षस्य रेरिवा — ‘I am the … of the tree’ — कीर्तिः पृष्ठं गिरेरिव — ‘my fame is like a mountain’s ridge’ — ऊर्ध्वपवित्रो वाजिनीव स्वमृतमस्मि, ‘purified on high, I am the true immortal, as in the one rich in strength’; ‘wealth with radiance’; ‘of good intelligence, immortal, undecaying’. It closes इति त्रिशङ्कोर्वेदानुवचनम् — ‘thus Triśaṅku’s declaration after his knowledge’.

The word left blank above, rerivā, is the key to the verse, and it is rare. The commentators do not agree on what it means, and their disagreement changes the speaker’s relation to the ‘tree’.

Three readings of ‘the tree’

Traditional Interpretation

For Śaṅkara the tree is the tree of saṃsāra, to be cut down, and rerivā is ‘the one who moves it’ — the speaker, having realised the Self, speaks as the inner controller of the world: अहं वृक्षस्य उच्छेद्यात्मकस्य संसारवृक्षस्य रेरिवा प्रेरयिता अन्तर्याम्यात्मना. The verse is the utterance of a knower declaring that he has done what was to be done, as Vāmadeva did: आत्मनः कृतकृत्यताप्रख्यापनार्थं वामदेववत्. It is placed here, after the duties of 1.9, for recitation by one seeking knowledge: अस्य च जपो विद्योत्पत्त्यर्थोऽधिगम्यते.

Kūranārāyaṇa reads the first two words as one compound, ahaṃvṛkṣa, ‘the great tree of I-making’ — अहङ्कारमहावृक्षस्य — and rerivā as ‘the one who utterly wears it away’: भृशं क्षपयिता. Rāmānanda gives the grammar of that reading: an intensive formed from a root meaning ‘to waste away’ — रेरिवा इति यङ्लुगन्तम् । रीङ्घातुः क्षयार्थः. Madhva, in a verse he cites, has the speaker as the one who cuts down the tree of saṃsāra — संसारवृक्षच्छेत्ताऽहं — and makes Triśaṅku a human king: त्रिशङ्कुर्मानवो नृपः. Gopālānanda gives both senses, wearer-away and mover (प्रेरकः), and allows either.

So the same words make the speaker the inner ruler of the world (Śaṅkara), the destroyer of his own ego (Kūranārāyaṇa), or the cutter of saṃsāra (Madhva). All agree that it is the voice of one who has reached knowledge, and that the verse is recited as an aid to it.

The teacher’s address (1.11)

Direct Textual Meaning

वेदमनूच्याचार्योऽन्तेवासिनमनुशास्ति — ‘having taught the Veda, the teacher instructs the pupil’. The instruction begins with the two phrases for which the anuvāka is best known: सत्यं वद । धर्मं चर — ‘speak the truth; practise dharma’. Then: do not neglect study; having given the teacher the wealth he values, do not cut off the line of offspring; do not swerve from truth, from dharma, from what is wholesome, from prosperity, from study and teaching, from the rites for gods and ancestors.

Four short commands follow: मातृदेवो भव । पितृदेवो भव । आचार्यदेवो भव । अतिथिदेवो भव — ‘be one for whom the mother is a god’, and likewise the father, the teacher and the guest. Then a qualification of the teacher’s own authority: only blameless acts are to be followed, नो इतराणि — not the others; ‘only our good conduct is to be followed by you’. On giving: श्रद्धया देयम् । अश्रद्धयादेयम् — give with faith; do not give without it; give in plenty, with modesty, with fear, with understanding.

Last, what to do in doubt. If there is uncertainty about an act or about conduct, look to the brāhmaṇas there who are thoughtful, devoted, not harsh, and who love dharma, and act as they would: यथा ते तत्र वर्तेरन् । तथा तत्र वर्तेथाः. The anuvāka ends with four names for what has been said — एष आदेशः । एष उपदेशः । एषा वेदोपनिषत् । एतदनुशासनम् — the command, the teaching, the secret of the Veda, the instruction.

Śaṅkara’s reading, phrase by phrase

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara’s glosses are exact and often restrictive. Truth is to say what one has known by valid means: सत्यं वद यथाप्रमाणावगतं वक्तव्यं च वद. The repeated command not to neglect truth adds something to ‘speak the truth’: the word ‘neglect’ means that even through forgetfulness no untruth is to be spoken — प्रमादशब्दसामर्थ्याद्विस्मृत्याप्यनृतं न वक्तव्यमित्यर्थः. ‘Mother as god’ is a compound of the bahuvrīhi type, माता देवो यस्य सः, meaning that these four are to be honoured as deities: देवतावदुपास्या एते इत्यर्थः.

He reads the limit on the teacher’s example strictly: not even what teachers themselves do, if it is contrary — नो इतराणि विपरीतान्याचार्यकृतान्यपि. ‘Giving with understanding’ (saṃvidā) is giving with friendliness and the like: संविदा च मैत्र्य्यादिकार्येण देयम्. The people to follow in doubt are सम्मर्शिनः विचारक्षमाः — able to deliberate — and अलूक्षाः अरूक्षाः अक्रूरमतयः, not harsh, of a mind without cruelty.

Śaṅkara: do works lead to the highest good?

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara uses this anuvāka for one of his longest arguments. The instruction is given before the knowledge of Brahman, he says, because duties prepare the mind: they bring knowledge about by destroying accumulated faults — पूर्वोपचितदुरितक्षयद्वारेण विद्योत्पत्त्यर्थानि कर्माणीति. He cites the Īśā (11) here: अविद्यया मृत्युं तीर्त्वा विद्ययामृतमश्नुते.

He then asks whether the highest good comes from works alone, from works aided by knowledge, from the two combined, from knowledge aided by works, or from knowledge alone, and argues against each of the first four. Liberation is eternal, and what action produces is not; knowledge, which sees no distinction of agent and act, contradicts action and cannot be combined with it. His conclusion: अतः केवलाया एव विद्यायाः परं श्रेय इति सिद्धम्. Nor does this make the householder’s life the only path. The works that help knowledge are not only the householder’s rites — न ह्यग्निहोत्रादीन्येव कर्माणि, ब्रह्मचर्यं तपः सत्यवचनं शमः दमः अहिंसा इत्येवमादीन्यपि कर्माणि — so the other stages of life stand: अतः सिद्धान्याश्रमान्तराणि; all have a claim to knowledge.

Kūranārāyaṇa: the same words, another emphasis

Traditional Interpretation

Kūranārāyaṇa’s comment on 1.11 shares many glosses with Śaṅkara’s, some word for word: प्रमादशब्दसामर्थ्यात् विस्मृत्यापि अनृतं न वक्तव्यमित्यर्थः. Where he differs, he widens or makes concrete. Truth is speech that is as seen and beneficial to beings: यथा दृष्टार्थभूतहितवचनं सत्यं. Dharma is the duty laid down for one’s class and stage of life: श्रुतिस्मृत्युदितं वर्णाश्रमनियतं धर्ममनुतिष्ठ. ‘Do not cut off the line of offspring’ means: do not renounce in haste — त्वरितेन न प्रव्रज्या कार्या.

On giving, his gloss of hriyā, ‘with modesty’, is giving a little out of shame when one has little — असति विभवे, ह्रिया - लज्जामात्रेण, किञ्चिद्धेयम् — and saṃvidā is giving even by agreement. The ‘instruction’ is to be obeyed like a king’s decree: अमोघफलं राजशासनवत् अनतिक्रमितव्यम्. He reads the final particle u as restrictive — this, and no other path is to be sought: नान्यः पन्था अन्वेषणीयः.

Where the readings divide

Comparative Vedānta

All three commentaries treat 1.9–1.11 as the place where the Upaniṣad joins conduct to knowledge, and none treats the convocation address as merely ethical advice: it is ‘the secret of the Veda’ and is to be practised. Their glosses of the commands themselves overlap a great deal.

They divide on the relation of the duties to knowledge. For Śaṅkara the duties purify the mind so that knowledge can arise; knowledge alone gives the highest good, and other stages of life, including renunciation, are open to it. Kūranārāyaṇa calls the duties auxiliaries of knowledge and reads the line about offspring as a warning against hasty renunciation. Madhva, on 1.9, makes study and exposition accompany every act. The digital text of Madhva’s commentary has no comment on 1.11; his reading of the address is not known from the materials of this edition. On 1.10 they divide over the speaker himself: inner ruler, destroyer of ego, cutter of saṃsāra.

The grammar of the commands

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The address mixes three forms of command. Imperatives: vada, cara, bhava. Prohibitions with mā: स्वाध्यायान्मा प्रमदः and मा व्यवच्छेत्सीः are aorists, which is the tense used with mā by P. 3.3.175 (माङि लुङ्), and they lack the augment, as P. 6.4.74 (न माङ्योगे) requires. And gerundives used impersonally: सत्यान्न प्रमदितव्यम्, ‘there is to be no swerving from truth’, with the thing not to be neglected in the ablative.

The four ‘…devo bhava’ commands are compounds of the bahuvrīhi type, as Śaṅkara analyses them, describing the pupil and not the mother: the pupil is to be ‘one whose god is the mother’. The text also has two readings worth noting. Madhva’s text has ayuktāḥ for āyuktāḥ in the description of the people to follow in doubt, where Śaṅkara reads युक्ता आयुक्ताः; and in 1.9 the base file’s अग्नयश्य is a keying slip for अग्नयश्च, corrected and logged. Madhva’s text also doubles ca after three words in 1.9. These are listed on the Sources page.

Reading the address today

Contemporary Reflection

These lines are among the most quoted in the Upaniṣads, and they read naturally as a graduation address: the teacher finishes the course and tells the student what to keep. As a reflection — not as the commentators’ reading — two features stand out. The teacher limits his own authority, telling the student to follow only what is good in the teacher’s conduct. And the last resort in doubt is not a rule but people: those who think things through, are not harsh, and care for what is right.

The tradition’s reading is more specific than that. For Śaṅkara the address is part of a discipline that prepares for knowledge and is not itself the goal; for Kūranārāyaṇa it is the duty of one’s station, practised as an auxiliary of knowledge. Neither reads it as a general ethics detached from the Veda. A modern reader can value the address in both ways, but should not confuse the two.

Open questions

  • What does rerivā in 1.10 mean? The commentators give ‘mover’ (Śaṅkara), ‘one who wears away’ (Kūranārāyaṇa, Rāmānanda) and ‘cutter’ (Madhva’s cited verse); the word is rare and the materials of this edition do not decide it. Whether ahaṃ vṛkṣasya is two words or one compound is part of the same question.
  • Who are Satyavacas Rāthītara, Taponitya Pauruśiṣṭi and Nāka Maudgalya, and Triśaṅku? Śaṅkara takes Satyavacas and Taponitya as either epithets or names; Madhva’s cited verse calls Triśaṅku a king. Nothing else in the sources of this edition identifies them.
  • Madhva’s comment on 1.11 is missing from the digital text used here. A printed edition of his bhāṣya is needed before his reading of the address can be described.
