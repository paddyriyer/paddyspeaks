Study VI · Taittirīya Upaniṣad

Bliss, its measure and its limit

Anuvākas 2.6–2.9: being and non-being, ‘he is flavour’, the ladder of bliss, and the bliss from which words turn back

Anuvākas 2.6, 2.7, 2.8, 2.9

The second half of the Brahmānandavallī turns from the five selves to bliss. It begins with a warning about whether Brahman is or is not, and three questions a pupil asks; it tells how the self desired to be many and entered what it made; it says of that self ‘he is flavour’ and finds in it the ground of fear and fearlessness; it measures bliss on a ladder from a young, learned, strong and wealthy man up to Brahman, each step a hundredfold; and it closes with the verse ‘from which words turn back’ and with the knower whom neither good undone nor evil done can burn. This study sets out the text and the readings of Śaṅkara, Kūranārāyaṇa and Madhva in their own words.

Is Brahman, or is it not? (2.6)

Direct Textual Meaning

The verse that belongs to the self of bliss is a warning: असन्नेव स भवति, ‘he becomes non-existent’, असद्ब्रह्मेति वेद चेत्, ‘if he knows Brahman as non-existent’; अस्ति ब्रह्मेति चेद्वेद, ‘if he knows that Brahman is’, सन्तमेनं ततो विदुरिति, ‘then they know him as existent’. Śaṅkara takes ‘non-existent’ as ‘as good as non-existent’: असत्सम एव, one without any connection with the aims of life.

Then come अथातोऽनुप्रश्नाः, ‘now the follow-up questions’, which Śaṅkara assigns to the pupil: श्रोतुः शिष्यस्य अनुप्रश्नाः. Does anyone who does not know, on dying, go to that world? Or does one who knows attain it? The text does not answer them in so many words; the commentators find the answer in what follows.

He desired to be many (2.6)

Direct Textual Meaning

The answer begins with a creation: सोऽकामयत, ‘he desired’, बहु स्यां प्रजायेयेति, ‘may I be many, may I be born’; he performed tapas, created all this, and तदेवानुप्राविशत्, ‘entered into it’. Having entered, सच्च त्यच्चाभवत् — he became the existent and the beyond, the defined and the undefined, the founded and the unfounded, knowledge and non-knowledge, truth and untruth — and, the anuvāka ends, सत्यं चानृतं च सत्यमभवत्, as truth he became both truth and untruth.

For Śaṅkara the creation and the entry are said of Brahman for the sake of teaching, not as events in it; at the end of 2.8 he states that creation, entry, the gaining of flavour, fearlessness and the passing-on are all imagined in Brahman to make the defined self known: सत्यं ज्ञानमनन्तं ब्रह्मेति यथोक्तलक्षणात्मप्रतिपत्त्यर्थमेव बहुभवनसर्गप्रवेशरसलाभाभयसङ्क्रमणादि परिकल्प्यते.

Raso vai saḥ (2.7)

Traditional Interpretation

A verse says that the non-existent was here in the beginning, and from it the existent was born; it made itself, and so it is called ‘well made’ (sukṛta). Then: रसो वै सः, ‘he is flavour’; रसं ह्येवायं लब्ध्वानन्दी भवति, ‘having gained flavour one becomes blissful’; को ह्येवान्यात्कः प्राण्यात्, ‘who would breathe out, who would breathe in’, if this bliss were not in space?

Śaṅkara takes rasa in its everyday sense first: रसो नाम तृप्तिहेतुः आनन्दकरो मधुराम्लादिः प्रसिद्धो लोके — what satisfies and delights, the sweet and the sour. He then argues from experience: learned men who want nothing outward are seen to be content, as if they had found such a flavour; नूनं ब्रह्मैव रसस्तेषाम्, surely Brahman is their flavour, and so तस्मादस्ति तत्तेषामानन्दकारणं रसवद्ब्रह्म — Brahman exists, as the cause of their bliss. For him the passage answers the doubt of 2.6, whether Brahman is.

For Kūranārāyaṇa ‘having gained flavour’ describes the knower who has reached the Lord: ज्ञानी यावदात्मसु उत्तमानन्दी भवति, highest in bliss among all selves. Madhva reads the verse as a sequence of names of the Lord — the non-existent, the existent, the well-made — and concludes स आनन्दो रसस्ततः, ‘therefore he is bliss, flavour’.

Fear and fearlessness (2.7)

Comparative Vedānta

The anuvāka continues: when one finds a fearless foundation in that which is invisible, selfless, undefined and unfounded, अथ सोऽभयं गतो भवति, ‘then he has gone to fearlessness’; when one makes उदरमन्तरं — a gap, even a small one — in it, अथ तस्य भयं भवति, ‘then there is fear for him’; तत्त्वेव भयं विदुषोऽमन्वानस्य, ‘that itself is fear for the knower who does not reflect’.

Śaṅkara: the gap is the seeing of difference, and that is the cause of fear: भेददर्शनमेव हि भयकारणम्; अन्यस्य ह्यन्यतो भयं भवति, fear is of another from another. When one sees nothing other in the self, fear has no ground. Kūranārāyaṇa: the fearless foundation is steady devotion to the Lord as the goal, and the ‘gap’ is any break in it, which is saṃsāra itself: भगवद्भक्तिविच्छेदः एव अनन्तदुःखभयरूपः संसारः. Madhva divides the word udaram into its parts (त उदा जीवसङ्घाः स्युरुदरं तद्गतान्तरम्) and reads the passage of the Lord, who causes fear to all until release: to the knower a little, विदुषोऽल्पभयं कुर्याद् यावन्मुक्तिं व्रजत्यसौ, and to the freed none — अथाभयं भवेद् ब्रह्म तस्य मुक्तस्य सर्वदा.

The same word, ‘gap’, is a gap in knowledge of the one self for Śaṅkara, a gap in devotion for Kūranārāyaṇa, and a matter of the Lord’s governance for Madhva.

From fear of him the wind blows (2.8)

Traditional Interpretation

The next verse returns to fear on a cosmic scale: भीषास्माद्वातः पवते, ‘from fear of him the wind blows’, भीषोदेति सूर्यः, ‘from fear the sun rises’, and from fear fire, Indra and Death, the fifth, run their course. Śaṅkara reads it as a further proof that Brahman exists: the powers of nature are lords in their own right and yet keep strictly to their laborious work, as servants do for fear of a king; so there must be a ruler — तस्मादस्ति भयकारणं तेषां प्रशास्तृ ब्रह्म — and that cause of fear is the bliss that is Brahman.

Kūranārāyaṇa draws the same conclusion of sovereignty, with the Lord as ruler of all up to Yama and the four-faced Brahmā: सर्वसंहाराधिकृतयमचतुर्मुखपर्यन्तानां सर्वेश्वरादेव भयमुक्तम्.

The ladder of bliss (2.8)

Direct Textual Meaning

सैषानन्दस्य मीमांसा भवति — ‘this is the inquiry into bliss’. It begins with a man: युवा स्यात्साधुयुवाध्यायकः, young, a good young man, learned; आशिष्ठो दृढिष्ठो बलिष्ठः, most swift, firm and strong; and the whole earth full of wealth his. स एको मानुष आनन्दः — ‘that is one human bliss’.

A hundred human blisses are one bliss of the human gandharvas; a hundred of those, one of the divine gandharvas; then the ancestors in their long-lasting world, the gods born as gods, the gods who became gods by works, the gods, Indra, Bṛhaspati and Prajāpati, until स एको ब्रह्मण आनन्दः, ‘that is one bliss of Brahman’. At every step from the second the text adds श्रोत्रियस्य चाकामहतस्य — ‘and of one learned in the Veda, untouched by desire’ — so that the learned man without desire is said to enjoy each of these blisses.

The ladder ends with the self in the person and the self in the sun: स यश्चायं पुरुषे, यश्चासावादित्ये, स एकः — they are one. One who knows this, departing from this world, passes on through the five selves, ending एतमानन्दमयमात्मानमुपसङ्क्रामति.

What the inquiry measures: Śaṅkara

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara states the question the inquiry asks: किमानन्दो विषयविषयिसम्बन्धजनितः लौकिकानन्दवत् — is bliss produced by contact of a subject with objects, like worldly joy — or is it natural to the self? His answer is that worldly joy is a fragment of the bliss of Brahman: लौकिकोऽप्यानन्दः ब्रह्मानन्दस्यैव मात्रा. He supports this with the Bṛhadāraṇyaka, cited by him: एतस्यैवानन्दस्यान्यानि भूतानि मात्रामुपजीवन्ति.

The hundredfold steps show that fragment growing as ignorance, desire and action diminish, up to the bliss of Hiraṇyagarbha, which is the ‘bliss of Brahman’ at the top of the ladder: स एष हिरण्यगर्भो ब्रह्मा. Beyond that is the bliss of which all these are drops: यस्य मात्रा समुद्राम्भस इव विप्रुषः प्रविभक्ताः — divided like spray from the ocean — natural and without a second.

The refrain is the practical point. Learning and blamelessness are fixed at every level, but freedom from desire increases, so it is freedom from desire that is the means to the highest bliss: अकामहतत्वस्य परमानन्दप्राप्तिसाधनत्वविधानार्थम्. And of the person and the sun: अभिन्नः स्वाभाविकः आनन्दः परमात्मैव — the undivided, natural bliss is the supreme self itself.

The ladder in Kūranārāyaṇa and Madhva

Comparative Vedānta

Kūranārāyaṇa reads the ladder as a counting of the Lord’s bliss that is never completed: the learned man without desire shares each level — तल्लोकवासिनः अकामहतश्रोत्रियस्य तत्समानैश्वर्यं प्राप्तस्य इति सर्वत्र योज्यम् — but the bliss of the Lord has no measure, and that is why words turn back from it (below).

Madhva reads the ladder as a hierarchy of the freed. Liberation is certain for those who know, but bliss in liberation differs: स्यादेव मोक्षस्तत्रापि ह्यानन्दस्य विचित्रता. The man of the first step is a freed human, and each step names a class of freed souls, up to Brahmā: तस्माद् ब्रह्मा शतगुणो मुक्त इत्येष निर्णयः. The grading holds, he adds, in knowledge and devotion as well as in bliss.

The three readings take the same refrain differently: for Śaṅkara the ladder shows bliss fragmented by desire and whole without it; for Kūranārāyaṇa it shows the Lord’s bliss as beyond counting; for Madhva it shows real and lasting differences among the freed.

Where words turn back (2.4, 2.9)

Comparative Vedānta

The verse occurs twice. यतो वाचो निवर्तन्ते, ‘from which words turn back’, अप्राप्य मनसा सह, ‘not reaching it, together with the mind’, ‘knowing the bliss of Brahman’, — in 2.4 न बिभेति कदाचनेति, ‘he never fears’, and in 2.9 न बिभेति कुतश्चनेति, ‘he fears nothing from anywhere’.

Śaṅkara explains why words turn back: they name things with distinctions, and when used of what has none they return without having lit it up: अप्राप्य अप्रकाश्यैव निवर्तन्ते. He also explains the change of word. In 2.4 the verse was applied to the self of mind, Brahman being superimposed on it in praise, and so only fear as such was denied: न बिभेति कदाचनेति भयमात्रं प्रतिषिद्धम्. In 2.9, of the non-dual itself, the cause of fear is denied: न बिभेति कुतश्चनेति भयनिमित्तमेव प्रतिषिध्यते.

Kūranārāyaṇa rejects the inference that the verse makes Brahman featureless: ततः निर्विशेषतां ब्रह्मणः गमयितुं न शक्नुयात् — if words turned back from Brahman, the verse could not show that it is without features; it would only show that words are no means of knowing it. His reading: having set out to count the bliss of Brahman a hundredfold at a time, speech and mind turn back because it has no limit — तस्य इयत्तायाः अभावादेव वाङ्मनसोः तत्र निवृत्तिः उच्यते. Madhva’s comment calls the Lord मनोवाचामगम्यं and the knower fearless.

Neither good undone nor evil done (2.9)

Traditional Interpretation

The vallī ends: एतं ह वाव न तपति, ‘this does not burn him’ — the thought किमहं साधु ना करवम्, ‘why did I not do good?’, and किमहं पापमकरवमिति, ‘why did I do evil?’. Of these two thoughts, one who knows thus आत्मानं स्पृणुते — the verb is usually rendered ‘protects’, and Śaṅkara glosses it प्रीणाति बलयति वा, ‘gladdens or strengthens’ the self — and the vallī ends इत्युपनिषत्, ‘such is the secret teaching’.

Śaṅkara places the burning at the end of life: regret for good left undone comes आसन्ने मरणकाले, as death approaches, and fear of the consequences of evil with it. They do not torment the knower — न तपति नोद्वेजयति न सन्तापयति — because he regards both, good and evil, in the light of the supreme self: परमात्मभावेन उभे पश्यतीत्यर्थः.

Reading the ladder today

Contemporary Reflection

As a modern reflection, not a commentator’s reading: the inquiry into bliss begins with the best life a person can imagine — youth, goodness, learning, strength, the whole earth’s wealth — and calls it one unit. It then multiplies that unit until the arithmetic stops meaning anything, and at every step quietly adds that the one ‘untouched by desire’ has it already. Read this way the ladder is less a list of heavens than a way of making the best imaginable worldly happiness look small, and of placing a different kind of happiness beside it.

The commentators would agree on that much and on little else: whether what is left at the top is the self’s own bliss, the Lord’s bliss beyond measure, or the graded bliss of the freed is exactly what they dispute.

Open questions

  • Madhva’s comment on 2.9 is absent from the digital text, and his comment on 2.7 ends in a gap (‘तमआख्यमनुत्थानम् ....’); his reading of the closing verse needs a printed edition.
  • How to construe abhayaṃ pratiṣṭhāṃ vindate (2.7): Śaṅkara allows abhayam both as an adverb and as an adjective changed in gender; the choice affects whether ‘fearless’ describes the finding or the foundation.
  • Whether brahmaṇa ānandaḥ at the top of the ladder (2.8) is the bliss of Brahmā (Hiraṇyagarbha), as Śaṅkara and Madhva take it, or points to Brahman itself, as the verse that follows might suggest.
  • The base text prints ‘साधु ना करवम्’ with a space in 2.9 where the commentators read नाकरवम्; the letters are the same.
