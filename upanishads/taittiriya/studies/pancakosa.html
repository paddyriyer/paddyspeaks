The five selves: from food to bliss

Anuvākas 2.1–2.5: the self made of food, of breath, of mind, of understanding and of bliss, each shaped like a bird, and the old dispute over the fifth

Anuvākas 2.1, 2.2, 2.3, 2.4, 2.5

After saying that Brahman is ‘set down in the cave’, the Upaniṣad describes how: from the self came space, wind, fire, water, earth, plants, food and the person; and within the person made of food it finds four more selves, made of breath, of mind, of understanding and of bliss, each ‘within’ the one before and each pictured as a bird with head, two wings, body and tail. Śaṅkara calls them sheaths (kośa) laid over the self by ignorance and takes even the self made of bliss to be a product, with Brahman as its ‘tail, the foundation’. Kūranārāyaṇa reads the self made of bliss as the supreme self, and Madhva sees Viṣṇu in each of the five. This study sets out the text, the figure of the bird, and the three readings of the fifth self in the commentators’ words.

From the self to food

Direct Textual Meaning

The prose that follows the verse begins with an order of emergence: तस्माद्वा एतस्मादात्मन आकाशः सम्भूतः — ‘from that very self, space came into being’; आकाशाद्वायुः, from space wind, then fire, water, earth, plants, food, and अन्नात्पुरुषः, from food the person. Then: स वा एष पुरुषोऽन्नरसमयः — ‘this person is made of the essence of food’.

The sequence moves outward from the self to the body, and the rest of the passage moves back inward. Having arrived at the person made of food, the text pictures him — तस्येदमेव शिरः, ‘this is his head’ — and then looks for another self within.

Five selves, each within the last

Direct Textual Meaning

Each new self is introduced with the same words: ‘from this self made of …, other than it, within, is a self made of …’ — अन्योऽन्तर आत्मा प्राणमयः, made of breath; then made of mind (manomaya), of understanding (vijñānamaya), and of bliss: अन्योऽन्तर आत्मानन्दमयः. Of each it is said तेनैष पूर्णः — ‘by it this one is filled’ — and स वा एष पुरुषविध एव, ‘it too has the shape of a person’.

Each is then pictured with a head, a right and a left (literally ‘northern’) wing, a body (ātman) and a tail that is its foundation. The self of breath: तस्य प्राण एव शिरः, व्यानो दक्षिणः पक्षः, अपान उत्तरः पक्षः, आकाश आत्मा, पृथिवी पुच्छं प्रतिष्ठा. The self of mind: तस्य यजुरेव शिरः, the ṛc and sāman the wings, आदेश आत्मा, अथर्वाङ्गिरसः पुच्छं प्रतिष्ठा. The self of understanding: तस्य श्रद्धैव शिरः, ṛta and satya the wings, योग आत्मा, महः पुच्छं प्रतिष्ठा. The self of bliss: तस्य प्रियमेव शिरः, मोदो दक्षिणः पक्षः, प्रमोद उत्तरः पक्षः, आनन्द आत्मा, and last ब्रह्म पुच्छं प्रतिष्ठा — ‘Brahman is the tail, the foundation’.

Each self is also followed by a verse: on food (2.2), on breath (2.3), the verse ‘from which words turn back’ (2.4), on understanding (2.5), and on being and non-being (2.6). The word kośa, ‘sheath’, by which the five are usually known, does not occur in this vallī; it is the commentators’ word.

Why a bird: Śaṅkara on the figure

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara explains why the text begins with the body and gives it a bird’s shape. A mind that takes the outer body for the self cannot at once turn to the innermost self without a support, so the text leads it inward from what it already knows, ‘as one shows the moon by way of a branch’: शाखाचन्द्रनिदर्शनवदन्तः प्रवेशयन्नाह. The head is the familiar head, the wings are the arms, and the tail is the part below the navel, called a tail because it hangs down and supports: पुच्छमिव पुच्छम्, अधोलम्बनसामान्यात्.

The inner selves take the shape of the outer one, not by their own nature, मूषानिषिक्तप्रतिमावत् — like metal poured into a mould — and each fills the one outside it, वायुनेव दृतिः, as air fills a bellows.

Husking the grain: Śaṅkara’s sheaths

Traditional Interpretation

For Śaṅkara the order is a method of teaching. The text, he says, wishes to show Brahman, innermost of all, as one’s own self, and does so by removing five coverings made by ignorance, as rice is freed from its husks: अविद्याकृतपञ्चकोशापनयनेन अनेकतुषकोद्रववितुषीकरणेनेव तण्डुलान् प्रस्तौति. Each self is ‘imagined as the self’ in turn and then set aside for the one within.

The five are therefore not five souls. All beings are ‘selved’ by the five sheaths, he says, and also by the one self that is beyond them: पञ्चकोशातिगेन सर्वात्मना आत्मवन्तः — the self that is all and passes beyond the five.

What each self is made of

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara glosses the suffix ‘made of’ in each case. The self of breath: प्राणः वायुः, तन्मयः तत्प्रायः. The self of mind: mind is सङ्कल्पविकल्पात्मकमन्तःकरणम्, the inner organ that intends and doubts; its limbs are the Vedas, and ‘ādeśa’, its body, is आदेशः अत्र ब्राह्मणम् — the Brāhmaṇa portion, which gives the injunctions.

The self of understanding is made of decisive knowledge of the Veda’s meaning: वेदार्थविषया बुद्धिर्निश्चयात्मिका विज्ञानम्. Faith is its head because it comes first in everything to be done — सा सर्वकर्तव्यानां प्राथम्यात् शिर इव शिरः — and its tail, mahaḥ, is the first-born great principle: मह इति महत्तत्त्वं प्रथमजम्.

The self of bliss is made of the joys that knowledge and works bring. Its head is the pleasure of seeing what one loves, such as a son: इष्टपुत्रादिदर्शनजं प्रियं. मोद इति प्रियलाभनिमित्तो हर्षः, joy at getting what one loves; स एव च प्रकृष्टो हर्षः प्रमोदः, the same joy heightened; and its body, आनन्द इति सुखसामान्यम्, happiness in general, which runs through the other three.

The fifth self: Śaṅkara’s reading

Traditional Interpretation

Is the self made of bliss Brahman? Śaṅkara says no. आनन्दमय इति कार्यात्मप्रतीतिः — it is understood as a product, for two reasons: अधिकारात् मयट्शब्दाच्च — because the selves under discussion, made of food and the rest, are products, and because of the suffix ‘made of’ (mayaṭ). He adds that the text later says the knower ‘passes on to’ the self of bliss as to the self of food — आनन्दमयमात्मानमुपसङ्क्रामति (2.8) — and passing on is said of selves that are products, such as the self of food: कार्यात्मनां च सङ्क्रमणमन्नात्मनां दृष्टम्.

The Brahman of the passage is the tail of the self of bliss: ब्रह्म पुच्छं प्रतिष्ठा. That Brahman is the one defined at the start as truth, knowledge and the infinite, for whose sake the five sheaths were set out: यस्य च प्रतिपत्त्यर्थं पञ्च अन्नादिमयाः कोशा उपन्यस्ताः. It is the ground in which all the duality imagined by ignorance ends: अद्वैतं ब्रह्म प्रतिष्ठा.

The fifth self: Kūranārāyaṇa’s reading

Traditional Interpretation

Kūranārāyaṇa reads the same words the other way. The self made of bliss, other than and within the self of understanding (which for him is the individual soul), is the supreme self: अत आनन्दमयः आनन्दैकतानः — ‘made of bliss’ means wholly bliss. He reads it through the Brahmasūtra’s discussion of the ānandamaya, which he quotes: आनन्दमयो अभ्यासात्, ‘the one made of bliss, because of repetition’.

He meets Śaṅkara’s argument from the suffix directly. The objection that ‘mayaṭ’ here means a product, as in ‘made of food’, is answered by the sūtra he cites, विकारशब्दान्नेति चेन्न प्राचुर्यात्, and his conclusion is प्राचुर्यार्थ एवायं मयडिति निश्चयात् — here mayaṭ means abundance, not product. The bliss multiplied a hundredfold step by step in 2.8 cannot belong to an individual soul, so ब्रह्माश्रयोऽयम् आनन्द.

And to the objection from ‘Brahman is the tail’ — ननु ब्रह्म पुच्छं प्रतिष्ठा इति आनन्दमयात् अन्यत् ब्रह्मेति प्रतीयते — he answers that the head, wings and tail are Brahman’s own parts, as the limbs of the body made of food are not something other than the body: ब्रह्मैव स्वभावविशेषैः पुरुषविधत्वेन रूपित शिरःपक्षपुच्छादिरूपेण व्यपदिश्यते.

Madhva: Hari in five forms

Traditional Interpretation

For Madhva each of the five selves is the Lord. (In the digital text used here his comments on these anuvākas are filed one anuvāka late, and his comment on the self of breath is missing.) The self made of food is Viṣṇu, called ‘food’ because he eats all the worlds, and ‘made of’ means ‘abundantly’: प्रचुरं मय इत्युक्तं. Its tail is Aniruddha: पुच्छमित्येष भगवाननिरुद्धः स्वयं हरिः. In the self of mind तस्मिन् सङ्कर्षणः स्थितः; within the self of understanding is Vāsudeva, वासुदेवोऽन्तरस्ततः; and in the self of bliss तस्मिन्नानन्दरूपोऽसौ स्थितो नारायणस्सदा.

The five are one Lord standing in five ways, as body and embodied: देहदेहिवदेवासौ पञ्चधाऽवस्थितो हरिः. Madhva also reads the passage as a warning: those who know the fivefold Brahman as nothing other than the individual soul are ‘non-existent’ in the sense of 2.6, पञ्चरूपं च तद् ब्रह्म जीवादन्यन्न विद्यते, and those who know it as other than the soul are ‘existent’.

The suffix in question

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The dispute over the fifth self is partly a dispute about one suffix, -maya (mayaṭ). Pāṇini gives it in two senses. In the section on products and materials, P. 4.3.143 (मयड्वैतयोर्भाषायामभक्ष्याच्छादनयोः) forms words meaning ‘made of’ — the sense of annamaya, a body made of food. P. 5.4.21 (तत्प्रकृतवचने मयट्) forms words meaning ‘abounding in’ what the stem names.

Śaṅkara takes ānandamaya in the first sense because the series begins with annamaya and the context is products; Kūranārāyaṇa and Madhva take it in the second, ‘full of bliss’. Both senses are grammatical; the choice is made by reading the context, not by the suffix alone.

Where the readings divide

Comparative Vedānta

All three commentators agree that the passage leads inward from the body, and that what it finally points to is Brahman. They divide over where the pointing stops. For Śaṅkara the self of bliss is still a sheath, the subtlest, made of the joys that come and go; the Brahman of the passage is its ‘tail, the foundation’, and the five sheaths are removed to reveal it. For Kūranārāyaṇa the self of bliss is Brahman itself, ‘within’ the soul, and ‘Brahman the tail’ names one of its own parts. For Madhva each of the five is the Lord in a different form.

The text supplies both kinds of evidence. Śaṅkara can point to ब्रह्म पुच्छं प्रतिष्ठा, which seems to set Brahman apart from the self of bliss, and to the knower ‘passing on to’ that self in 2.8. Kūranārāyaṇa can point to the measure of bliss in 2.8, which makes bliss belong to Brahman, and to the Bhṛguvallī, where Bhṛgu’s last discovery is ‘bliss is Brahman’.

The five selves today

Contemporary Reflection

As a modern reflection, not a commentator’s reading: the five selves are often presented now as a map of the human being — body, energy, mind, intellect, bliss — and the bird’s shape as a picture of balance. The text does something more particular. It does not list five layers; it moves, one step at a time, from what a person most readily calls ‘myself’ to what is within that, and at each step says ‘this one, too, has the shape of a person’. The question it keeps asking is where the self that is being sought actually is.

The commentators do not agree on where the inward movement ends, and the edition does not decide for them. It notes only that none of them reads the passage as a description of the body’s anatomy, and none as a technique; each reads it as instruction toward knowing Brahman.

Open questions

  • Madhva’s comment on the self made of breath (2.2 in the text, filed under 2.3 in the digital source) is missing; which form of the Lord he places there needs a printed edition.
  • Whether ‘uttara pakṣa’ should be rendered ‘left’ or ‘northern’ wing: Śaṅkara’s gloss (the left arm, for one facing east) explains the word but does not show whether a ritual altar is in view.
  • Whether the five ‘selves’ were already understood as ‘sheaths’ (kośa) when the text was composed; the word is the commentators’, and the Upaniṣad’s own word is ātman.
