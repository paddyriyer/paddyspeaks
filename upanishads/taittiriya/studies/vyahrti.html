Study II · Taittirīya Upaniṣad

Bhūr bhuvaḥ suvaḥ, the fivefold and Om

Anuvākas 1.4–1.8: a teacher’s prayer for intelligence and for students, the fourth vyāhṛti, the person in the heart, the world counted in fives, and the syllable Om

Anuvākas 1.4, 1.5, 1.6, 1.7, 1.8

Five anuvākas move from prayer to meditation. A teacher asks the ‘bull of the Vedas’ for intelligence and then for prosperity and pupils (1.4); a sage adds a fourth word, mahaḥ, to the three utterances bhūḥ, bhuvaḥ, suvaḥ, and calls it Brahman (1.5); the person within the heart and the way out of the body are described (1.6); the world and the body are counted in fives (1.7); and the syllable Om is praised through its uses in the Veda (1.8). This study sets out what the text says and how Śaṅkara, Kūranārāyaṇa and Madhva each read it — readings that agree on the form of the meditations and differ on what they are about.

Five anuvākas, one movement

Direct Textual Meaning

The fourth anuvāka is a prayer in verse and formula. It addresses the one who is यश्छन्दसामृषभो विश्वरूपः — ‘the bull of the metres, of every form’ — and asks: स मेन्द्रो मेधया स्पृणोतु, ‘may that Indra strengthen me with intelligence’. It then asks for prosperity — ततो मे श्रियमावह — and for students, in a series of offerings each ending in svāhā: आमायन्तु ब्रह्मचारिणः स्वाहा, ‘may students come to me’.

The fifth names the three utterances (vyāhṛti) भूर्भुवः सुवरिति and a fourth, mahaḥ, taught by a sage called Māhācamasya: मह इति । तद्ब्रह्म । स आत्मा । अङ्गान्यन्या देवताः. The sixth places a person ‘made of mind’ in the space within the heart and describes a way out through the crown of the head: तस्मिन्नयं पुरुषो मनोमयः. The seventh lists three fives outside the body and three within it and concludes पाङ्क्तं वा इदं सर्वम्, ‘all this is fivefold’. The eighth begins ओमिति ब्रह्म and lists the places in ritual and recitation where Om is said.

The anuvākas are separate, but they move in one direction: from asking for the capacity to learn, to meditations on the world and the self, to the one syllable that, in this Upaniṣad’s words, is ‘all this’.

A prayer for intelligence, and for wealth (1.4)

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara divides the anuvāka in two: muttered prayers (japa) for one who wants intelligence, and offerings (homa) for one who wants prosperity — मेधाकामस्य श्रीकामस्य च तत्प्राप्तिसाधनं जपहोमावुच्येते. The ‘bull of the metres’ is Om, praised with these titles because it is the object of meditation here: ओङ्कारो ह्यत्रोपास्य इति. Om is called ‘a sheath of Brahman’ (brahmaṇaḥ kośaḥ) because Brahman is perceived in it, as a sword in its sheath, and ‘covered by intelligence’ because ordinary understanding does not see what it is.

Why should a teaching about knowledge ask for cattle and clothes? Śaṅkara’s answer is a chain. Prosperity is asked for only after intelligence, because wealth without intelligence does harm — अमेधसो हि श्रीरनर्थायैवेति — and wealth serves ritual, ritual removes accumulated faults, and when they are removed knowledge shines: धनं च कर्मार्थम् ; कर्म च उपात्तदुरितक्षयार्थम् ; तत्क्षये हि विद्या प्रकाशते. The offerings asking that students come to the teacher ‘as waters run downhill’ belong, in his division, to the same set of offerings for prosperity.

Madhva on the prayer

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva reads the anuvāka as addressed to Hari throughout, in verses he cites: यो विश्वरूपो भगवांश्च्छन्दसामधिपो हरिः. The ‘sheath of Brahman’ is Viṣṇu, so called because the Vedas dwell in him (‘vedāvāsatvato viṣṇur brahmakośa iti smṛtaḥ’), and bhaga, the name used in the offerings, is again the Lord, full of six qualities. These are Madhva’s citations; the works he names have not been checked independently, and they are reported here as cited by him.

The fourth utterance (1.5)

Direct Textual Meaning

The three vyāhṛtis — bhūḥ, bhuvaḥ, suvaḥ — were well known; the anuvāka presents mahaḥ as a fourth, made known by Māhācamasya: तासामु ह स्मैतां चतुर्थीम् । माहाचमस्यः प्रवेदयते. It then sets out four sets of four. Bhūḥ is this world, fire, the ṛc verses and the in-breath (prāṇa); bhuvaḥ is the mid-region, wind, the sāmans and the out-breath (apāna); suvaḥ is the world beyond, the sun, the yajus formulas and the diffused breath (vyāna).

Mahaḥ is in each case what makes the others great: आदित्येन वाव सर्वे लोका महीयन्ते (the sun, among the worlds), the moon among the lights, brahman among the Vedas, and food among the breaths — अन्नेन वाव सर्वे प्राणा महीयन्ते. The sun therefore appears twice, as mahaḥ among the worlds and as suvaḥ among the lights. The anuvāka sums up चतस्रश्चतस्रो व्याहृतयः — four times four — and promises that the one who knows them knows Brahman, and all the gods bring offerings to him: सर्वेऽस्मै देवा बलिमावहन्ति.

The words bhūḥ, suvaḥ, mahaḥ and ātman

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Where most Vedic texts have svar, the Taittirīya texts — all the witnesses of this edition among them — write suvar, with a vowel between s and v. Kūranārāyaṇa analyses it as su-, ‘well’, and a form of √vṛ, ‘to choose’: सुष्ठुशोभनं वरणीयं प्रापणीयं सुवः. He derives bhūḥ as the place in which all beings come to be (भवन्त्यस्मिन् सर्वाणि भूतानीति भूः), and mahaḥ as that by which the universe is honoured.

The commentators exploit the verb that the anuvāka itself repeats, mahīyante, ‘are made great, are honoured’. Śaṅkara connects the word mahaḥ with Brahman’s greatness — महद्धि ब्रह्म ; महश्च व्याहृतिः — and derives ātman, as often, from √āp, ‘to pervade’: आप्नोतेर्व्याप्तिकर्मणः आत्मा. The brahman that makes all the Vedas great is, for him, the syllable Om, since only a sound fits in a list about the Vedas: ब्रह्मेत्योङ्कारः, शब्दाधिकारे अन्यस्यासम्भवात्. Kūranārāyaṇa takes it the same way: ब्रह्मणा - प्रणवेन कारणभूतेन सर्वे वेदाः महीयन्ते.

Śaṅkara: one meditation in two anuvākas

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara insists that 1.5 and 1.6 teach a single meditation: उभयोर्ह्यनुवाकयोरेकमुपासनम्. 1.5 says that mahaḥ is Brahman, the self whose limbs are the other gods; 1.6 says where that Brahman is to be met and what its qualities are. The fourth utterance names Hiraṇyagarbha, Brahman with the vyāhṛtis as its body — मह इत्येतस्य हिरण्यगर्भस्य व्याहृत्यात्मनो ब्रह्मणोऽङ्गान्यन्या देवता — and the space of the heart is its place of meeting, as the śālagrāma stone is for Viṣṇu: सालग्राम इव विष्णोः.

The ‘udder-like’ hanging between the palates and the parting of the hair at the crown mark the course of a channel which Śaṅkara identifies with the suṣumnā, सुषुम्ना नाम नाडी योगशास्त्रेषु प्रसिद्धा, the ‘womb of Indra’, that is, the way to Brahman. The one who meditates so passes out through the crown and rests in fire, wind, the sun and Brahman, and gains ‘self-rule’ (svārājya): स्वयमेव राजा अधिपतिर्भवति — he becomes lord of the gods who are Brahman’s limbs, as Brahman is. This is a real attainment, but a limited one. Opening the next vallī, Śaṅkara describes what has been taught as a vision of the self still with its limiting adjuncts, whose fruit is self-rule — अन्तःसोपाधिकमात्मदर्शनमुक्तं व्याहृतिद्वारेण स्वाराज्यफलम् — and says that it does not uproot the seed of saṃsāra; the knowledge that does is the subject of that vallī.

Kūranārāyaṇa: the body of Brahman, and the self that goes

Traditional Interpretation

Kūranārāyaṇa reads the same words through the relation of body and self. Mahaḥ names the supreme Brahman as the whole whose parts the others are — अनया चतुर्थ्या व्याहृत्या अङ्गिभूतं परं ब्रह्म प्रतिपाद्यते — and the other three name its body. It is the self of all beings as the embodied self is of a body: यथैकस्य शरीरस्य शरीरि तथा सर्वेषां भूतानाम् आत्मा. The gods, ‘its limbs’, are its subordinates.

In 1.6 he takes the person ‘made of mind’ in the heart to be the individual self — अयं पुरुषः जीवात्मा — grasped by a mind purified in yoga, and the anuvāka to describe its departure by the path of the gods to the Lord: देवयानमार्गेण भगवत्प्राप्तिप्रकार उच्यते. ‘Self-rule’ is then freedom from the sway of karma, which is service of the Lord with every obstacle removed: अकर्मवश्यत्वं भगवत्कैङ्कर्यं प्रतिबन्धकनिवृत्तिरूपं.

Madhva: the four forms of the Lord

Traditional Interpretation

For Madhva the four utterances name four forms of the Lord, from Aniruddha to Vāsudeva, in verses he cites from works he calls the Vyāhṛtitattva and the Vyāhṛtisāra: भूरादिव्याहृतीभिस्तु वाच्यं मूर्तिचतुष्टयम्; mahaḥ is Vāsudeva — वासुदेवो महोनामा. The sixteen correspondences are sixteen forms of one Lord, and Māhācamasya, who knew them, is Brahmā: माहाचमस्यस्तज्ज्ञानी ब्रह्मा सम्परिकीर्तितः.

His verses on 1.6, cited from a Brahmasāra, place the same four forms at points from the heart to the crown of the head, and in fire, wind, sun and the four-faced Brahmā. Even the closing address to Prācīnayogya is read as Viṣṇu’s words to Brahmā, the ‘first fit one’: इत्युपास्व प्राथमिको योग्यस्त्वं मदुपासने. All of this is Madhva’s citation, reported as such.

The world in fives (1.7)

Traditional Interpretation

The seventh anuvāka lists fifteen things outside the body in three fives — worlds and quarters, fire and the other lights, and waters, plants, trees, space and ātman — and fifteen within it: the five breaths, the five faculties, and skin, flesh, sinew, bone and marrow. Then a seer is quoted: पाङ्क्तं वा इदं सर्वम् । पाङ्क्तेनैव पाङ्क्तं स्पृणोतीति — ‘all this is fivefold; by the fivefold one strengthens the fivefold’.

Śaṅkara explains ‘fivefold’ through the pankti metre of five feet and the sacrifice, which is also called pāṅkta — ‘पञ्चपदा पङ्क्तिः पाङ्क्तो यज्ञः’ इति श्रुतेः — so that the one who sees all as fivefold sees all as sacrifice. The inner five fill the outer and are known to be one with it: पाङ्क्तं बाह्यं स्पृणोति बलयति पूरयति एकात्मतयोपलभ्यत इत्येतत्. The commentators differ on the puzzling ātman in the outer list: Śaṅkara takes it as Virāj (आत्मेति विराट्), Madhva as the principle of ego (आत्मा अहङ्कारतत्त्वम्); Kūranārāyaṇa calls the whole a particular meditation taught as an auxiliary of knowledge: इदानीं ध्यानविशेषं ज्ञानाङ्गत्वेनोपदिशति.

Om (1.8)

Traditional Interpretation

The eighth anuvāka praises Om by its uses: it is the word of assent — ओमित्येतदनुकृतिः — the sāman singers begin with it, the adhvaryu gives his response with it (ओमित्यध्वर्युः प्रतिगरं प्रतिगृणाति), the brahman priest gives permission with it, and a brāhmaṇa about to recite says it, wishing to attain brahman: ओमिति ब्राह्मणः प्रवक्ष्यन्नाह ब्रह्मोपाप्नवानीति । ब्रह्मैवोपाप्नोति.

Śaṅkara makes Om a support for meditating on Brahman, as an image is for Viṣṇu — प्रतिमेव विष्णोः — and explains why a sound can be ‘all this’: what is named depends on its name, अभिधानतन्त्रं ह्यभिधेयम्. He leaves the last line open between two senses of brahman: the Veda the reciter wishes to learn (ब्रह्म वेदम् उपाप्नवानि), or the supreme Self (अथवा, ब्रह्म परमात्मानम्). Gopālānanda calls Om the support of the meditation on the supreme Brahman, परब्रह्मोपासनालम्बनभूतं प्रणवं स्तौति. Madhva, in verses he cites, makes Om a name of Viṣṇu: ॐनामा भगवान् विष्णुरधिकोच्चगुणत्वतः.

Where the readings divide

Comparative Vedānta

On the form of the teaching there is little disagreement: the vyāhṛtis, the person in the heart, the fives and Om are objects of meditation (upāsanā), and each has a stated result. The division is over what is meditated on. Mahaḥ is Hiraṇyagarbha, Brahman with the utterances for its body, for Śaṅkara; the supreme Brahman, with everything else as its body, for Kūranārāyaṇa; Vāsudeva, the highest of four forms, for Madhva.

The person in the heart is Brahman met in the heart for Śaṅkara, the individual self that departs to the Lord for Kūranārāyaṇa, and a form of the Lord residing there for Madhva. ‘Self-rule’ is lordship over the gods for Śaṅkara — and still short of liberation — but for Kūranārāyaṇa a state of the released self, free of karma and in the Lord’s service. The Sanskrit does not settle these questions; it says that mahaḥ is brahman and ātman and that the others are its limbs, and the readings divide on what ‘brahman’, ‘ātman’ and ‘limb’ mean.

Notes on the text

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

In the last line of 1.4 the base file reads pāhi, ‘protect’, but Śaṅkara’s gloss on the same text explains bhāhi, ‘shine’ — मा मां प्रति प्रभाहि प्रकाशयात्मानम् — and the Sāyaṇa-pāṭha and Madhva’s text read bhāhi. The edition reads प्र मा भाहि and logs the correction. Madhva’s text also reads a different line in the offerings, ‘dhātar ā yantu brahmacāriṇaḥ’, where the others have ‘dhātar āyantu sarvataḥ’.

In 1.7 the Sāyaṇa-pāṭha and Madhva’s text read avāntaradiśāḥ for avāntaradiśaḥ; in 1.8 they read anukṛti for the base’s anukṛtiḥ, which Śaṅkara glosses अनुकृतिः अनुकरणम्. These are listed and classified on the Sources page. The odd line ॐ शोमिति शस्त्राणि शंसन्ति is printed as all the witnesses have it; Gopālānanda explains that the hymns are begun with ‘om śam’.

Reading the five anuvākas today

Contemporary Reflection

A modern reader may be surprised that the same passage asks for intelligence, cattle, clothes and students, and then for a vision of the world as fivefold. Read as a reflection, not as the commentators’ meaning, it shows a teacher’s life whole: the capacity to understand, the means to live, people to teach, and a way of seeing that gathers the scattered parts of the world and the body into a few patterns. The tradition is unembarrassed by the request for prosperity; Śaṅkara’s own reason is that wealth serves knowledge only when intelligence comes first.

What does not carry over is the specific content — the offerings with svāhā, the channel through the crown, the four forms of Vāsudeva. These belong to particular practices and particular schools, and a page like this one can only describe them.

Open questions

  • Who was Māhācamasya? The anuvāka names him only as the one who made the fourth vyāhṛti known; Śaṅkara explains the name as ‘son of Mahācamasa’, and Madhva’s cited verses identify him with Brahmā. Nothing else in the materials of this edition identifies him.
  • Madhva cites verses from works he calls the Vyāhṛtitattva, Vyāhṛtisāra, Brahmasāra and Tattvasaṃhitā. They are reported as cited by him; the edition has no independent text of these works.
  • The line ‘om śom iti śastrāṇi śaṃsanti’ (1.8) is printed alike in every witness. How it is to be understood in recitation would need a printed Āraṇyaka with Sāyaṇa’s commentary.
