Study IX · Taittirīya Upaniṣad

The Taittirīya as one teaching

Anuvākas 1.1–3.10: instruction, knowledge and inquiry; the prayers that frame them; and the song with which the Upaniṣad ends, ‘I am food, I am the eater of food’

Anuvākas 1.1, 1.12, 2.1, 3.1, 3.10

The three vallīs of the Taittirīya can be read as separate lessons, but the commentators read them as one movement. The Śīkṣāvallī trains the student — sound, meditation, conduct; the Brahmānandavallī teaches Brahman and the five selves; the Bhṛguvallī shows a student reaching that teaching by his own inquiry, and closes with the knower singing ‘I am food, I am the eater of food’. Śaṅkara takes the whole as a ladder to the knowledge that ends action and its round; Kūranārāyaṇa as a single teaching of the Lord, confirmed at the end by the example of teacher and student. This study traces the frame of prayers, the joints between the vallīs, and the three readings of the final song.

Three vallīs, one text

Direct Textual Meaning

The Upaniṣad is the seventh, eighth and ninth chapters of the Taittirīya Āraṇyaka. The first vallī is named from its lesson on sound, शीक्षां व्याख्यास्यामः, ‘we shall explain pronunciation’; the second opens with its theme, ब्रह्मविदाप्नोति परम्, ‘the knower of Brahman reaches the highest’; the third with its scene, भृगुर्वै वारुणिः, ‘Bhṛgu, son of Varuṇa’.

The order is from the outside in. The first vallī deals with what a student does — how he recites, what he meditates on, how he lives; the second with what is to be known; the third with how a student comes to know it.

The joints between the vallīs: Śaṅkara

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara states the plan at each joint. Before the Upaniṣad, he says, the Veda taught rites; now the knowledge of Brahman is introduced to remove the cause of taking up rites: इदानीं कर्मोपादानहेतुपरिहाराय ब्रह्मविद्या प्रस्तूयते. The first vallī gave meditations that do not conflict with rites: संहितादिविषयाणि कर्मभिरविरुद्धान्युपासनान्युक्तानि.

At the head of the third he sums up: परिसमाप्ता च ब्रह्मविद्या — the knowledge of Brahman is complete — so what remains to be said is its means, tapas: अतः परं ब्रह्मविद्यासाधनं तपो वक्तव्यम्. At the very end he looks back over the two later vallīs as a commentary on one verse: the meaning of ‘satyaṃ jñānam’ has been explained इत्यस्या ऋचः अर्थः व्याख्यातः विस्तरेण तद्विवरणभूतया आनन्दवल्ल्या, and he closes: इति वल्लीद्वयविहिता उपनिषत् परमात्मज्ञानम्.

The same plan, read as one teaching of the Lord: Kūranārāyaṇa

Traditional Interpretation

Kūranārāyaṇa opens the Bhṛguvallī with a survey of everything before it. He even sorts the voices of the Śīkṣāvallī: from ‘śaṃ no mitraḥ’ on it is the teacher who speaks — इत्यारभ्य आचार्यवचनम् — and only two passages are the student’s own, ‘yaś chandasām’ and ‘ahaṃ vṛkṣasya’: इति द्वयमेव शिष्यवचनम्.

The Bhṛguvallī, for him, adds the vows that the earlier meditations had not given — व्रतानि सांहितीषु अनुक्तानि च उच्यन्ते — and confirms what the Brahmānandavallī had taught, by setting it in the form of a teacher instructing a student: आनन्दवल्लयां प्रस्तुतमेव सर्वं साक्षिवाक्येन गुरुशिष्योपदेशरूपेण द्रढयति. Knowledge received from a teacher is the knowledge that prospers.

The frame of prayers

Direct Textual Meaning

The first vallī begins and ends with the same prayer to Mitra, Varuṇa and the gods, with one change of tense: at the start ऋतं वदिष्यामि, ‘I shall speak what is right’, at the end ऋतमवादिषम्, ‘I have spoken what is right’, and तन्मामावीत्, ‘that has protected me’. The second and third vallīs are preceded by another prayer, for teacher and student together: सह नाववतु, ‘may it protect us both’.

Śaṅkara explains the change of prayer: the first was recited to remove obstacles to the knowledge already gained — अतीतविद्याप्राप्त्युपसर्गप्रशमनार्था शान्तिः पठिता — and the second to remove obstacles to the knowledge about to be taught: इदानीं तु वक्ष्यमाणब्रह्मविद्याप्राप्त्युपसर्गोपशमनार्था शान्तिः पठ्यते.

The closing song

Direct Textual Meaning

The Upaniṣad ends not with a statement but with a song. The knower, having passed beyond this world through the five selves, moves through the worlds eating as he wills and taking what form he wills, and एतत्साम गायन्नास्ते, ‘sits singing this sāman’. The song opens with the cry हा३वु, a sound of wonder, three times.

Then: अहमन्नमहमन्नमहमन्नम् — ‘I am food, I am food, I am food’ — and ‘I am the eater of food’, ‘I am the maker of the verse’, ‘I am the first-born of the right’, यो मा ददाति स इदेव मा३वाः — ‘whoever gives me, he has indeed protected me’ — and ‘I, food, eat the one who eats food’. It ends: अहं विश्वं भुवनमभ्यभवा३म्, ‘I have overcome the whole world’, and सुवर्न ज्योतीः, ‘like the sun, light’.

Who sings? Śaṅkara

Traditional Interpretation

For Śaṅkara the singer is the knower who has become the non-dual self: अद्वैत आत्मा निरञ्जनोऽपि सन् अहमेवान्नमन्नादश्च — though without stain, he is food and eater alike. The sāman is Brahman itself, ‘same’ in everything: समत्वाद्ब्रह्मैव साम. The threefold repetition expresses wonder: त्रिरुक्तिः विस्मयत्वख्यापनार्था.

He raises an objection — if the freed one becomes food, should one not fear liberation? — and answers that eater and eaten are a matter of ordinary dealing, not of reality: योऽयमन्नान्नादादिलक्षणः संव्यवहारः कार्यभूतः, स संव्यवहारमात्रमेव. The line ‘whoever gives me’ he reads of giving food to those who ask: यः कश्चित् मा माम् अन्नमन्नार्थिभ्यो ददाति.

Who sings? The Viśiṣṭādvaita commentators

Traditional Interpretation

For Kūranārāyaṇa the singer is the freed self, overwhelmed at finding itself enjoyed by the Lord: आत्मनः भगवद्भोग्यतां दृष्ट्वा तृप्त्यतिशयेन माद्यति. ‘Maker of the verse’ means he has become the Lord’s praiser — स्तोता संवृत्तः इति मन्यते — and ‘whoever gives me’ is his own teacher, who made him fit for the Lord and gave him to the Lord: यः अस्मदाचार्यः मां ज्ञानेन भगवद्योग्यं कृत्वा भगवते ददाति. Kauśika Govindarāja reads alike: ‘I am food’ means भगवद्भोग्यभूतोऽस्मि, ‘I am one to be enjoyed by the Lord’, and the verse-maker is a servant: श्लोककृत् पद्यकृत् दासभूतः इत्यर्थः.

Raṅgarāmānuja and Rāmānanda take another way. The word ‘I’ here reaches through the self to the supreme Self who is its inner ruler: अत्र अहं शब्दः परमात्मपर्यन्तः (Raṅgarāmānuja); अहं शब्दः परमात्मपर्यन्तार्थवाची (Rāmānanda). It is the Lord, as the self of the knower, who is food and eater and maker of the world’s wonders; ‘whoever gives me’ is the teacher who imparts this knowledge to a fit student: यो मां योग्याय शिष्याय ददाति उपदिशति.

Three readings of ‘I am food’

Comparative Vedānta

For Śaṅkara the ‘I’ is the knower who has found that he was always Brahman, and the song celebrates the end of every difference between eater and eaten. For Kūranārāyaṇa and Govindarāja the ‘I’ is the freed self, distinct from the Lord, and its joy is to be his — enjoyed by him and praising him. For Raṅgarāmānuja and Rāmānanda the ‘I’ points through the self to the Lord who is its inner self.

The three agree that the song expresses wonder and that it belongs to one who has completed the inquiry of the vallī; they disagree on who the ‘I’ is. Madhva’s digital commentary has no comment on the song, so no Dvaita reading of it is given here (see open questions).

A reflection: a school that ends in a song

Contemporary Reflection

The Taittirīya begins with how to pronounce a vowel and ends with a cry of wonder. Between the two lie a curriculum, a definition and a story. A modern reader can see in that arc a picture of learning in which discipline, understanding and one’s own inquiry are not stages to be left behind but parts of one life, and in which knowledge, when it comes, is greeted with astonishment rather than certainty.

This is a modern reflection on the shape of the text, not a reading any commentator gives.

Open questions

  • Madhva’s digital commentary ends at the meditations of 3.10 and has nothing on the closing song; whether a printed edition of his bhāṣya comments on it needs checking.
  • Whether the Śīkṣāvallī was composed as a preparation for the other two vallīs or joined to them later; the commentators read the three as one plan, and the edition does not go beyond them.
  • How ‘sitting singing this sāman’ relates to an actual chant of the Sāmaveda; the stobha syllables (hā u) are the commentators’ evidence that it is sung, but no melody is recorded in the witnesses.
