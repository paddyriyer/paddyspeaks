Study VII · Taittirīya Upaniṣad

Bhṛgu’s inquiry: tapas and the five definitions

Anuvākas 3.1–3.6: a son asks his father to teach him Brahman, is given a definition instead of an answer, and finds the answer himself, five times over

Anuvākas 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, 3.4, 3.5, 3.6

The Bhṛguvallī opens with a scene. Bhṛgu, son of Varuṇa, asks his father to teach him Brahman. Varuṇa names food, breath, sight, hearing, mind and speech, states a definition — that from which beings are born, by which they live and into which they return — and sends him away to inquire by tapas. Bhṛgu comes back five times, each time with a fuller answer: food, breath, mind, understanding and bliss. Śaṅkara reads the scene as a lesson in method: the father points instead of telling, and tapas is the concentration of the senses and the mind. Kūranārāyaṇa reads the same tapas as what turns the knowledge given in words into direct seeing; Madhva sees in each answer a name of Viṣṇu. This study follows the six anuvākas and the readings in the commentators’ own words.

The question, and an answer that is not one

Direct Textual Meaning

Bhṛgu goes to his father with the formal request of a student: अधीहि भगवो ब्रह्मेति — ‘Sir, teach me Brahman’. Varuṇa does not say what Brahman is. He names six things — अन्नं प्राणं चक्षुः श्रोत्रं मनो वाचमिति, ‘food, breath, eye, ear, mind, speech’ — and then gives a test that anything called Brahman must pass.

The test has three parts: यतो वा इमानि भूतानि जायन्ते, ‘that from which these beings are born’; येन जातानि जीवन्ति, ‘that by which, once born, they live’; यत्प्रयन्त्यभिसंविशन्ति, ‘that into which they go and enter’. Then: तद्विजिज्ञासस्व, ‘seek to know that’. The anuvāka ends with Bhṛgu’s response, not with an answer: स तपोऽतप्यत, ‘he performed tapas’.

Pointing, not telling: Śaṅkara

Traditional Interpretation

For Śaṅkara the six things Varuṇa names are not answers but openings. Food is the body, breath the one within it, and then the means of perceiving: चक्षुः श्रोत्रं मनो वाचम् इत्येतानि ब्रह्मोपलब्धौ द्वाराण्युक्तवान् — he named eye, ear, mind and speech as ‘doors to the apprehension of Brahman’. The three-part test is the definition proper: तदेतद्ब्रह्मणो लक्षणम्.

Why did the father not simply say what Brahman is? Śaṅkara answers that he deliberately left the teaching incomplete — सावशेषं हि तत्, ‘it was said with something left over’, साक्षाद्ब्रह्मणोऽनिर्देशात्, ‘because Brahman itself was not named’. Had he wished, he could have told his son ‘Brahman is this’; he did not, and Bhṛgu understood from the gap that another means was required.

That means is tapas, which Bhṛgu takes up without being told because it is, Śaṅkara says, the most effective of all means. What tapas is, he defines at once: तच्च तपो बाह्यान्तःकरणसमाधानम् — ‘that tapas is the collectedness of the outer and inner organs’. He supports this with a verse he cites as smṛti: मनसश्चेन्द्रियाणां च ह्यैकाग्र्यं परमं तपः, ‘the one-pointedness of the mind and the senses is the highest tapas’ (reported as cited by Śaṅkara).

Tapas as the way from words to seeing: Kūranārāyaṇa and Madhva

Traditional Interpretation

Kūranārāyaṇa reads tapas against the background of the whole Upaniṣad. What the earlier vallīs taught could be taught in words; what remains cannot. In his summary at the head of the Bhṛguvallī he says that the knowing which leads to the highest is वेदनमपि न वाक्यजन्यज्ञानमात्रं — ‘not merely knowledge produced by a sentence’ — but direct seeing reached by great tapas practised again and again. On the second anuvāka he puts it shortly: साक्षात्कारस्तु न वाक्येन शक्यते, ‘direct seeing is not possible by a sentence’; किन्तु तपसा एव, ‘only by tapas’.

He also reads Varuṇa’s ‘seek to know’ as an instruction to meditate: तद्विजिज्ञासस्व उपासनं कुरु. The instrumental of tapasā in the later anuvākas shows, for him, that tapas serves meditation: तृतीयश्रुत्या तपसः ध्यानाङ्गत्वम्.

Madhva’s verses give tapas a different centre: it is a form of knowledge. तपो ज्ञानस्वरूपत्वाद् — tapas is so called because it is of the nature of knowledge — and Bhṛgu came to know the Lord ज्ञानानुसन्धानरूपतपसा, by the tapas that is the steady pursuit of knowledge. What he came to know, step by step, was Viṣṇu under many names: क्रमाद् भृगुर्व्यजानात् तमन्नादिबहुरूपिणम्.

Five returns

Direct Textual Meaning

Anuvākas 2 to 6 repeat one pattern. Bhṛgu finds an answer that passes the test — अन्नं ब्रह्मेति व्यजानात्, ‘he knew that food is Brahman’, for from food beings are born, by food they live, into food they return — goes back to his father with the same request, and is sent away with the same words: तपसा ब्रह्म विजिज्ञासस्व, ‘seek to know Brahman by tapas’, and तपो ब्रह्मेति, ‘tapas is Brahman’.

The answers rise in order: प्राणो ब्रह्मेति व्यजानात्, breath; मनो ब्रह्मेति व्यजानात्, mind; विज्ञानं ब्रह्मेति व्यजानात्, understanding; and at the sixth anuvāka आनन्दो ब्रह्मेति व्यजानात्, bliss. Only then does the pattern break. Bhṛgu does not go back, Varuṇa does not speak, and the text itself names what has happened: सैषा भार्गवी वारुणी विद्या, ‘this is the knowledge of Bhṛgu and of Varuṇa’, परमे व्योमन् प्रतिष्ठिता, ‘established in the highest space’.

Why no answer short of bliss was enough

Traditional Interpretation

Each answer passed the test as Bhṛgu understood it, so why did he doubt it? For food, Śaṅkara gives the reason in three words: अन्नस्योत्पत्तिदर्शनात्, ‘because food is seen to come into being’ — what is itself born cannot be the source of all birth. The father’s repeated instruction is not mere repetition: तपसः पुनः पुनरुपदेशः साधनातिशयत्वावधारणार्थः, tapas is enjoined again and again to fix it as the supreme means; it remains the means for as long as the inquiry has not come to rest.

Kūranārāyaṇa gives Bhṛgu’s own objection to his first answer in the form of a reply to his father: त्वया उक्तमन्नं नानन्तं — ‘the food you spoke of is not infinite’ — nor the cause of every principle, nor bliss without limit. Each answer is measured against the full definition, and found partial.

‘Tapas is Brahman’

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The refrain तपो ब्रह्मेति is, read literally, an identity: ‘tapas is Brahman’. Neither commentator takes it so. Śaṅkara reads it with the instrumental beside it — तपसैव ब्रह्म विजिज्ञासस्वेत्यर्थः, ‘the meaning is: seek to know Brahman by tapas alone’. Kūranārāyaṇa: ब्रह्मसाक्षात्कारसाधनं तपः एव — tapas alone is the means to the direct seeing of Brahman, not the sentence.

Both, that is, take ‘is’ as ‘is the way to’, a common Sanskrit idiom in which a means is called by the name of its end. The grammar allows the stronger reading, and the text does not explain itself.

Bliss, set in the highest space

Comparative Vedānta

Śaṅkara reads the five answers as a movement inward: purified by tapas, Bhṛgu found the definition only partly met in breath and the rest, and so, शनैः शनैः अन्तरनुप्रविश्य, ‘entering inward little by little’, he came to know अन्तरतममानन्दं ब्रह्म, Brahman as the innermost bliss. The ‘highest space’ in which the knowledge is established is the space of the heart, and the bliss is non-dual: परमे आनन्दे अद्वैते प्रतिष्ठिता.

Kūranārāyaṇa reads the same words of the self made of bliss of vallī 2, which for him is the supreme person, and of his highest abode: एवं परमपदनिलये आनन्दमये विद्या पर्यवसिता — ‘thus the knowledge comes to rest in the one made of bliss, whose dwelling is the highest abode’. The definition’s test of birth, life and return is met only by the cause of all, and the cause, for him, is the Lord with his qualities.

The five answers and the five selves

Comparative Vedānta

The five answers are the five selves of the Brahmānandavallī in the same order: food, breath, mind, understanding, bliss. The Upaniṣad itself draws the two together at its close, where the knower passes through them one by one: एतमन्नमयमात्मानमुपसङ्क्रम्य, ‘passing on to this self made of food’, and so to एतमानन्दमयमात्मानमुपसङ्क्रम्य, the self made of bliss.

Śaṅkara says so in nearly the same words — अन्नमयादिक्रमेण आनन्दमयमात्मानमुपसङ्क्रम्य. Kūranārāyaṇa, introducing the vallī, makes the parallel explicit: the five meditations that follow, and the order in which the selves are reached at the end, are one order — प्राप्तिक्रमश्च तथैव उच्यते. For him each of the five is a real part of Brahman’s body: अतः अन्नमयादिरपि ब्रह्मांशः एव. Vallī 2 describes the selves from outside in; vallī 3 shows a person discovering them, one test at a time.

Are the things born real?

Comparative Vedānta

Varuṇa’s definition speaks of beings that are born, live and return. Kūranārāyaṇa draws a consequence: since their birth and destruction are spoken of, so is their reality — जन्मविनाशादिवचनात् सत्यत्वमपि भूतानाम् उक्तम् — for nothing is born or destroyed in a merely apparent snake seen in a garland: न हि मिथ्याभूतस्य स्रक्सर्पस्य तत्र जन्मविनाशौ.

Śaṅkara, at the end of the vallī, draws the opposite line: the round of eater and eaten belongs to what is produced, and not to the self — कार्यविषय एव भोज्यभोक्तृत्वकृतः संसारः — and is attributed to the self only by error. The same definition of Brahman as cause supports, in one school, the reality of the world that depends on it, and in the other, its dependence so complete that it has no being of its own. The text does not settle which.

A reflection: learning by being sent away

Contemporary Reflection

A modern reader may notice the shape of the teaching more than its doctrine. The father gives a question that can be checked rather than an answer that must be believed, and sends the student away five times. Each return is a small failure that is also a step. The answer is the student’s own when it comes, and the text marks the moment by having the father fall silent.

Whatever tapas meant in the forest schools, the scene suggests that some understanding cannot be handed over, only tested and grown. This is a modern reflection, not a claim about what the commentators intended.

Open questions

  • Whether Varuṇa’s six named things (food, breath, eye, ear, mind, speech) correspond one by one to the five answers that follow; Kūranārāyaṇa maps speech to the selves made of understanding and bliss, Śaṅkara treats all six as ‘doors’.
  • Whether tapas here meant bodily austerity as well as concentration; Śaṅkara and Kūranārāyaṇa both read it inwardly, and the text gives no detail.
  • Madhva’s digital text has no separate comment on 3.1; his verses on Bhṛgu’s inquiry are filed under 3.2, and whether a printed edition has more needs checking.
  • Whether Kūranārāyaṇa’s first-person objection (‘the food you spoke of is not infinite’) is his paraphrase of Bhṛgu’s doubt or a reading of the text’s ‘tad vijñāya’; the text gives no words for the doubt.
