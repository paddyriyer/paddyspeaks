Study IV · Taittirīya Upaniṣad

Satyaṃ jñānam anantaṃ brahma: defining what cannot be defined

Anuvāka 2.1: three words for Brahman, and what Śaṅkara, Kūranārāyaṇa and the other commentators take each of them to do

Anuvākas 2.1

The Brahmānandavallī opens with a sentence that names its subject — ‘the knower of Brahman reaches the highest’ — and a verse that says what Brahman is: satyam, jñānam, anantam, ‘truth, knowledge, the infinite’. Śaṅkara attaches to these three words one of the longest discussions in his commentary: they are a definition, not a description, and each word works by excluding what Brahman is not. Kūranārāyaṇa reads the same words as marking Brahman off, step by step, from matter and bound souls, from the freed, and from the eternal ones, while also naming its qualities. This study sets out the sentence, the grammar of the three words, and the two readings in the commentators’ own words, and notes what the digital text of Madhva’s commentary does not preserve here.

The sentence and its verse

Direct Textual Meaning

The vallī begins with a short prose statement and then quotes a verse in support of it. The statement: ब्रह्मविदाप्नोति परम् — ‘the knower of Brahman reaches the highest’. Then: तदेषाभ्युक्ता — ‘on that, this has been said’ — and the verse: सत्यं ज्ञानमनन्तं ब्रह्म, ‘Brahman is truth, knowledge, the infinite’; यो वेद निहितं गुहायां परमे व्योमन्, ‘whoever knows it, set down in the cave, in the highest space’; सोऽश्नुते सर्वान् कामान् सह, ‘he attains all desires together’, ब्रह्मणा विपश्चितेति, ‘with Brahman, the wise one’.

Nothing else in the Upaniṣad says what Brahman is in so few words. Everything that follows in the vallī — the emergence of space, wind, fire, water, earth, plants, food and the person, and the five selves from food to bliss — is presented as an explanation of how Brahman, so described, is ‘set down in the cave’.

A definition, not a description: Śaṅkara

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara states the function of the sentence at once: सत्यं ज्ञानमनन्तं ब्रह्म इति ब्रह्मणो लक्षणार्थं वाक्यम् — it is a sentence for defining (lakṣaṇa) Brahman. Grammatically the three words are qualifiers (viśeṣaṇa) of the thing qualified, Brahman: सत्यादीनि हि त्रीणि विशेषणार्थानि पदानि विशेष्यस्य ब्रह्मणः.

He then raises the obvious objection. A qualifier distinguishes one thing from others of its kind, as ‘blue’ distinguishes one lotus from red ones; but there is only one Brahman, so there is nothing for the qualifiers to distinguish it from. His answer turns on the difference between a qualifier and a definition: लक्षणार्थप्रधानानि विशेषणानि, न विशेषणप्रधानान्येव — these qualifiers serve chiefly to define. सजातीयेभ्य एव निवर्तकानि विशेषणानि विशेष्यस्य — a qualifier sets a thing apart only from things of the same kind; लक्षणं तु सर्वत एव — a definition sets it apart from everything, as when space is defined as what gives room.

For the same reason each word attaches to Brahman separately, not to the others: सत्यं ब्रह्म ज्ञानं ब्रह्म अनन्तं ब्रह्मेति — Brahman is truth, Brahman is knowledge, Brahman is infinite.

Satyam: what does not swerve from its nature

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara’s definition of satya is not ‘a true statement’ but ‘what holds to its form’: सत्यमिति यद्रूपेण यन्निश्चितं तद्रूपं न व्यभिचरति — that which does not depart from the form in which it has been ascertained. What does depart from it is unreal, so every modification is: अतो विकारोऽनृतम्. He supports this with the Chāndogya’s example of clay and the things made of it — मृत्तिकेत्येव सत्यम्, only ‘clay’ is real — and concludes that calling Brahman satya sets it apart from every modification.

The three words then follow one another as corrections. If Brahman is the real cause of modifications, it might be taken for a material cause, and so, like clay, as insentient: वस्तुत्वात् मृद्वत् अचिद्रूपता च प्राप्ता. That is why the next word is jñāna. Satya is also pure being — सन्मात्रत्वं च सत्यम् — which becomes part of his argument against taking jñāna as ‘a knower’: a knower, having the further property of knowing, would not be pure being.

Jñānam: knowing, not a knower

Traditional Interpretation

The second word is the centre of Śaṅkara’s discussion. ज्ञानं ज्ञप्तिः अवबोधः — jñāna is knowing, awareness; भावसाधनो ज्ञानशब्दः, the word is formed to name the act itself, न तु ज्ञानकर्तृ, not the one who knows. His reason is the company the word keeps: as a qualifier beside satya and ananta it must agree with them, and a knower cannot be either — ज्ञानकर्तृत्वेन हि विक्रियमाणं कथं सत्यं भवेत्, how could what changes by becoming the agent of knowing be satya? — nor can it be infinite, since knower, knowing and known would then be divided.

He meets the objection that ‘knowing’ is an act, and so would make Brahman impermanent and dependent on a knower. Brahman’s knowing is not a passing act but its own nature: आत्मनः स्वरूपं ज्ञप्तिः न ततो व्यतिरिच्यते, ‘knowing is the very nature of the self and is not separate from it’; अतो नित्यैव, ‘therefore it is eternal’. It is called an act only figuratively, in so far as the passing cognitions of the mind appear pervaded by it.

The word jñāna, he concludes, is used कर्तृत्वादिकारकनिवृत्त्यर्थं मृदादिवदचिद्रूपतानिवृत्त्यर्थं च प्रयुज्यते — to exclude agency and the other roles in an action, and to exclude the insentience of things like clay. It does not name Brahman directly: तस्मादेव च न ज्ञानशब्दवाच्यमपि तद्ब्रह्म.

Anantam: without limit of place, time or thing

Traditional Interpretation

The third word works differently from the first two. तत्र अनन्तशब्दः अन्तवत्त्वप्रतिषेधद्वारेण विशेषणम् — ananta qualifies by denying an end; सत्यज्ञानशब्दौ तु स्वार्थसमर्पणेनैव विशेषणे भवतः — satya and jñāna qualify by offering their own meaning. Of the infinite he says यद्धि न कुतश्चित्प्रविभज्यते, it is what is divided from nothing.

Later in the anuvāka he explains the three ways in which Brahman has no end. Space is unlimited in place but not in time, because it is produced; Brahman is not produced — अकार्यं च ब्रह्म — तस्मात्कालतोऽस्यानन्त्यम्, so it is endless in time too. And it is endless as a thing, सर्वानन्यत्वात्, because it is not other than anything, since it is the cause of everything: सर्ववस्तुकारणत्वात्.

If each word only excludes, does the sentence describe nothing — like ‘a barren woman’s son, armed with a hare’s-horn bow’? Śaṅkara’s answer is again the definition: शून्ये हि लक्ष्ये अनर्थकं लक्षणवचनम् — to define an empty thing would be pointless, so the sentence presupposes what it defines.

The grammar of the three words

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

satyam, jñānam and anantam are neuter nominatives standing in apposition with brahma; Śaṅkara notes that they share a case ending and so refer to one thing: एकविभक्त्यन्तानि पदानि समानाधिकरणानि. There is no verb; the sentence is a nominal identification.

jñāna is a noun in -ana from jñā, ‘to know’. The suffix (lyuṭ) forms an action noun in the neuter by P. 3.3.115 (ल्युट् च), which continues the sense ‘the bare action’ from P. 3.3.114 (नपुंसके भावे क्तः); the same suffix can also name the instrument or locus by P. 3.3.117 (करणाधिकरणयोश्च). Śaṅkara’s ‘formed to name the act itself’ is the first of these; a reading of jñāna as ‘that by which one knows’ would be the second. Neither formation gives an agent, ‘a knower’, which is why the grammar suits his argument.

ananta, ‘without end’, is a bahuvrīhi of the negative particle with anta, ‘end’. Kūranārāyaṇa raises a small grammatical question about it: if ‘ananta’ were a name of the Lord one might expect the masculine. His answer is that the neuter is governed by the verb ‘knows’ in the next line — यो वेद इत्यस्य कर्मतया अनन्तम् इति निर्देशात् — the words are the object of yo veda, ‘whoever knows’.

Three words, three exclusions: Kūranārāyaṇa

Traditional Interpretation

Kūranārāyaṇa agrees that the sentence states Brahman’s distinguishing nature — सकलेतरव्यावृत्तं स्वरूपमभिधीयते — but he assigns each word a different group of beings from which it sets Brahman apart.

satya: सत्यपदं निरुपाधिकसत्तायोगि ब्रह्म आह — Brahman’s being is unconditioned; तेन विकारास्पदम् अचेतनं तत्संसृष्टश्चेतनश्च व्यावृत्तः — this excludes insentient matter, which is subject to change, and the sentient soul bound up with it. jñāna: ज्ञानपदं नित्यासङ्कुचितज्ञानैकाकारम् आह — Brahman’s knowledge is never contracted; तेन कदाचित् सङ्कुचितज्ञानत्वेन मुक्ता व्यावृत्ताः — this excludes the freed souls, whose knowledge was once contracted. ananta: अनन्तपदं देशकालवस्तुपरिच्छेदरहितम् आह — without limit of place, time or thing; it excludes even the eternal ones, the sūris who ‘always see’ the highest abode, because their nature and qualities admit of degree.

For him the words therefore do two things at once: they set Brahman apart and they affirm its qualities. अतः सत्यादीनि पदानि कल्याणगुणप्रतिपादनपराण्येव निखिलहेयव्यावर्तकानि — they are aimed at stating auspicious qualities and exclude everything to be rejected. Brahman is infinite स्वरूपेण गुणैश्च, in its nature and in its qualities.

He also answers the reading of jñāna as ‘bare knowing’ directly: ननु ज्ञानमात्रं ब्रह्मेति किमिति नाभ्युपेयते — why not accept that Brahman is knowledge alone? प्रथमं तावत् ब्रह्मसूत्रलङ्घनभयात् — first, for fear of going against the Brahmasūtra, and he cites तद्गुणसारत्वात्तु तद्व्यपदेशः प्राज्ञवत् (reported here as cited by him). Knowledge is Brahman’s essential quality, so Brahman is called knowledge; it is also, he adds, a knower, as the word vipaścit, ‘the wise one’, in the same verse shows: ज्ञानपदस्य स्ववाक्ये एव विपश्चित्पदेन अनुभाषणं च निरपेक्षं प्रमाणम्.

The other Viśiṣṭādvaita comments

Traditional Interpretation

The modern Gopālānanda follows Kūranārāyaṇa closely: सत्यं निरुपाधिकसत्तायोगि, ज्ञानं नित्यासङ्कुचितज्ञानगुणसारत्वम्, एतेन मुक्तेभ्यो व्यावृत्तिः गम्यते, and ananta as free from limit of place, time or thing.

Rāmānanda’s Ānandabhāṣya (printed in the six-commentary edition as running notes marked ‘आ.भा.’) adds a point about satya: सत्यपदं स्वरूपतो गुणतश्च विकारराहित्यं बोधयति — it denies change both in Brahman’s nature and in its qualities. It reads jñāna, like Kūranārāyaṇa, as excluding the freed, and closes the three words with एवमेतैः सत्यज्ञानानन्तपदैः त्रिभिरपि ब्रह्मशब्दार्थः एव वर्णितो भवति — together they explain what the word brahman means.

Madhva’s comment

Traditional Interpretation

The digital text of Madhva’s commentary used by this edition preserves only the last verse of his comment on this anuvāka: स्वयोग्यं ब्रह्मविद् भुङ्क्ते विरिञ्चेन परेण च — the knower of Brahman enjoys what is fitting for him, with Viriñci (Brahmā) and with the Supreme. It reads ‘with Brahman’ (brahmaṇā saha) as the company in which the freed enjoy, each according to capacity, a theme Madhva develops at length in the measure of bliss (2.8).

His explanation of satyaṃ jñānam anantam itself is not in the file and is not reported here. His comment on the self made of food, filed in the source under the next anuvāka, names that self as Viṣṇu: स एष भगवान् विष्णुर्भूतस्रष्टा गुणाधिकः. That Madhva reads the Brahman of this verse as Viṣṇu with qualities is consistent with that comment, but his own words on the three terms would be needed to say how.

Set down in the cave, in the highest space

Comparative Vedānta

For Śaṅkara the ‘cave’ is the intellect, so called because knower, knowing and known are hidden in it: गुहा बुद्धिः. Brahman is ‘perceived there, set apart’ from the intellect, as one sees what the intellect is not. The ‘desires’ attained ‘together’ are attained at once, not one after another: सह युगपत्, in a single knowing identical with Brahman, the knowing called jñāna in the verse.

For the Viśiṣṭādvaita commentators the cave is the cave of the heart, where the Lord is present to the meditator: Gopālānanda glosses हृदयगुहायां निहितं यो वेद and takes ‘knows’ as ‘meditates’. The ‘desires’ are Brahman’s auspicious qualities, the things desired: Gopālānanda काम्यन्ते इति कल्याणगुणाः, Kūranārāyaṇa सर्वान् कामान् काम्यन्त इति कामाः. ‘Together with Brahman’ means that the freed enjoy those qualities and Brahman at the same time; Rāmānanda: समकालमेव अनुभवति.

Where the readings divide

Comparative Vedānta

Both readings take the three words as defining Brahman and as excluding. They divide over what the exclusion leaves. For Śaṅkara each word removes what Brahman is not and points to it without naming it; jñāna is knowing without a knower, and the definition is of a reality without distinctions. For Kūranārāyaṇa each word removes a class of beings and at the same time affirms a quality; jñāna is knowledge as Brahman’s essential attribute, possessed by a knower who is also called vipaścit.

The disagreement is not about the words but about the grammar of their use: Śaṅkara’s लक्षणं तु सर्वत एव against Kūranārāyaṇa’s कल्याणगुणप्रतिपादनपराण्येव. Each reading is defended from the same verse, and the verse itself does not settle it.

Reading the definition today

Contemporary Reflection

As a modern reflection, not a commentator’s reading: the sentence is a good example of how a definition can work by subtraction. We often define something by listing what it has; here each word is chosen as much for what it rules out — change, insentience, limit — as for what it says. Readers who meet the sentence in translation as ‘Brahman is truth, knowledge, infinity’ may hear three abstractions; the commentators hear three careful acts of exclusion, and argue about whether anything is left over to be described.

The tradition does not offer this as an exercise in logic. In the verse the definition is followed at once by ‘whoever knows it, set down in the cave’: the point of defining is that one may know.

Open questions

  • Madhva’s comment on 2.1 is missing from the digital text except for its final verse; his reading of satyam, jñānam and anantam needs a printed edition of his bhāṣya.
  • Whether parama vyoman, ‘the highest space’, is the unmanifest (Śaṅkara’s avyākṛta), the space in the heart, or the Lord’s supreme abode — the commentators’ readings differ and the verse allows each.
  • Whether ‘with Brahman, the wise one’ (brahmaṇā vipaścitā) qualifies the attaining (all desires at once, as Brahman) or the company of the attainer (together with Brahman); the readings follow from the school, not from the grammar.
