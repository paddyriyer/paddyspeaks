Taittirīya Upaniṣad

Special philosophical studies

The central interpretive problems of the Upaniṣad, each with the Sanskrit, the commentators in their own words, and the open questions.

  1. I. Śīkṣā: the science of sound and the saṃhitā meditations

    Anuvākas 1.2–1.3: why an Upaniṣad about Brahman begins with a lesson in pronunciation, and how the joining of two sounds becomes a way of looking at the world

    The second anuvāka names the six things a reciter of the Veda must learn and stops; the third takes the last of them, the joining of sounds (saṃhitā), and turns it into five meditations — on the worlds, the lights, learning, offspring and the body. This study sets out what the words say, how Śaṅkara, Kūranārāyaṇa and Madhva explain them, and why all three treat the passage as a meditation rather than a manual of phonetics.

  2. II. Bhūr bhuvaḥ suvaḥ, the fivefold and Om

    Anuvākas 1.4–1.8: a teacher’s prayer for intelligence and for students, the fourth vyāhṛti, the person in the heart, the world counted in fives, and the syllable Om

    Five anuvākas move from prayer to meditation. A teacher asks the ‘bull of the Vedas’ for intelligence and then for prosperity and pupils (1.4); a sage adds a fourth word, mahaḥ, to the three utterances bhūḥ, bhuvaḥ, suvaḥ, and calls it Brahman (1.5); the person within the heart and the way out of the body are described (1.6); the world and the body are counted in fives (1.7); and the syllable Om is praised through its uses in the Veda (1.8). This study sets out what the text says and how Śaṅkara, Kūranārāyaṇa and Madhva each read it — readings that agree on the form of the meditations and differ on what they are about.

  3. III. The teacher’s last instruction: satyaṃ vada, dharmaṃ cara

    Anuvākas 1.9–1.11: the duties that go with study, a seer’s declaration, and the address of a teacher to the pupil who is leaving

    The Śīkṣāvallī ends its teaching with conduct. 1.9 lists the duties of a householder, each to be kept ‘together with study and teaching’, and records three teachers’ views of what matters most. 1.10 is a seer’s declaration, Triśaṅku’s, which the commentators read three different ways. 1.11 is the teacher’s address to the departing student — speak the truth, practise dharma, honour mother, father, teacher and guest, give with faith, and in doubt follow the thoughtful. This study sets out the text, the commentators’ readings, and Śaṅkara’s long argument, attached to this anuvāka, about whether works or knowledge lead to the highest good.

  4. IV. Satyaṃ jñānam anantaṃ brahma: defining what cannot be defined

    Anuvāka 2.1: three words for Brahman, and what Śaṅkara, Kūranārāyaṇa and the other commentators take each of them to do

    The Brahmānandavallī opens with a sentence that names its subject — ‘the knower of Brahman reaches the highest’ — and a verse that says what Brahman is: satyam, jñānam, anantam, ‘truth, knowledge, the infinite’. Śaṅkara attaches to these three words one of the longest discussions in his commentary: they are a definition, not a description, and each word works by excluding what Brahman is not. Kūranārāyaṇa reads the same words as marking Brahman off, step by step, from matter and bound souls, from the freed, and from the eternal ones, while also naming its qualities. This study sets out the sentence, the grammar of the three words, and the two readings in the commentators’ own words, and notes what the digital text of Madhva’s commentary does not preserve here.

  5. V. The five selves: from food to bliss

    Anuvākas 2.1–2.5: the self made of food, of breath, of mind, of understanding and of bliss, each shaped like a bird, and the old dispute over the fifth

    After saying that Brahman is ‘set down in the cave’, the Upaniṣad describes how: from the self came space, wind, fire, water, earth, plants, food and the person; and within the person made of food it finds four more selves, made of breath, of mind, of understanding and of bliss, each ‘within’ the one before and each pictured as a bird with head, two wings, body and tail. Śaṅkara calls them sheaths (kośa) laid over the self by ignorance and takes even the self made of bliss to be a product, with Brahman as its ‘tail, the foundation’. Kūranārāyaṇa reads the self made of bliss as the supreme self, and Madhva sees Viṣṇu in each of the five. This study sets out the text, the figure of the bird, and the three readings of the fifth self in the commentators’ words.

  6. VI. Bliss, its measure and its limit

    Anuvākas 2.6–2.9: being and non-being, ‘he is flavour’, the ladder of bliss, and the bliss from which words turn back

    The second half of the Brahmānandavallī turns from the five selves to bliss. It begins with a warning about whether Brahman is or is not, and three questions a pupil asks; it tells how the self desired to be many and entered what it made; it says of that self ‘he is flavour’ and finds in it the ground of fear and fearlessness; it measures bliss on a ladder from a young, learned, strong and wealthy man up to Brahman, each step a hundredfold; and it closes with the verse ‘from which words turn back’ and with the knower whom neither good undone nor evil done can burn. This study sets out the text and the readings of Śaṅkara, Kūranārāyaṇa and Madhva in their own words.

  7. VII. Bhṛgu’s inquiry: tapas and the five definitions

    Anuvākas 3.1–3.6: a son asks his father to teach him Brahman, is given a definition instead of an answer, and finds the answer himself, five times over

    The Bhṛguvallī opens with a scene. Bhṛgu, son of Varuṇa, asks his father to teach him Brahman. Varuṇa names food, breath, sight, hearing, mind and speech, states a definition — that from which beings are born, by which they live and into which they return — and sends him away to inquire by tapas. Bhṛgu comes back five times, each time with a fuller answer: food, breath, mind, understanding and bliss. Śaṅkara reads the scene as a lesson in method: the father points instead of telling, and tapas is the concentration of the senses and the mind. Kūranārāyaṇa reads the same tapas as what turns the knowledge given in words into direct seeing; Madhva sees in each answer a name of Viṣṇu. This study follows the six anuvākas and the readings in the commentators’ own words.

  8. undefined. Food: the vows and the meditations

    Anuvākas 3.7–3.10: four vows about food for the one who has known Brahman, food and its eater, the guest at the door, and the meditations that close the Upaniṣad

    Having reached bliss, the Bhṛguvallī turns back to food. Four anuvākas each give a vow (vrata) — do not despise food, do not reject it, make much of it, turn no one away who comes for shelter — and each pairs a food with its eater: breath and body, water and light, earth and space. Then come meditations on Brahman in speech, breath, hands, rain and space, and the knower’s song. The commentators agree that the vows belong to the one who knows and that their purpose lies in what food has been in the inquiry; they differ on what ‘food’ finally is. This study sets out the text and their readings. It explains a text; it gives no advice about diet or health.

  9. undefined. The Taittirīya as one teaching

    Anuvākas 1.1–3.10: instruction, knowledge and inquiry; the prayers that frame them; and the song with which the Upaniṣad ends, ‘I am food, I am the eater of food’

    The three vallīs of the Taittirīya can be read as separate lessons, but the commentators read them as one movement. The Śīkṣāvallī trains the student — sound, meditation, conduct; the Brahmānandavallī teaches Brahman and the five selves; the Bhṛguvallī shows a student reaching that teaching by his own inquiry, and closes with the knower singing ‘I am food, I am the eater of food’. Śaṅkara takes the whole as a ladder to the knowledge that ends action and its round; Kūranārāyaṇa as a single teaching of the Lord, confirmed at the end by the example of teacher and student. This study traces the frame of prayers, the joints between the vallīs, and the three readings of the final song.
