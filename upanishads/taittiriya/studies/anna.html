Food: the vows and the meditations

Anuvākas 3.7–3.10: four vows about food for the one who has known Brahman, food and its eater, the guest at the door, and the meditations that close the Upaniṣad

Anuvākas 3.7, 3.8, 3.9, 3.10

Having reached bliss, the Bhṛguvallī turns back to food. Four anuvākas each give a vow (vrata) — do not despise food, do not reject it, make much of it, turn no one away who comes for shelter — and each pairs a food with its eater: breath and body, water and light, earth and space. Then come meditations on Brahman in speech, breath, hands, rain and space, and the knower’s song. The commentators agree that the vows belong to the one who knows and that their purpose lies in what food has been in the inquiry; they differ on what ‘food’ finally is. This study sets out the text and their readings. It explains a text; it gives no advice about diet or health.

Four vows and three pairs

Direct Textual Meaning

Each of the four anuvākas opens with a prohibition or a command and the words तद्व्रतम्, ‘that is the vow’: अन्नं न निन्द्यात्, ‘one should not despise food’; अन्नं न परिचक्षीत, ‘one should not reject food’; अन्नं बहु कुर्वीत, ‘one should make food plentiful’; and न कञ्चन वसतौ प्रत्याचक्षीत, ‘one should turn no one away from one’s dwelling’.

The first three then pair a food with an eater, each resting in the other: प्राणो वा अन्नम्, breath is food, शरीरमन्नादम्, the body its eater; आपो वा अन्नम्, water is food, ज्योतिरन्नादम्, light its eater; पृथिवी वा अन्नम्, earth is food, आकाशोऽन्नादः, space its eater. Each pair ends with the same sentence: तदेतदन्नमन्ने प्रतिष्ठितम्, ‘so food is established in food’.

Why the knower keeps them: Śaṅkara

Traditional Interpretation

Śaṅkara ties the vows to Bhṛgu’s inquiry. Food was the first door through which Brahman was known, and so it is to be honoured like a teacher: गुरुमिव अन्नं न निन्द्यात्. The vow is given in praise of food — व्रतोपदेशो अन्नस्तुतये — and food deserves praise because it was a means to the apprehension of Brahman: स्तुतिभाक्त्वं च अन्नस्य ब्रह्मोपलब्ध्युपायत्वात्.

The pairs teach that each thing is both food and eater by turns, since each rests in the other: तस्मात् प्राणः शरीरं च उभयमन्नमन्नादं च. The second vow he glosses simply as ‘should not refuse’, न परिहरेत्, and he reads the remaining pairs on the pattern of the first.

Food as one’s own self: Kūranārāyaṇa

Traditional Interpretation

For Kūranārāyaṇa the vows are rules binding on the knower — विदुषः वश्यानुपालनीयः नियमः. Because breath depends on food, body on breath, and the whole inner being of a person on food, to despise food is to despise one’s own self: अतः अन्ननिन्दकोऽध्यात्मनिन्दकः स्यात्. Not to reject food means not to refuse with contempt what is given: निन्दापूर्वकं न निषेधेत्.

Making food plentiful he reads as honouring it — बहुकरणं सम्माननम्. And the guest is not to be turned away whoever he is: वसतिम् आगतम् अन्नापेक्षिणम् आचण्डालं प्रत्याख्यानेन न विमुखं कुर्यात्. For one who sees the Lord as the self of all, a refusal would be a refusal of the Lord himself: भगवतः एव प्रत्याख्यानं कृतं स्यात्.

The Lord named food: Madhva

Traditional Interpretation

Madhva’s verses read every food and every eater as names of Viṣṇu, who stands in the form of both enjoyed and enjoyer by his own play: भोग्यभोक्तृस्वरूपेण तिष्ठति क्रीडया स्वयम्. Being enjoyed does not lessen him — न च भोग्यत्वमात्रेण हीनत्वं तस्य कुत्रचित् — any more than a husband is lessened by being cherished: न हि भार्योपभोग्यस्तु भर्ता हीनत्वमाप्नुयात्.

The vows then become attitudes towards him: अन्नाख्यं तं नैव निन्द्याद् बहु मन्येत तं सदा — ‘never despise him who is called food; always think much of him’. The vow about guests is stated as the knower’s vow: आगतं नेति न ब्रूयात् तदेतद् विदुषो व्रतम्.

The guest at the door

Traditional Interpretation

The fourth vow is followed by its reason. Śaṅkara explains: whoever comes for a place to stay must not be sent away — वसत्यर्थमागतं न निवारयेदित्यर्थः — and once shelter is given, food must be given too: वासे च दत्ते अवश्यं हि अशनं दातव्यम्. That is why one should gather much food by whatever means; the generous, he says, tell a guest the food is ready and never say there is none: न नास्तीति प्रत्याख्यानं कुर्वन्ति.

The text adds that food given ‘at the beginning’, ‘in the middle’ and ‘at the end’ comes back in the same way: एतद्वै मुखतोऽन्नं राद्धम्, मुखतोऽस्मा अन्नं राध्यते. Śaṅkara reads the three as stages of life or as degrees of courtesy — मुखतः पूर्वे वयसि मुख्यया वा वृत्त्या — and the lesson as plain reciprocity: it returns as it was given, यथादत्तमुपतिष्ठत इत्यर्थः.

Brahman in speech, breath and rain

Traditional Interpretation

The last anuvāka then lists ‘human’ and ‘divine’ meditations: क्षेम इति वाचि, Brahman as safekeeping in speech; कर्मेति हस्तयोः, as work in the hands; and so on to इति मानुषीः समाज्ञाः, ‘these are the human meditations’, अथ दैवीः, ‘now the divine’ — as satisfaction in rain, strength in lightning, light in the stars, the whole in space.

Śaṅkara takes each as a way of meditating on Brahman under one aspect: ब्रह्म वाचि क्षेमरूपेण प्रतिष्ठितमित्युपास्यम्, and in general तेन तेन आत्मना ब्रह्मैवोपास्यम्. What one meditates on, one becomes; he cites the principle as another śruti: तं यथा यथोपासते तदेव भवति. Madhva reads each name as the Lord’s own: क्षेमकृत्वात् क्षेमनामा वाचिस्थः स परो हरिः — Hari in speech is called safekeeping because he makes it safe.

Food and eater: what the pairing teaches

Comparative Vedānta

All three schools read the pairs of food and eater as teaching something about the self, and draw opposite conclusions. For Śaṅkara the whole round of eater and eaten belongs to what is produced, not to the self — कार्यविषय एव भोज्यभोक्तृत्वकृतः संसारः — and is ascribed to the self only by error: आत्मनि तु भ्रान्त्या उपचर्यते.

For Kūranārāyaṇa the dependence of breath, body and mind on food is real, and the Lord who has entered them is sustained in the body through it; despising food is despising the self and its inner ruler. For Madhva both food and eater are the Lord in play, and the vows are reverence for him under those names. The text says only that each rests in the other.

Words of the vows

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The vows are in the optative of rule: nindyāt, paricakṣīta, kurvīta, pratyācakṣīta — ‘one should (not) …’. paricakṣīta (pari + cakṣ, ‘look round at, reject’) Śaṅkara glosses as ‘should not avoid’, न परिहरेत्; bahu kurvīta, ‘should make much’, Kūranārāyaṇa takes as honouring rather than amassing, बहुकरणं सम्माननम्.

In the guest passage arādhi is an aorist passive of rādh, ‘to succeed, be ready’: ‘food has been made ready for him’. Śaṅkara glosses it अराधि संसिद्धम्.

A reflection: honouring what sustains

Contemporary Reflection

Read today, the vows have a direct ethical edge: do not disdain what sustains you, do not waste or refuse it, see that there is enough, and do not turn away whoever comes to your door. The commentators place them after knowledge, not before it: they are what the one who has understood does, not a method for understanding.

The edition offers them as an ethics of gratitude and hospitality, not as guidance about diet, fasting or health, on which the text says nothing. This is a modern reflection.

Open questions

  • Whether the three stages ‘at the beginning, in the middle, at the end’ mean periods of life or degrees of courtesy; Śaṅkara gives both.
