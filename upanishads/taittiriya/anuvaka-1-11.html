Taittirīya Upaniṣad · Śīkṣāvallī · Anuvāka 11 of 12

The teacher’s parting words: ‘Speak the truth; practise dharma’

At the end of study the teacher sends the pupil into life with a short code: truth, dharma, study, family, honour for parents, teachers and guests, generous giving, and the example of the wise when in doubt.

Having brought wealth dear to the teacher, do not cut off the thread of offspring. There is to be no negligence of truth. There is to be no negligence of dharma. There is to be no negligence of what is wholesome. There is to be no negligence of prosperity. There is to be no negligence of recitation and teaching.

1.11.2 There is to be no negligence of the rites for the gods and the ancestors. Be one whose god is the mother. Be one whose god is the father. Be one whose god is the teacher. Be one whose god is the guest. Those deeds which [are] blameless are to be practised, not the others. Those which [are] our good deeds are to be followed by you,

1.11.3 not the others. Whatever brāhmaṇas [are] better than us, for them relief is to be given by you with a seat. With faith [it] is to be given; without faith [it is] not to be given. With grace [it] is to be given. With modesty [it] is to be given. With awe [it] is to be given. With understanding [it] is to be given. 7 Explain it to a beginner

This is the most famous farewell speech in the Upaniṣads. When the student has finished learning, the teacher sends him into the world with a few plain rules. Tell the truth. Do what is right. Keep learning, and keep teaching. Look after your family and those who came before you. Treat your mother, your father, your teacher and any guest who comes to your door as if they were sacred. Copy only the good things your teachers did, not their mistakes. Give to others — with faith, generously, humbly, and with care. And when you are unsure what is right, watch people who are wise, gentle and fair, and do what they would do. The teacher ends by saying: this is the whole teaching. Live it.

An analogy Like the last words of a coach before a team leaves the training ground: no new technique, just the few things that matter most, said plainly enough to remember on the field.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the passage actually say?

After teaching the Veda, the teacher instructs the pupil: speak truth, practise dharma, do not neglect study; give the teacher his gift and continue the family line; do not neglect truth, dharma, welfare, prosperity, study and teaching, or the rites for gods and ancestors. Treat mother, father, teacher and guest as gods. Follow blameless deeds and the teachers’ good deeds — ‘not the others’. Honour those better than ‘us’. Give with faith (not without), with śrī, hrī, bhī and saṃvid. In doubt about a rite or conduct, or about people accused, act as discerning, devoted, gentle brāhmaṇas who love dharma would act. ‘This is the command … the counsel … the secret of the Veda … the instruction.’

Traditional Interpretation

Why does the teaching close with rules of conduct?

Śaṅkara reads the anuvāka as enjoining the rites of śruti and smṛti that are to be done before the knowledge of Brahman, because they purify: संस्कृतस्य हि विशुद्धसत्त्वस्य आत्मज्ञानमञ्जसैवोपजायते. From ‘having taught’ he also infers that a pupil should not leave the teacher’s house before inquiring into dharma: अतोऽवगम्यते अधीतवेदस्य धर्मजिज्ञासामकृत्वा गुरुकुलान्न समावर्तितव्यमिति. Kūranārāyaṇa: these duties, known from other sources too, are repeated after the Upaniṣad’s teaching for greater fruit (प्रमाणान्तरसिद्धानामपि सत्यवचनादीनाम् उपनिषद्विद्यानुवचनानन्तरं फलाधिक्यार्थमनुवचनम्).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Why ‘satyam vada’ and then again ‘satyān na pramaditavyam’?

Śaṅkara finds a difference in the word pramāda, ‘negligence’: the first forbids lying; the second forbids even an untruth spoken by inattention — प्रमादशब्दसामर्थ्याद्विस्मृत्याप्यनृतं न वक्तव्यमित्यर्थः, otherwise it would merely repeat the prohibition of falsehood. Gopālānanda puts the distinction as one between speaking truly and acting truly.

Traditional Interpretation

Must the pupil follow everything the teachers did?

No: ‘those which are our good deeds are to be followed by you — not the others’. Śaṅkara: not the contrary deeds, even if the teachers did them (नो इतराणि विपरीतान्याचार्यकृतान्यपि), and not blameworthy deeds even if the cultured do them (नो न कर्तव्यानि इतराणि सावद्यानि शिष्टकृतान्यपि). Gopālānanda gives the reason: even the cultured sometimes act badly when passion rises (शिष्टानामपि जातु रजसः समुन्मेषेण भवेदेव निन्दिताचरणम्).

Traditional Interpretation

Does the pupil leave at once for renunciation?

The instruction ‘do not cut off the thread of offspring’ is read by all the commentators as a direction to marry with the teacher’s leave. Śaṅkara: one should even make efforts by rites if a son is not born. Kūranārāyaṇa draws the conclusion that one who has acquired knowledge should not hurry into renunciation: त्वरितेन न प्रव्रज्या कार्या, सन्तानार्थं निवेशनं तावत् कर्तव्यम्. In his long discussion Śaṅkara nevertheless defends the other stages of life, holding that knowledge can arise before or without the householder’s rites.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What is ‘vedopaniṣat’, and why four names for the teaching?

upaniṣad here means the secret or inner sense of the Veda: एषा वेदोपनिषत् वेदरहस्यम्. Śaṅkara distinguishes ādeśa (injunction) from upadeśa (counsel, as from a father to his son) and calls anuśāsana the word of the Lord (एतदेव अनुशासनम् ईश्वरवचनम्). Kūranārāyaṇa compares it to a king’s decree not to be transgressed (अमोघफलं राजशासनवत् अनतिक्रमितव्यम्) and concludes: no other path is to be sought (नान्यः पन्था अन्वेषणीयः).

Comparative Vedānta

Do rites and knowledge together bring liberation?

Śaṅkara uses this anuvāka for his longest argument in the Śīkṣāvallī. He rejects every combination of knowledge with action as the cause of liberation, because what action produces is impermanent and liberation is eternal, and because knowledge, which dissolves the distinctions of agent and instrument, contradicts action: विरोधाच्च विद्याकर्मणोः समुच्चयानुपपत्तिः. Rites help by removing accumulated sin so that knowledge can arise (पूर्वोपचितदुरितक्षयद्वारेण विद्योत्पत्त्यर्थानि कर्माणीति); his conclusion: अतः केवलाया एव विद्यायाः परं श्रेय इति सिद्धम्. Gopālānanda grants that knowledge alone is the means to the highest good but holds that action contributes to its accomplishment (यद्यपि निःश्रेयसे विद्यैव साधनम्, अथापि तन्निष्पत्तौ कर्मणामस्त्युपयोगः), citing the Īśā’s ‘doing works here’. Rāmānanda requires meditation together with the rites that are its auxiliaries.

Contemporary Reflection

What does the teacher’s advice for doubt say about authority?

The instruction for hard cases is not ‘look it up’ but ‘look to good people’: those who think carefully, are committed, act on their own judgement, are not harsh, and want only what is right. The qualities listed are as much of temperament as of learning. It is an old answer to a modern question — how to act well where no rule settles the matter. This is a modern reflection.

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Śīkṣāvallī 11

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

The duties are to be done before the knowledge of Brahman, because they purify the mind and remove accumulated sin; knowledge alone is the cause of liberation, and action cannot be combined with it as a co-cause (the long discussion at the end of the anuvāka). Rāmānanda reads the instructions as the duties without which the knowledge of Brahman is obstructed (विद्याङ्गतयाऽवश्यकर्तव्याणि कर्माण्युपदिशति), and the closing words as meditation on the Supreme, to be practised together with them. Kūranārāyaṇa reads the anuvāka as instruction whose fruit is increased by coming after the Upaniṣad’s teaching, to be obeyed like a royal decree. Gopālānanda: knowledge alone is the means, but action helps it to arise.

Dvaita

No position is given for this school here: the edition does not force one.

Where they disagree

All read the anuvāka as binding instruction for the pupil entering household life. They differ on its relation to liberation: for Śaṅkara the duties purify and are then transcended by knowledge alone; for Kūranārāyaṇa, Rāmānanda and Gopālānanda they remain auxiliaries of meditative knowledge. Madhva’s bhāṣya, as imported, has no comment on this anuvāka; no Dvaita reading is given here.

    vowel sandhi (savarṇa-dīrgha)

    ā + ā → ā, P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः). The same letters also divide as tvayā āsane na (see ambiguities).

  • 1.11.3 अश्रद्धयादेयम् aśraddhayādeyam → अश्रद्धया + अदेयम्

  • 1.11.4 युक्ता आयुक्ताः yuktā āyuktāḥ → युक्ताः + आयुक्ताः

    visarga sandhi (āḥ before a vowel)

    After ā a visarga is dropped before a vowel, and the hiatus stays.

  • 1.11.4 अथाभ्याख्यातेषु athābhyākhyāteṣu → अथ + अभ्याख्यातेषु

    vowel sandhi (savarṇa-dīrgha)

    a + a → ā: P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः).

  • 1.11.4 एष आदेशः eṣa ādeśaḥ → एषः + आदेशः

    visarga sandhi (aḥ before a vowel other than a)

    aḥ before a vowel other than a loses its visarga and the hiatus stays.

  • 1.11.4 एवमु चैतदुपास्यम् evamu caitadupāsyam → एवम् + उ + च + एतत् + उपास्यम्

Morphology

1.11.1 प्रमदः
With the prohibitive mā the verb stands without the augment, P. 6.4.74 (न माङ्योगे): mā pramadaḥ, ‘do not be negligent’. Śaṅkara glosses प्रमादं मा कार्षीः.
1.11.1 व्यवच्छेत्सीः
s-aorist of chid with vi + ava, 2nd sg., without augment after mā (P. 6.4.74 (न माङ्योगे)): ‘do not cut off’.
1.11.1 प्रमदितव्यम्
Gerundive (-tavya) of pra + mad with the connecting i, used impersonally with na: ‘there is to be no neglecting’. The thing not to be neglected stands in the ablative, as with other verbs of turning away from (P. 1.4.24 (ध्रुवमपायेऽपादानम्) defines the ablative’s point of departure).
1.11.1 भूत्यै
Formally the dative of bhūti, ‘for prosperity’. Śaṅkara treats it like the surrounding ablatives — ‘from acts that lead to prosperity’ (भूत्यर्थान्मङ्गलयुक्तात्कर्मणः); the -ai form can also be read as the old ablative-genitive of a feminine in -i. Either way the sense is ‘do not neglect prosperity’.
1.11.1 अनूच्य
Absolutive of anu + vac with samprasāraṇa (vac → uc) and the lengthened prefix-vowel: ‘having recited after’, i.e. having taught the Veda by recitation, the pupil repeating.
1.11.1 आहृत्य
Absolutive in -ya of ā + hṛ; the short root-vowel takes t before the -ya, P. 6.1.71 (ह्रस्वस्य पिति कृति तुक्).
1.11.2 मातृदेवः
Not ‘mother-god’ but a bahuvrīhi describing the pupil: ‘be one whose god is the mother’. Śaṅkara: देवतावदुपास्या एते इत्यर्थः — they are to be honoured as deities.
1.11.2 नो
na + u, ‘and not’, ‘not indeed’. Ending in o, the particle is pragṛhya and stands unjoined before itarāṇi, P. 1.1.15 (ओत्).
1.11.2 उपास्यानि
Gerundive of upa + ās, ‘to be sat near, attended to’; here, of conduct, ‘to be followed’. Śaṅkara: तान्येव त्वया उपास्यानि अदृष्टार्थान्यनुष्ठेयानि.
1.11.3 श्रेयांसः
Nominative plural of the comparative śreyas, ‘better’, with the ablative asmat, ‘than us’.
1.11.3 प्रश्वसितव्यम्
Gerundive of pra + śvas, ‘to breathe out; to be relieved’. Śaṅkara’s first reading makes it causal in sense: their fatigue is to be relieved by you (तेषां श्रमस्त्वया अपनेतव्य इत्यर्थः).
1.11.3 कर्मविचिकित्सा
In both compounds (karma- and vṛtta-vicikitsā) the second member vicikitsā is a desiderative noun from cit (‘to perceive’), ‘wishing to discern’, hence doubt, hesitation.
1.11.3 स्यात्
Optative of as in a conditional clause with yadi: ‘if there should be’.
1.11.4 वर्तेथाः
Optative 2nd sg. ātmanepada of vṛt: ‘you should conduct yourself’. The optative here is an instruction.
1.11.4 अलूक्षाः
a- + lūkṣa, a variant of rūkṣa, ‘rough, harsh’ (Śaṅkara: अलूक्षाः अरूक्षाः अक्रूरमतयः).
1.11.4 अभ्याख्यातेषु
Locative plural of the past participle of abhi + ā + khyā, ‘spoken against’: people accused of a fault that is not yet established (Śaṅkara: दोषेण सन्दिह्यमानेन संयोजिताः).
1.11.4 उ
An emphatic particle: ‘thus indeed’. Śaṅkara takes the repetition as emphasis (इत्यादरार्थं पुनर्वचनम्).

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

A frame sentence (‘having taught the Veda, the teacher instructs the pupil’) followed by the instruction itself, in four registers. First, imperatives: vada, cara, and two prohibitives with mā. Second, a series of impersonal gerundives, ‘na pramaditavyam’ with an ablative, ‘there is to be no neglect of …’. Third, ‘X-devo bhava’ four times, then relative–correlative pairs (‘yāni … tāni …’) with gerundives, and six short sentences ‘[instrumental] deyam’ on giving. Fourth, a conditional (‘atha yadi … syāt’) whose answer is a long relative clause describing the wise and a comparison (‘yathā te … varteran, tathā … vartethāḥ’), repeated for the accused. Four nominal sentences name what has been said, and two gerundives close it.

Ambiguities

āsanena praśvasitavyam or āsane na praśvasitavyam?
The letters त्वयासनेन divide two ways. Śaṅkara gives both: ‘relieve their fatigue by offering a seat’, or ‘in their assembly, do not even breathe — only take in the essence of what they say’ (तेषु न प्रश्वसितव्यं प्रश्वासोऽपि न कर्तव्यः, केवलं तदुक्तसारग्राहिणा भवितव्यम्). Rāmānanda also gives the second (‘āsane is a locative, na a separate word’: आसने इति सप्तम्यन्तं पदम्, न इति पृथक्पदमव्ययम्). Madhva’s text prints ‘tvayāsane na’ — a spacing, not a different reading.
aśraddhayā adeyam or aśraddhayā deyam?
Śaṅkara, Kūranārāyaṇa, Rāmānanda and Gopālānanda read adeyam: what is given without faith should not be given (अश्रद्धया अदेयं न दातव्यम्). Madhva’s text prints ‘aśraddhayā deyam’ — ‘[even] without faith it is to be given’ — the same letters with different spacing; Madhva’s bhāṣya does not comment on this anuvāka, so his reading of it is unknown.
śriyā, hriyā, bhiyā, saṃvidā
Śaṅkara: with prosperity (according to one’s means), with modesty, with fear, with friendliness (संविदा च मैत्र्य्यादिकार्येण देयम्). Rāmānanda adds alternatives: śrī as a glad mind, hrī as embarrassment that so little is given, saṃvid as knowledge — ‘give with understanding’ (ज्ञानपूर्वकं देयम्). Gopālānanda: modesty, ‘not with the pride of “I am giving much”’ (न तु मया बहु दीयते इत्यभिमतिपूर्वकम्).
āyuktāḥ or ayuktāḥ
The base, the Sāyaṇa-pāṭha and Śaṅkara read āyuktāḥ, which Śaṅkara glosses a-para-prayuktāḥ, ‘not directed by others’ (अपरप्रयुक्ताः), i.e. acting on their own judgement; Rāmānanda glosses ‘mature in worldly matters’. Madhva’s text reads ayuktāḥ (corrections.json).
What does ‘evam upāsitavyam’ mean?
upās- can mean ‘to practise, observe’ or ‘to meditate on’. Śaṅkara takes it as ‘to be done’ (उपासितव्यं कर्तव्यम्); Rāmānanda takes the last sentence as the meditation on the Supreme Self, to be practised without abandoning the rites (तदङ्गभूतकर्मानुष्ठानम् अपरित्यज्यैव उपासना विधेया इति भावः).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Ethical leadership

Organisations write long codes of conduct; this one fits on a page and ends with a method rather than a rule. When you are unsure, find people who think carefully, are committed, act on their own judgement, are not harsh and want only what is right — and do as they would. Two details stand out for anyone who leads: the teacher tells the pupil not to copy the teacher’s own failings, and he asks that the accused be treated by the standard of fair-minded people, not of rumour. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

Further notes

  • Śaṅkara’s comment on this anuvāka contains a long discussion of whether knowledge and rites together lead to liberation, citing the Īśā (11), Chāndogya, Bṛhadāraṇyaka, Kaṭha and the Gītā; it is summarised above.
  • Madhva’s bhāṣya as imported (dvaitavedanta.in text) has no comment between the mūla of this anuvāka and the next; his text’s readings are recorded in corrections.json.

Uncertainties recorded

  • variant Madhva’s text reads ayuktāḥ for āyuktāḥ (twice); the Sāyaṇa-pāṭha differs only in spelling (ऱ, anusvāra, visarga written as s before a sibilant). See corrections.json.
  • interpretation Two word divisions are possible in tvayāsanena and aśraddhayādeyam; the padaccheda follows Śaṅkara (his first reading of tvayāsanena; his only reading of aśraddhayādeyam). Madhva’s printed spacing implies the other division of both.
