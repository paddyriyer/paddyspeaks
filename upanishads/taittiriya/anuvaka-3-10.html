Taittirīya Upaniṣad · Bhṛguvallī · Anuvāka 10 of 10

The vow of food, the meditations on Brahman, and the song of the one who knows

The Bhṛguvallī ends with a vow never to turn away a guest, a list of the names under which Brahman is to be meditated on in the body and in nature, and the knower who has passed through the five selves singing that he is food, the eater of food and the one who joins them.

7 Explain it to a beginner

The last section of the Upaniṣad comes back to food. First, a rule for daily life: never turn away anyone who needs a place to stay, and keep enough food to feed them. Food given generously comes back generously; food given grudgingly comes back the same way. Then the teacher lists ordinary things — speech, breath, hands, feet, rain, lightning, cattle, stars, space — and asks the student to see the same deep reality at work in each. Finally the Upaniṣad pictures someone who has understood everything it taught. That person is so full of wonder that they burst into song: “I am food! I am the one who eats! I am the one who brings them together!” The song is strange on purpose. It is meant to show a mind that no longer feels separate from the world it once tried to grasp.

An analogy It is like the end of a long apprenticeship in a kitchen: the cook who began by fetching ingredients ends by seeing the meal, the eaters and the cooking as one occasion — and laughs.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

Three things. A vow: never refuse anyone who comes for lodging, and so gather much food by any means; the knowers say to the guest, ‘food is ready for you’, and food returns to the giver in the manner it was given. A list of meditations: Brahman as keeping in speech, gaining-and-keeping in the breaths, action in the hands, movement in the feet, release in the organ of excretion; then in nature as satisfaction in rain, strength in lightning, fame in cattle, light in the stars, procreation, immortality and joy in the organ of generation, all in space; then as foundation, greatness, mind, homage, Brahman and ‘the dying-around of Brahman’, each with its fruit. And the knower: having passed through the five selves he moves through the worlds at will and sings the chant ‘I am food, I am the eater of food, I am the maker of the śloka; I am the first-born of the true order … I have overcome the whole world; my light is like the sun.’

Traditional Interpretation

Why hospitality here?

Śaṅkara links the vow to the meditation of the previous anuvāka, on earth and space as food and eater: one who meditates so must not turn a guest away (वसत्यर्थमागतं न निवारयेदित्यर्थः), and since lodging implies a meal, must have food. He reads the three pairs as the return of a gift in kind: given in the first part of life or with full respect, food comes back so (मुख्ये प्रथमे वयसि मुख्यया वा वृत्त्या). Kūranārāyaṇa extends the guest to anyone, ‘down to the outcaste’ (आचण्डालं), and grounds it in the knower’s seeing the Lord in every self.

Traditional Interpretation

What are the meditations, and what do they give?

Śaṅkara defines the first two terms — keeping is guarding what one has (क्षेमो नाम उपात्तपरिरक्षणम्), gaining is acquiring what one has not (योगः अनुपात्तस्योपादानम्) — and says that in each case it is Brahman that is to be meditated on under that form (ब्रह्म वाचि क्षेमरूपेण प्रतिष्ठितमित्युपास्यम्). The fruit follows the form of the meditation, as a śruti he cites says: तं यथा यथोपासते तदेव भवति. Raṅgarāmānuja names the same principle: तत्क्रतुन्यायेन प्रतिष्ठादिकं भवति इत्यर्थः.

Traditional Interpretation

The person and the sun are one: why repeat it here?

Śaṅkara uses the line to close a long argument that the self which entered creation is the supreme self and does not transmigrate: श्रुत्युपपत्तिभ्यां च सिद्धम् आत्मनोऽसंसारित्वम् , एकत्वाच्च. For Kūranārāyaṇa the person and the sun stand for the human and the divine, and ‘one’ means the one Lord over both (उभयत्र एकः एवेति). Rāmānanda names that one as Śrī Rāma (परमात्मानं श्रीरामम्).

Traditional Interpretation

What is the sāman, and why ‘hā3vu’?

Śaṅkara: Brahman itself is the sāman because it is the same in all (समत्वाद्ब्रह्मैव साम); the knower sings it to proclaim the oneness of the self and the fulfilment of his knowledge, and hā3vu is wonder, ‘aho!’ (अहो इत्येतस्मिन्नर्थेऽत्यन्तविस्मयख्यापनार्थम्). Rāmānanda agrees that the syllables mean ‘aho’ and that prolation and threefold repetition mark extreme wonder (प्लुतत्वं त्रिरुच्चारणं च विस्मयातिशयख्यापनार्थम्). Govindarāja has the freed soul sing it for as long as he remains (यावत्कालं तदेव साम पठति).

Traditional Interpretation

Is release something to fear, if the released one becomes food?

Śaṅkara raises the objection himself: then I would fear liberation, since even freed I would be food for another. His answer is that food and eater belong to the world of transaction, which the knower has gone beyond; there is no second thing to fear, so release is not to be feared (अतो न भेतव्यं मोक्षात्). The song praises the state of Brahman by speaking in the terms of that world.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

How do the words of the chant work?

Several words appear in their sung forms: annādo3, ṛtā3sya, nā3bhāyi (nābhiḥ), mā3vāḥ, ā3dmi, abhyabhavā3m. The padaccheda keeps the sung forms where the prolation changes a vowel, and gives the pausal form where it does not (annādaḥ). suvar na uses the Vedic comparative na, ‘like’, which Śaṅkara names (नकार उपमार्थे).

Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Bhṛguvallī 10

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

The knower passes beyond the five selves imagined by ignorance and attains the self that is real, knowledge, infinite, unborn, fearless and non-dual; he then moves through the worlds ‘as their self’, and his song is the joy of having nothing left to do. ‘I am food and the eater of food’ is said of the one self which is all: अद्वैत आत्मा निरञ्जनोऽपि सन् अहमेवान्नमन्नादश्च. The two vallīs together are the Upaniṣad, knowledge of the supreme self (इति वल्लीद्वयविहिता उपनिषत् परमात्मज्ञानम्).

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Kūranārāyaṇa; Raṅgarāmānuja; Bhāradvāja-Rāmānuja; Govindarāja; Rāmānanda; Gopālānanda (modern), commentaries on Bhṛguvallī 10 (six-commentary edition)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

The freed self reaches the self of bliss, the Lord, and remains distinct from him: Bhāradvāja draws the point from the gerunds and the participle — the released one stands apart from the supreme self, not having become one with it (परमात्मनः पृथक्त्वेन मुक्तपुरुषः स्थितः इत्युच्यते नत्त्वैक्यं प्राप्य). Its song is the wonder of the servant who finds himself the Lord’s delight: Kūranārāyaṇa, आत्मनः भगवद्भोग्यतां दृष्ट्वा तृप्त्यतिशयेन माद्यति; Govindarāja makes the śloka-maker the Lord’s servant (श्लोककृत् पद्यकृत् दासभूतः इत्यर्थः). Where the song says ‘I’, Raṅgarāmānuja reads it as reaching to the supreme self within the speaker (अत्र अहं शब्दः परमात्मपर्यन्तः). The released one has a shining body: Raṅgarāmānuja कमनीयदेदीप्यमानशरीरः भवति, Gopālānanda कमनीयप्रकाशमानदिव्यरूपः भवति.

Dvaita · Madhva, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Bhṛguvallī 10, in verse (the imported text ends before the knower’s song)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

The vow is the knower’s: one who has come for learning or lodging is not to be refused (आगतं नेति न ब्रूयात् तदेतद् विदुषो व्रतम्). The names in speech, breath, hands and so on are names of Hari dwelling there, each for the gift it gives (क्षेमकृत्वात् क्षेमनामा वाचिस्थः स परो हरिः); and the ‘dying-around’ is Viṣṇu as destroyer even of Brahmā (विरिञ्चमारकश्चासौ ब्रह्म पूर्णगुणत्वतः). The imported text of Madhva has nothing on the closing chant.

Where they disagree

All agree that the knower passes through the five selves and sings. They disagree on what he has become: for Śaṅkara the one self, so that ‘I am food and the eater’ states identity with all; for the Viśiṣṭādvaita commentators a freed self, distinct from the Lord, whose ‘I’ either reaches to the Lord within or declares itself his food and servant; for Madhva every name in the list is a name of Viṣṇu. They also read the last words differently: ‘a light like the sun’ (Śaṅkara, with the base text) or ‘golden light’ (Madhva’s text and the Viśiṣṭādvaita commentators).

Morphology

3.10.1 अराधि
Passive aorist (‘it has been made ready’) of rādh; Śaṅkara glosses it अराधि संसिद्धम्. The words are what the knowers say to the guest at the door.
3.10.1 मुखतः
The suffix -tas makes an adverb of mukha, ‘mouth, front’: ‘from the front’. Śaṅkara gives two senses together, ‘in the first part of life’ and ‘in the foremost manner’, and the same pair for madhyataḥ and antataḥ.
3.10.1 राध्यते
Present passive of rādh, ‘is accomplished, comes to’; it answers the participle rāddham of the line before: food given thus comes back thus.
3.10.2 समाज्ञाः
From sam-ā-jñā, ‘to know together, to designate’: names under which a thing is to be known. Śaṅkara takes them as meditations (उपासनानीत्यर्थः).
3.10.2 दैवीः
Vedic nominative plural feminine in -īḥ (classical daivyaḥ); Śaṅkara restates it as दैव्यः.
3.10.3 प्रतिष्ठावान्
Possessive -vat: ‘one who has a foundation’. The pattern holds through the list — meditate on it as X, become X-vat — which Śaṅkara grounds in a śruti he cites: तं यथा यथोपासते तदेव भवति.
3.10.3 मानवान्
Possessive -vat of māna (from man, ‘think’ or ‘honour’). Śaṅkara: मननसमर्थो भवति; Kūranārāyaṇa takes māna as esteem (‘mana’ as बहुमानः वा).
3.10.4 एणम्
enam with Vedic ṇ: Raṅgarāmānuja पर्येणम् इति णत्वं छान्दसम्. The preverb pari stands apart from its verb mriyante (tmesis) and is repeated in the next line.
3.10.4 परिमरः
From pari-mṛ, ‘to die around’: ‘that in which [things] die’. Śaṅkara identifies it as the wind, the five deities lightning, rain, moon, sun and fire dying into it, and the wind as one with space; Raṅgarāmānuja, as space, the place of all dying (परितः म्रियन्ते अस्मिन् इति परिमरः).
3.10.4 भ्रातृव्याः
Derived from bhrātṛ, ‘brother’: the rival among one’s own kin. Śaṅkara distinguishes those who hate from rivals who do not (अद्विषन्तोऽपि).
3.10.5 हा३वु
A stobha, a syllable of the sāman chant without lexical meaning; the 3 marks pluta. Śaṅkara: an exclamation of wonder, ‘aho!’. Kūranārāyaṇa derives it from ho with the pluti of P. 8.2.107 (एचोऽप्रगृह्यस्यादूराद्धूते पूर्वस्यार्धस्यादुत्तरस्येदुतौ), which splits the diphthong (that is his citation). Raṅgarāmānuja calls them stobha syllables (स्तोभाक्षराणि).
3.10.5 उपसङ्क्रम्य
Gerund of upa-sam-kram, repeated five times. Whether it means ‘passing beyond’ each self (Śaṅkara) or ‘coming near to’ the self of bliss as to another (Bhāradvāja, Kūranārāyaṇa) is the main question of this unit.
3.10.6 प्रथमजाः
jan + the suffix viṭ, whose nasal becomes ā: Kūranārāyaṇa and Govindarāja cite P. 3.2.67 (जनसनखनक्रमगमो विट्) and P. 6.4.41 (विड्वनोरनुनासिकस्यात्‌) (their citations). The nominative is masculine prathamajāḥ; the text has ‘prathamajā’ before the vowel of ṛtasya.
3.10.6 ऋता३स्य
ṛtasya with its second vowel prolated in the chant (ṛtā3sya); the word is the genitive of ṛta.
3.10.6 ना३भायि
The chant’s form of nābhiḥ, ‘navel’: Raṅgarāmānuja calls it a change made by singing (नाभा इ इति गानकृतः विकारः), and Rāmānanda the same (विकृतगानप्रयुक्ता). The padaccheda keeps the sung form; Śaṅkara glosses अमृतस्य नाभिः.
3.10.6 आवाः
A Vedic form without a classical equivalent. Śaṅkara: आवा अवतीत्यर्थः, ‘he protects [me]’, from av; Raṅgarāmānuja and Rāmānanda derive it from vā, ‘to go’ (‘he reaches me’); Govindarāja calls it a Vedic subjunctive (लेटि छान्दसं रूपम्).
3.10.6 आ३द्मि
admi, ‘I eat’, with its first vowel prolated in the chant.
3.10.6 अभ्यभवा३म्
abhyabhavam, imperfect first person of abhi-bhū, prolated at the end. Śaṅkara gives it a present sense (अभिभवामि); Kūranārāyaṇa reads a Vedic subjunctive with āṭ, ‘may I overcome’, citing P. 3.4.94 (लेटोऽडाटौ) (his citation).
3.10.6 ज्योतीः
jyotiḥ with a Vedic long ī.

Syntax

The anuvāka is prose in short clauses. Unit 1 is a prohibition in the optative (na … pratyācakṣīta), a statement (tad vratam), an injunction (prāpnuyāt) and a quoted saying (arādhy asmā annam iti), followed by three parallel pairs: ‘this food [given] mukhataḥ … mukhataḥ for him food comes’. Units 2–4 are a list of the form ‘X iti [locus in the locative]’, with upāsīta understood from later lines; from tat pratiṣṭhety upāsīta each meditation has its fruit: ‘as X meditate on that; one becomes X-vat’. Unit 4 ends with the identity statement sa yaś cāyaṃ puruṣe yaś cāsāv āditye sa ekaḥ. Unit 5 is one sentence: subject saḥ, five gerunds of passing (upasaṅkramya), a participle (anusañcaran), and the main verb āste with the participle gāyan; its object, etat sāma, is the chant that follows. Unit 6 is that chant, in the first person: nominal sentences (aham annam), a relative clause with its correlative (yo mā dadāti sa …), and the closing formula ya evaṃ veda, ity upaniṣat.

Ambiguities

suvar na jyotīḥ or suvarṇa jyotīḥ
The base text and the Sāyaṇa-pāṭha read सुवर्न ज्योतीः, which Śaṅkara explains as ‘a light like the sun’ (सुवः आदित्यः ; नकार उपमार्थे). Madhva’s text, and the Viśiṣṭādvaita commentators, read सुवर्णज्योतीः, one compound: Kūranārāyaṇa ‘like the light called suvarṇa, the supreme abode’ (सुवर्णशब्दवाच्यं परमपदं), Raṅgarāmānuja ‘with a lovely, shining body’ (कमनीयदेदीप्यमानशरीरः भवति). The letters differ only in n against ṇ (and the space); corrections.json classifies Madhva’s reading as a variant. This edition keeps the base reading and gives both.
Who is the ‘I’ of the song?
For Śaṅkara the knower who has become Brahman speaks as the one self of all (अद्वैत आत्मा निरञ्जनोऽपि सन् अहमेवान्नमन्नादश्च). For Raṅgarāmānuja and Rāmānanda ‘I’ reaches to the supreme self who is the inner self of the speaker (अत्र अहं शब्दः परमात्मपर्यन्तः). For Kūranārāyaṇa and Govindarāja the freed self speaks of itself as the Lord’s food and servant (अहमन्नं भगवतः भोग्यभूतः).
mukhataḥ, madhyataḥ, antataḥ
Śaṅkara gives two senses at once: the three periods of life, and the three manners of giving, from respectful to grudging. Gopālānanda (modern) takes them as the best, middling and least food (मुख्यमध्यमाधमभेदेन). Madhva reads the whole passage of the stage of life at which Brahman, called food, is realised, and what of him the freed soul then sees (मध्ये वयसि चेत् सिद्धं मुक्तो मध्यं प्रपश्यति).
yayā kayā ca vidhayā
‘By whatever means.’ Rāmānanda limits it to the means proper to one’s station (तत्तद्वर्णविहितवृत्तिषु अन्यतमया वृत्त्या); Kūranārāyaṇa’s gloss as printed reads ‘even by a forbidden means’ (प्रतिषिद्धेनापि), which may be an OCR slip for ‘not forbidden’ and should be checked against the book.
tat in the meditations
Śaṅkara: the Brahman just named; Govindarāja: space, from the preceding sarvam ity ākāśe (सर्वत्र तच्छब्दात् आकाशम् उच्यते). The parimara is the wind for Śaṅkara and Govindarāja (who cite a brāhmaṇa naming the five deities that die into it), and space for Raṅgarāmānuja.
yo mā dadāti sa id eva mā3vāḥ
Śaṅkara: who gives me, food, to those who need it, preserves me (यः कश्चित् मा माम् अन्नमन्नार्थिभ्यो ददाति). Raṅgarāmānuja and Rāmānanda: who teaches me to a fit pupil reaches me. Govindarāja: he who gave me [into being] has protected us both. The verb āvāḥ is read from av, ‘protect’, or from vā, ‘go’.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Wealth and consumption

The vow at the end of the Upaniṣad is practical before it is mystical: keep enough to share, and do not send away the person at the door. Its claim that food returns in the manner it was given — generously or grudgingly — reads as a rule about character more than about reward: what we give shapes the kind of person who receives. The closing song then reverses ownership altogether. The one who knows does not say ‘I have food’ but ‘I am food’, as if the boundary between consumer and consumed had dissolved. In an economy built on that boundary, the image is worth sitting with. (A modern reflection, not the commentators’ reading.)

Further notes

  • Śaṅkara’s comment includes, under 3.10.4, a long argument that the self which entered creation does not transmigrate; it is summarised above, not translated.
  • The six-commentary edition gives Raṅgarāmānuja, Govindarāja and Bhāradvāja on this anuvāka with their closing colophons; Govindarāja’s section also contains an editor’s footnote quoting Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara, and Bhāradvāja’s a footnote giving Śaṅkara’s reading suvar na jyotīḥ. Neither footnote is quoted here as the commentator’s.

Uncertainties recorded

  • variant suvar na jyotīḥ (base, S, Śaṅkara) against suvarṇa jyotīḥ (Madhva’s text; read so by Kūranārāyaṇa, Raṅgarāmānuja, Govindarāja, Rāmānanda and Gopālānanda). See ambiguities and corrections.json.
  • variant The Sāyaṇa-pāṭha writes the pluti of annādo3 as ‘ऽ३ओ’, keeps the hiatus devebhyo amṛtasya, and keys lokān as लोकन् (a slip); Madhva’s text writes nābhāi for nābhāyi and spells the anusvāra as ग्ं. All are spelling or notation (corrections.json).
  • uncertain The sung forms nā3bhāyi and mā3vāḥ, and the form āvāḥ itself, are explained differently by every commentator; the word table gives Śaṅkara’s reading and notes the others.
  • review Kūranārāyaṇa’s gloss on yayā kayā ca vidhayā reads प्रतिषिद्धेनापि, ‘even by a forbidden [means]’, in the OCR text; it may be a misreading and should be checked against the printed edition.
  • interpretation Whether the knower of unit 5 passes through the selves into identity (Śaṅkara) or comes near to the self of bliss as to the Lord, remaining distinct (the Viśiṣṭādvaita commentators), is the main question of the anuvāka; the edition presents both.
