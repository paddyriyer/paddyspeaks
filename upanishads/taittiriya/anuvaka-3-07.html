Taittirīya Upaniṣad · Bhṛguvallī · Anuvāka 7 of 10

The first vow: do not speak ill of food

After the teaching, a rule of life: since food was the first door to Brahman, the knower should not speak ill of it — and breath and body are seen as food and eater, each resting in the other.

5 Faithful English translation

One should not blame food. That [is] the vow. Breath indeed [is] food; the body [is] the eater of food. In breath the body is established; in the body breath is established. So this food is established in food. He who knows this food established in food is established. He becomes possessed of food, an eater of food. He becomes great — by offspring, by cattle, by the lustre of sacred learning; great by fame.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Do not speak ill of food: that is the vow. Breath is food, and the body eats it; the body rests on breath, and breath rests in the body. So food rests in food. Whoever knows that food rests in food stands firm, has food and enjoys it, and grows great in family, wealth, learning and good name.

Key concepts अन्न food प्राण breath, life-force उपासना worship, meditation ब्रह्मन् Brahman कोश sheath व्रत vow, observance

7 Explain it to a beginner

The story of Bhṛgu is over, and the text now gives four rules of life, one in each of the last anuvākas. The first is simple: do not speak badly of food. Why? Because food was the first answer Bhṛgu tried on the way to the truth, and a step on the way deserves respect, the way one respects a teacher. The text then looks at the body and the breath. Breath keeps the body alive, and the body houses the breath; each holds up the other. In the Upaniṣad’s language, each is both food and eater. Whoever sees this mutual support is promised a firm place in life and the blessings named before. The commentators read the rule as an expression of gratitude, not a rule about diet.

An analogy Like two people leaning back to back on a beach: each one stands only because the other does.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

Do not blame food; that is the vow. Breath is food and the body is the eater; the body rests on breath and breath rests in the body; so food rests in food. Whoever knows this is established, has food, eats it, and becomes great in offspring, cattle, learning and fame.

Traditional Interpretation

Why should food not be blamed?

Śaṅkara: because Brahman was known through food as a door, food is to be honoured like a teacher — तस्मात् गुरुमिव अन्नं न निन्द्यात्. The vow is given to praise food, and food deserves praise as the means to Brahman: स्तुतिभाक्त्वं च अन्नस्य ब्रह्मोपलब्ध्युपायत्वात्. Bhāradvāja-Rāmānuja repeats the comparison: अन्नं गुरुमिव निन्दां न कुर्यात्.

Traditional Interpretation

What does ‘blame’ cover?

Kūranārāyaṇa: neither saying nor thinking that the food is bad — एतदन्नम् अशोभनम् इत्यादि न वदेत् न च चिन्तयेत्. Govindarāja: not finding fault with it for faults in its preparation, संस्कारवैकल्यादिना न दूषयेत्. Gopālānanda (modern): not even food that is poor — यत् अन्नं समुपनतं तदपकृष्टमपि न निन्द्यात्. Rāmānanda makes it a lifelong resolve: अन्नमहं कदापि न निन्दिष्यामि इति सङ्कल्पं सदैव धारयेत्.

Traditional Interpretation

How can breath be food?

Śaṅkara states a rule: what is established within something is its food — यत् यस्यान्तः प्रतिष्ठितं भवति, and so the pair is both food and eater — तदेतत् उभयं शरीरं प्राणश्च अन्नमन्नादश्च. Kūranārāyaṇa adds that food itself becomes the breath of all — अन्नं हि सर्वेषां प्राणरूपेण परिणमते, citing the Chāndogya on the mind made of food.

Traditional Interpretation

How do Raṅgarāmānuja and Madhva place the vow?

Raṅgarāmānuja (Prakāśikā) calls it a vow that is a limb of the knowledge just taught: एतद्विद्याङ्गं व्रतमाह. Madhva’s verses, filed in his digital text under the following anuvākas, read ‘food’ as a name of Viṣṇu and give two vows together: अन्नाख्यं तं नैव निन्द्याद् बहु मन्येत तं सदा.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Bhṛguvallī 7

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

The vow praises food as the door through which Brahman was known; the identification of breath and body as food and eater is part of that praise, and the knower is established ‘as food and eater’ — प्रतितिष्ठति अन्नान्नादात्मनैव.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Kūranārāyaṇa; Raṅgarāmānuja; Kauśika Govindarāja; Rāmānanda; Gopālānanda (modern), commentaries on Bhṛguvallī 7 (six-commentary edition)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

A discipline belonging to the knowledge of Brahman (एतद्विद्याङ्गं व्रतमाह, Raṅgarāmānuja): the self in which the Lord dwells depends on food, so to blame food is to blame the self — अतः अन्ननिन्दकोऽध्यात्मनिन्दकः स्यात् (Kūranārāyaṇa). Rāmānanda and Gopālānanda also read a meditation on breath and body with its own fruit.

Dvaita · Madhva, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, on the Bhṛguvallī (verses filed under 3.8–3.9)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Food, breath and body are names of Viṣṇu, who indwells and moves the embodied — शराख्यदेहिनश्चैव शरीरं प्रेरणात् स्मृतः; being ‘eaten’ is no lessening of him, न च भोग्यत्वमात्रेण हीनत्वं तस्य कुत्रचित्. So the Lord named ‘food’ is never to be blamed.

Where they disagree

All take the vow as a rule for the knower. For Śaṅkara it praises food as the means to Brahman; for the Viśiṣṭādvaita commentators it protects the self that is the Lord’s body; for Madhva ‘food’ is the Lord himself.

Morphology

निन्द्यात्
Optative of nind: ‘one should not blame’ — the optative of injunction, as in the vows of 3.8 and 3.9.
अन्नादम्
Neuter, agreeing with śarīram: ‘the body is the eater of food’.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

An injunction in the optative with na (annaṃ na nindyāt), named as a vow by a short nominal sentence (tad vratam). Two nominal sentences identify food and eater (prāṇo vā annam, śarīram annādam), two more state their mutual support with participles in the locative construction, and a summary sentence (tad etad annam anne pratiṣṭhitam) leads to the relative-correlative sentence of the result and the refrain shared with 3.6–3.10.

Ambiguities

Why a vow about food here?
Śaṅkara: food was the door by which Brahman was known, so it is honoured like a teacher — अन्नेन द्वारभूतेन ब्रह्म विज्ञातं यस्मात्, तस्मात् गुरुमिव अन्नं न निन्द्यात् — and the vow is given to praise food. Kūranārāyaṇa: the whole person depends on food, so to blame food is to blame the self one meditates on — अतः अन्ननिन्दकोऽध्यात्मनिन्दकः स्यात्. Govindarāja: a rule for the knower of Brahman, and so all the more for others — किं पुनः अन्येषामिति भावः.
Which is food and which is eater?
The text says breath is food and the body its eater. Śaṅkara shows each is both, since each rests in the other: येनान्योन्यस्मिन्प्रतिष्ठितं तेनान्नम्, येनान्योन्यस्य प्रतिष्ठा तेनान्नादः. Rāmānanda takes vā as allowing the reverse view as well: वा शब्दो वैपरीत्यमपि बोधयति.
A vow of the knower, or a meditation for a result?
Śaṅkara treats the passage as praise of food within the knower’s discipline. Rāmānanda and Gopālānanda (modern) see in it also a meditation with its own fruit, long life in this world — स प्रतितिष्ठति लोके प्रतिष्ठावान् भवति (Rāmānanda).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Wealth and consumption

Complaining about food, and wasting it, can become a habit that says something about how we receive what comes to us. The vow asks for a different attitude: gratitude for the thing that keeps us alive, whatever its quality. It is not a rule about diet or health, and the commentators never treat it as one. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin When did you last complain about a meal? What would gratitude have looked like instead?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Śaṅkara says food is honoured ‘like a teacher’ because it was the first step to the truth. Should we honour the steps we have outgrown?

A contemplation exercise Before your next meal, pause for a moment and notice where the food came from and what it will do for you.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
