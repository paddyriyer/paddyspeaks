The name and its parts

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The Upaniṣad is named after the Taittirīya school of the Kṛṣṇa Yajurveda, whose Āraṇyaka it closes. Its three parts are called vallīs, ‘creepers’, and are named from their subjects or their opening: the Śīkṣāvallī from its lesson on śīkṣā (pronunciation) — शीक्षां व्याख्यास्यामः — the Brahmānandavallī from its teaching on Brahman and bliss, and the Bhṛguvallī from Bhṛgu, son of Varuṇa: भृगुर्वै वारुणिः.

The tradition also divides the Upaniṣads of this part of the Āraṇyaka by kind. Kauśika Govindarāja names three — साहित्यो याज्ञिक्यो वारुण्यश्चेति — and gives the twelve anuvākas from ‘śaṃ no mitraḥ’ as the Sāṃhitī: शं नो मित्र इत्यारभ्य द्वादशानुवाकाः. The Vāruṇī, beginning with ‘saha nāv avatu’, he describes as taught by the seer Varuṇa to his son; the Yājñikī, which he places in a further chapter of the Āraṇyaka, is not part of this Upaniṣad.

Why this Upaniṣad matters

Traditional Interpretation

The Taittirīya is one of the principal Upaniṣads on which Śaṅkara, Madhva and the Viśiṣṭādvaita commentators all wrote, and it holds two of the most cited sentences of Vedānta. The Brahmānandavallī opens ब्रह्मविदाप्नोति परम् — ‘the knower of Brahman reaches the highest’ — and defines Brahman in three words: सत्यं ज्ञानमनन्तं ब्रह्म.

It also moves, more visibly than most Upaniṣads, from practice to knowledge. Śaṅkara reads its order that way. The Śīkṣāvallī taught meditations not opposed to ritual — संहितादिविषयाणि कर्मभिरविरुद्धान्युपासनान्युक्तानि — and they do not uproot the seed of saṃsāra; the second vallī begins in order to remove the ignorance that is that seed. He concludes his long discussion on 1.11 with the thesis that governs his reading of the whole: अतः केवलाया एव विद्यायाः परं श्रेय इति सिद्धम्. The other schools read the same order differently, as the pages show.

A narrative introduction

Contemporary Reflection

The Upaniṣad begins in a school. Teacher and pupil pray together; the pupil is reminded how the Veda must be pronounced; the joining of two sounds becomes a way of looking at the worlds, the lights, learning, family and one’s own mouth. A teacher prays for intelligence and for students to come to him ‘as waters run downhill’. Three utterances recited every day are given a fourth, and the person within the heart is described. The world is counted in fives; Om is praised. Then the course ends: the teacher tells the departing student to speak the truth, practise dharma, honour mother, father, teacher and guest, give with faith, and in doubt follow those who think carefully and are not harsh.

The second vallī leaves the school for the question of what is ultimately real. It says that from the self came space, then wind, fire, water, earth, plants, food and the person, and it describes that person as a series of selves, each within the last — made of food, of breath, of mind, of understanding, of bliss. It measures bliss by stages, from a young and able man’s up to Brahman’s, and ends with a line on which much of later Vedānta rests: यतो वाचो निवर्तन्ते । अप्राप्य मनसा सह — ‘from which words turn back, with the mind, not reaching it’.

The third vallī tells a story. Bhṛgu goes to his father and asks अधीहि भगवो ब्रह्मेति — ‘teach me Brahman, sir’. Varuṇa does not define it; he says what Brahman does — that from which beings are born, by which they live, into which they enter — and sends him to find it by tapas. Five times he practises tapas, and he understands Brahman in turn as food, breath, mind, understanding and finally bliss: आनन्दो ब्रह्मेति व्यजानात्. The vallī ends with rules about food — अन्नं न परिचक्षीत, ‘do not disparage food’ — and a song of one who has understood.

The principal themes

Direct Textual Meaning

The discipline of learning: pronunciation, the joining of sounds, the student’s and the teacher’s prayers (1.1–1.4, 1.12). Meditations on the utterances, the person in the heart, the fivefold and Om (1.5–1.8). Duty and study; the teacher’s instruction (1.9–1.11). Brahman as truth, knowledge and the infinite; creation from the self; the five sheaths; the measure of bliss; fearlessness (2.1–2.9). Inquiry by tapas, and the knowledge of Brahman as bliss; food and its observances (3.1–3.10).

Witnesses and variants

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The base text is the mūla printed with Śaṅkara’s commentary (Advaita Sharada). It is collated with the Sāyaṇa-pāṭha of Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 7–9, which keeps the accents and is the source of the svara shown on each page, and with the text printed with Madhva’s commentary, which divides several anuvākas differently and is therefore collated vallī by vallī. Where the base file has a slip, it is corrected only on the evidence of the other witnesses or of the commentary it accompanies — in 1.4, for example, the base reads pāhi where Śaṅkara’s own gloss explains bhāhi (प्रभाहि प्रकाशयात्मानम्) — and every correction is logged.

Real variants are small, such as suvargeṇa or suvargyeṇa in 1.3 and anukṛtiḥ or anukṛti in 1.8. The peace verse ‘saha nāv avatu’, recited before the second and third vallīs, is printed apart from the numbered text. Every difference is listed and classified on the Sources page.

What you will learn

  • Read all three vallīs in Sanskrit with a transliteration you can trust, and know what each word is doing.
  • Explain why an Upaniṣad about Brahman opens with pronunciation, meditations on ‘joining’ and the utterances bhūr bhuvaḥ suvaḥ.
  • Say what the teacher’s address of 1.11 asks of a student, and how Śaṅkara and Kūranārāyaṇa differ on what those duties are for.
  • Explain what ‘satyaṃ jñānam anantaṃ brahma’ says, and why Śaṅkara, Kūranārāyaṇa and Madhva disagree over the fifth self, the one made of bliss.
  • Follow the movement of the whole Upaniṣad, from the discipline of a school to the teaching on Brahman and bliss.
  • Explain how Bhṛgu comes to know Brahman by tapas, and how Śaṅkara and the Viśiṣṭādvaita commentators read the closing song ‘I am food’.
  • Tell the ancient text from the modern voice explaining it, on any page.

A beginner’s reading guide

  1. Śīkṣāvallī 1 and 12: the frame The same prayer to Mitra, Varuṇa and the other gods opens and, in the past tense, closes the vallī.
  2. Śīkṣāvallī 2–8: sound, meditation, Om The lesson on pronunciation; the joining of sounds as five meditations; a teacher’s prayer; the four utterances and the person in the heart; the world in fives; Om.
  3. Śīkṣāvallī 9–11: conduct Duties with study and teaching; Triśaṅku’s declaration; the teacher’s address to the departing student.
  4. Brahmānandavallī 1–5: the five selves ‘The knower of Brahman reaches the highest’; Brahman as truth, knowledge and the infinite; from the self to food, and back inward through the selves made of food, breath, mind, understanding and bliss.
  5. Brahmānandavallī 6–7: being, creation, flavour Whether Brahman is or is not; the pupil’s questions; the self that desired to be many and entered what it made; ‘he is flavour’; fear and fearlessness.
  6. Brahmānandavallī 8–9: the measure of bliss From fear of him the wind blows; the ladder of bliss from a human being to Brahman; the verse ‘from which words turn back’; the knower whom neither good undone nor evil done can burn.
  7. Bhṛguvallī 1–6: the inquiry Bhṛgu asks his father Varuṇa to teach him Brahman. Varuṇa names food, breath, sight, hearing, mind and speech, gives a definition — that from which beings are born, by which they live, into which they return — and sends him to find it by tapas. Five times Bhṛgu returns: food, breath, mind, understanding, and at last bliss.
  8. Bhṛguvallī 7–10: food and the song Vows about food — do not despise it, do not reject it, make much of it, turn no one away from your house — and meditations on food and its eater; then the knower who passes through the five selves and sings ‘I am food, I am the eater of food’.

Special philosophical studies

  1. I. Śīkṣā: the science of sound and the saṃhitā meditations — Anuvākas 1.2–1.3: why an Upaniṣad about Brahman begins with a lesson in pronunciation, and how the joining of two sounds becomes a way of looking at the world (anuvākas 1.2, 1.3)
  2. II. Bhūr bhuvaḥ suvaḥ, the fivefold and Om — Anuvākas 1.4–1.8: a teacher’s prayer for intelligence and for students, the fourth vyāhṛti, the person in the heart, the world counted in fives, and the syllable Om (anuvākas 1.4, 1.5, 1.6, 1.7, 1.8)
  3. III. The teacher’s last instruction: satyaṃ vada, dharmaṃ cara — Anuvākas 1.9–1.11: the duties that go with study, a seer’s declaration, and the address of a teacher to the pupil who is leaving (anuvākas 1.9, 1.10, 1.11)
  4. IV. Satyaṃ jñānam anantaṃ brahma: defining what cannot be defined — Anuvāka 2.1: three words for Brahman, and what Śaṅkara, Kūranārāyaṇa and the other commentators take each of them to do (anuvākas 2.1)
  5. V. The five selves: from food to bliss — Anuvākas 2.1–2.5: the self made of food, of breath, of mind, of understanding and of bliss, each shaped like a bird, and the old dispute over the fifth (anuvākas 2.1, 2.2, 2.3, 2.4, 2.5)
  6. VI. Bliss, its measure and its limit — Anuvākas 2.6–2.9: being and non-being, ‘he is flavour’, the ladder of bliss, and the bliss from which words turn back (anuvākas 2.6, 2.7, 2.8, 2.9)
  7. VII. Bhṛgu’s inquiry: tapas and the five definitions — Anuvākas 3.1–3.6: a son asks his father to teach him Brahman, is given a definition instead of an answer, and finds the answer himself, five times over (anuvākas 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, 3.4, 3.5, 3.6)
  8. . Food: the vows and the meditations — Anuvākas 3.7–3.10: four vows about food for the one who has known Brahman, food and its eater, the guest at the door, and the meditations that close the Upaniṣad (anuvākas 3.7, 3.8, 3.9, 3.10)
  9. . The Taittirīya as one teaching — Anuvākas 1.1–3.10: instruction, knowledge and inquiry; the prayers that frame them; and the song with which the Upaniṣad ends, ‘I am food, I am the eater of food’ (anuvākas 1.1, 1.12, 2.1, 3.1, 3.10)
