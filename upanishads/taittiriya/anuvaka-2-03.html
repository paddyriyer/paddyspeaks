Taittirīya Upaniṣad · Brahmānandavallī · Anuvāka 3 of 9

Breath, the life of all, and the third self, made of mind

A verse praises breath as the life of every creature; then a third self, made of mind, appears inside the self of breath, and its limbs are the Vedas.

5 Faithful English translation

Following breath the gods breathe, [and] the humans and animals, whichever [there are]. For breath [is] the life of beings; therefore it is called the life of all. They reach the whole lifespan who meditate on breath as Brahman. For breath [is] the life of beings; therefore it is called the life of all. Of that [one], this indeed [is] the embodied self — [of that] which [is] the former. From that, indeed, from this [self] made of breath, [there is] another, inner self, made of mind. By it this [one] is filled. That, indeed, this [one] is of a person’s shape. Following that one’s person-form, this one is of a person’s shape. Of it the Yajus indeed [is] the head; the Ṛc the right wing; the Sāman the left wing; instruction the trunk; the Atharvāṅgirasa the tail, the support. On that too there is this verse.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

The gods breathe by breath, and so do humans and animals. Breath is the life of every being, so it is called the life of all. Those who revere breath as Brahman live out their full span. This self of breath is the embodied self of the one before it. Within the self of breath there is another self, made of mind, which fills it. It too has a person’s shape, following the shape of the self of breath: the Yajus is its head, the Ṛc its right side, the Sāman its left, instruction its body, the hymns of Atharvan and Aṅgiras its tail and support. There is a verse on this too.

7 Explain it to a beginner

This passage praises breath. The gods, people and animals all live by breathing; breath is the life of every creature, so it is called the life of all. Those who honour breath as the deepest reality, the text says, live out their full span. Then the text takes another step inward. Inside the self made of breath is a self made of mind, which fills it as breath fills the body. It too has a person’s shape, but its parts are surprising: its head, arms, body and tail are the different parts of the Veda — the sacrificial formulas, the verses, the chants, the instructions and the hymns of the Atharvaveda. The mind, on this picture, is shaped by what it learns and recites.

An analogy A singer who knows a song by heart can sing it silently; Śaṅkara argues that the Vedic mantras are the mind’s own activity, which is why they can be repeated silently in the mind.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the passage say?

A verse says that gods, humans and animals live by breath; that breath is the life of beings and is called the life of all; and that those who meditate on breath as Brahman reach their full span. It says that this is the embodied self of the former. Prose names a third self, made of mind, inside the self of breath, and makes the Yajus, Ṛc, Sāman, instruction and the Atharvāṅgirasa its head, wings, trunk and tail.

Traditional Interpretation

What does it mean that breath is the ‘life of all’?

Śaṅkara: beings are not selves only through the body; they have a self of breath common to all, pervading the whole body, and through it a self of mind and the rest, and finally the one Self beyond the five sheaths — स हि परमार्थत आत्मा सर्वेषामित्येतदप्यर्थादुक्तं भवति. The fruit of meditating on breath as Brahman is the full lifespan, not dying before one’s time (नापमृत्युना म्रियन्ते प्राक्प्राप्तादायुष इत्यर्थः).

Traditional Interpretation

Why are the Vedas the limbs of the mind?

Śaṅkara: yajus is the head because the offering is made with Yajus formulas. But the deeper point is that the mantras, as known and recited, are activities of the mind: so the mind can repeat them silently (मानसो जप उपपद्यते), and the Vedas can be eternal, being the consciousness of the Self conditioned by the mind’s activity. ādeśa is the Brāhmaṇa, which ‘enjoins what is to be enjoined’; the Atharvāṅgirasa is the tail because its rites of peace and prosperity give stability.

Traditional Interpretation

How do the Viśiṣṭādvaita commentators read the passage?

Kūranārāyaṇa reads ‘the embodied self of the former’ as the supreme Self, already named in 2.1, which is also the self of the self of breath; the sheaths are its body. Rāmānanda takes the limbs as the mind’s activities that produce knowledge of each Veda, and reminds the reader that the five sheaths are imagined only to point to the supreme Self, as one shows the star Arundhatī by way of a brighter one (स्थूलारुन्धतीन्यायेन परशब्दवाच्यपरमात्मानमेव ग्राहयितुं पञ्चकोशकल्पना). Gopālānanda (a modern commentator) agrees: यद्यपि यजुरादि वाग्रूपम्, अथापि तत्तद्विषयकज्ञानजनकमनोव्यापार इह यजुरादिशब्देन विवक्षितः.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What do the forms tell us?

sarvāyuṣa is sarvāyus with the compound-final a: Śaṅkara, सर्वेषामायुः सर्वायुः, सर्वायुरेव सर्वायुषम्. śārīra is a taddhita of śarīra, ‘in the body’. ṛc and sāman stand in their nominative forms ṛk and sāma.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Brahmavallī 3

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

The selves of breath and mind are, like the body, imagined through ignorance, each the self of the one outside it. Through them the text shows that beings have a self beyond the body, and finally the one Self beyond all five sheaths. The mantras that make up the self of mind are the Self’s consciousness conditioned by mental activity: मनोवृत्त्युपाधिपरिच्छिन्नं मनोवृत्तिनिष्ठमात्मचैतन्यमनादिनिधनं यजुःशब्दवाच्यम्.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Kūranārāyaṇa; Rāmānanda, commentaries on Ānandavallī 3 (six-commentary edition)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

The supreme Self, the cause of space and the rest, is the self of every sheath, which is its body: अतः तस्माद्वा एतस्मादित्यत्रैव अन्नमयादि आनन्दमयपर्यन्तानां परमात्मा एव आत्मा इत्यवगम्यते. ‘The embodied self of the former’ is that same Self. Rāmānanda: the sheaths are a graded way of pointing to it.

Dvaita · Madhva, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya (printed under Ānandavallī 4)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

In the self of mind Saṅkarṣaṇa abides (तस्मिन् सङ्कर्षणः स्थितः), and the Veda-limbs are his: the head is called Yajus because he takes the sacrifices (शिरस्तस्य यजुर्नाम यज्ञानां हरणात् स्मृतम्); the trunk, ‘instruction’, is the Pañcarātra (पञ्चरात्रगतं मध्यमादेशाख्यं सुविस्तृतेः). This comment is printed at the head of Madhva’s note on the next anuvāka in the digital text, after a gap.

Where they disagree

The decisive difference is the half-line yaḥ pūrvasya: for Śaṅkara the inner sheath is the self of the outer sheath; for Kūranārāyaṇa the supreme Self is the self of every sheath. They also differ on ‘instruction’: the Brāhmaṇa (Śaṅkara), injunction and prohibition (Rāmānanda), the Pañcarātra (Madhva).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • प्राणं देवा अनु prāṇaṃ devā anu → प्राणम् + देवाः + अनु

    final m; āḥ before a vowel

    prāṇam → prāṇaṃ before d (P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः)); devāḥ before a loses its visarga and the hiatus stays (P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि), P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य)).

  • पशवश्च paśavaśca → पशवः + च

    visarga before c

    The visarga becomes ś before c.

  • प्राणो हि prāṇo hi → प्राणः + हि

    aḥ before a voiced consonant

    P. 6.1.114 (हशि च): visarga → u, a + u → o.

  • भूतानामायुः bhūtānāmāyuḥ → भूतानाम् + आयुः

    final m before a vowel

    m stays m before a vowel (no anusvāra).

  • त आयुर्यन्ति ta āyuryanti → ते + आयुः + यन्ति

    e before a vowel; visarga as r

    te + ā: e becomes ay (P. 6.1.78 (एचोऽयवायावः)) and the y drops (P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य)), leaving ta āyuḥ; the visarga after u becomes r before the voiced y.

  • तस्यैष एव शारीर आत्मा tasyaiṣa eva śārīra ātmā → तस्य + एषः + एव + शारीरः + आत्मा

    vṛddhi; visarga before vowels

    tasya + e → ai; eṣaḥ before e and śārīraḥ before ā lose the visarga and keep the hiatus (P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि), P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य)).

  • यजुरेव yajureva → यजुः + एव

    visarga as r

    yajuḥ (stem yajus): before a vowel the final s appears as r.

  • ऋग्दक्षिणः ṛgdakṣiṇaḥ → ऋक् + दक्षिणः

    consonant sandhi

    k → g before the voiced d: P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते).

  • सामोत्तरः sāmottaraḥ → साम + उत्तरः

    vowel sandhi (guṇa)

    a + u → o: P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः).

  • आदेश आत्मा ādeśa ātmā → आदेशः + आत्मा

    aḥ before ā

    Visarga lost before a vowel other than a, hiatus kept: P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि), P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य).

Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • सर्वायुषम् sarvāyuṣam

    tatpuruṣa (with the samāsānta a) · सर्व + आयुस्

    Vigraha सर्वेषाम् आयुः सर्वायुः, सर्वायुरेव सर्वायुषम्

    the life of all. Śaṅkara analyses it सर्वेषामायुः सर्वायुः, सर्वायुरेव सर्वायुषम्.

  • मनोमयः manomayaḥ

    taddhita (mayaṭ) · मनस् + मय

    Vigraha मनः प्रचुरम् अस्मिन् इति मनोमयः

    made of mind, abounding in mind; Kūranārāyaṇa: अत्र प्राचुर्यार्थो मयट्.

  • अथर्वाङ्गिरसः atharvāṅgirasaḥ

    dvandva, used of the mantras they saw · अथर्वन् + अङ्गिरस्

    Vigraha अथर्वा च अङ्गिराः च अथर्वाङ्गिरसौ, तैः दृष्टाः मन्त्राः अथर्वाङ्गिरसः

    the mantras of Atharvan and Aṅgiras, i.e. of the Atharvaveda. Rāmānanda: अथर्वा नाम कश्चिदृषिः, अङ्गिराश्च ताभ्यां दृष्टा मन्त्रा अथर्वाङ्गिरसः.

  • पुरुषविधः puruṣavidhaḥ

    bahuvrīhi · पुरुष + विधा

    Vigraha पुरुषस्य विधा इव विधा यस्य सः पुरुषविधः

    having a person’s shape (as in 2.2).

Morphology

प्राणन्ति
pra + an, ‘to breathe’; the n of the preverb’s ending conditions the cerebral ṇ. With anu, ‘breathe after, live by following’.
शारीरः
Taddhita from śarīra with vṛddhi of the first vowel: ‘belonging to, being in, the body’. Śaṅkara: शरीरे अन्नमये भवः शारीरः.
आयुः
Neuter s-stem āyus, ‘life, length of life’; nominative and accusative alike.
ऋक्
Nominative of the feminine c-stem ṛc, ‘verse’; final c becomes k in pause.
साम
Nominative of the neuter n-stem sāman, ‘chant’; the n drops at the end of the word.
एषः
The pronoun eṣaḥ (pausal form). After tena it fuses (tena + eṣaḥ → tenaiṣa) and loses its visarga before the consonant of pūrṇaḥ, P. 6.1.132 (एतत्तदोः सुलोपोऽकोरनञ्समासे हलि).

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

As in 2.2: a verse (lines 1–8), then the fixed prose pattern. The verse has a main clause (prāṇaṃ devā anu prāṇanti) whose subject is extended by a relative (manuṣyāḥ paśavaś ca ye), reason and result clauses with hi and tasmāt, and a relative-correlative sentence (sarvam eva ta āyur yanti ye prāṇaṃ brahmopāsate). Lines 9–10 are a short nominal sentence with a relative clause (tasyaiṣa eva śārīra ātmā yaḥ pūrvasya) whose reference is disputed. The prose then gives the self of mind and its five limbs, each a nominal sentence.

Ambiguities

tasyaiṣa eva śārīra ātmā yaḥ pūrvasya
Two quite different readings. Śaṅkara: ‘of that former one (the self of food), this one (the self of breath) is the embodied self’ — तस्य पूर्वस्य अन्नमयस्य एष एव शरीरे अन्नमये भवः शारीरः आत्मा; each inner self is the self of the outer one. Kūranārāyaṇa: the self of the former, the supreme Self named in 2.1 (‘from that Self’), is also the embodied self of the self of breath — स एव प्राणमयस्यापि शारीरः आत्मा. Gopālānanda takes śārīra as ‘the Self whose body all this is’ (यस्य इदं सर्वं शरीरं स शारीर आत्मा), and Rāmānanda likewise moves the sense of ‘inner self’ beyond the self of breath. The words allow both.
devāḥ
Śaṅkara gives two senses: the gods such as Agni, or, in this inward context, the senses (अध्यात्माधिकारात् देवाः इन्द्रियाणि प्राणमनु प्राणन्ति). Rāmānanda: the gods Indra, Brahmā and the rest.
The Vedas as limbs of the self of mind
All the commentators take yajus, ṛc and sāman here not as words on a page but as the mind’s activity that has them as content (Rāmānanda: यजुर्वेदार्थविषयकज्ञानोत्पादकमनोव्यापार एव). They differ on ādeśa: the Brāhmaṇa portion of the Veda (Śaṅkara: आदेशः अत्र ब्राह्मणम्), injunction and prohibition (Rāmānanda: आदेशो विधिनिषेधरूपः), or the teaching and its rules (Kūranārāyaṇa).

Metre

mixed · syllables counted as written: 9 + 8 + 8 + 9 + 9 + 8 + 8 + 11 + 10 + 4 + 10 + 10 + 5 + 11 + 8 + 8 + 8 + 6 + 6 + 5 + 11 + 9

Lines 1–8 are the śloka announced at the end of 2.2, in short lines of about eight syllables; the rest is prose. Some lines as written differ from eight (line 5, with ta āyur, has 7); the edition does not regularise them.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Technology and digital distractions

The passage makes the shape of the mind out of what the mind recites: the texts it has learned are its head and limbs. Today the mind is shaped by what it reads and scrolls through as surely as by what it once memorised. The passage invites the question of what one’s mind is made of, and whether that is chosen. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Which words, songs or sayings do you know by heart? Do they shape how you think?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Is the self of breath ‘the self of the self of food’, or is something else the self of both? What turns on the answer?

A contemplation exercise Watch the breath for a minute, then notice the thoughts that ride on it, as the text places mind within breath.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.

Uncertainties recorded
