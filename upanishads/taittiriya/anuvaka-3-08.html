The second vow: do not refuse food

The second rule — do not refuse the food that comes to you — with water and light as food and eater, each resting in the other.

5 Faithful English translation

One should not reject food. That [is] the vow. Waters indeed [are] food; light [is] the eater of food. In the waters light is established; in light the waters are established. So this food is established in food. He who knows this food established in food is established. He becomes possessed of food, an eater of food. He becomes great — by offspring, by cattle, by the lustre of sacred learning; great by fame.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Do not refuse food: that is the vow. Water is food, and light, or fire, eats it; light rests in water, and water rests in light. So food rests in food. Whoever knows that food rests in food stands firm, has food and enjoys it, and grows great in family, wealth, learning and good name.

Key concepts अन्न food उपासना worship, meditation अग्नि Agni, fire लोक world, realm व्रत vow, observance

7 Explain it to a beginner

The second rule follows the first: do not refuse the food that comes to you. The commentators explain it in different ways — do not push away what is on your plate, do not refuse with complaints what someone gives you, do not judge food good or bad and reject it. Then the text looks outward, at water and light. Light, or fire, feeds on water, and water, the commentators say, comes from light; each holds the other up. So again food rests in food. Whoever sees this is promised the same blessings as before. Like the first rule, this one is about receiving with grace, not about what to eat; one commentator notes that it does not cancel other rules about food.

An analogy Like a guest who accepts what the host offers without inspecting it, out of respect for the giver.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the anuvāka say?

Do not reject food; that is the vow. Water is food and light is the eater; light rests in water and water rests in light; so food rests in food. Whoever knows this is established, has food, eats it, and becomes great in offspring, cattle, learning and fame.

Traditional Interpretation

Why this vow?

Śaṅkara: like the first, it praises food; food not rejected through notions of good and bad is honoured and made great — तदेवं शुभाशुभकल्पनया अपरिह्रीयमाणं स्तुतं महीकृतमन्नं स्यात्. The same explanation is to be applied to the rest — एवं यथोक्तमुत्तरेष्वपि आपो वा अन्नम् इत्यादिषु योजयेत्. Bhāradvāja-Rāmānuja gives the reason as before: food is the door to the knowledge of the supreme Brahman, परब्रह्मज्ञानस्य द्वारभूतत्वात्.

Traditional Interpretation

How do the Viśiṣṭādvaita commentators gloss ‘reject’?

Kūranārāyaṇa: not refusing, with blame, what is given — येन केन वा आत्मने दीयमानं निन्दापूर्वकं न निषेधेत्. Raṅgarāmānuja: not pushing away the food on one’s plate. Gopālānanda (modern): not refusing it even if it is poor — अन्नं पात्रे समुपनतम् अवरमपि न परिचक्षीत. Rāmānanda: whatever comes by the rule of life — यदन्नं विधिवशात् प्राप्तं भवेत् तत् न निराकुर्यात्.

Traditional Interpretation

How are water and light food and eater?

Rāmānanda: water is the effect of light, and a cause is present in its effect — आपो हि ज्योतिषः कार्यं कार्ये च कारणं समनुगतं भवति; each therefore rests in the other, and each can be seen as food or eater. Kūranārāyaṇa traces a circle from the offering in the fire through rain to food: मन्त्रपूतम् अन्नं हि जलरूपेण परिणमति.

Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Bhṛguvallī 8

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

The vow, like the first, is praise of food: तद्व्रतं पूर्ववत्स्तुत्यर्थम्. Water and light are taken as before, each food and eater.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Kūranārāyaṇa; Raṅgarāmānuja; Kauśika Govindarāja; Rāmānanda; Gopālānanda (modern), commentaries on Bhṛguvallī 8 (six-commentary edition)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

A further rule for the knower of Brahman — ब्रह्मविद एव व्रतान्तरमाह (Govindarāja): not to refuse what is given. The waters are the Lord’s dwelling, Kūranārāyaṇa says, and the circle of offering, rain and food shows everything resting in the one Self.

Dvaita · Madhva, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, verses filed under 3.8–3.9

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Madhva’s verses as imported do not gloss this vow separately. They state the principle that governs all four pairs: the Lord abides in forms as enjoyer and enjoyed — भोग्यभोक्तृस्वरूपेण तिष्ठति क्रीडया स्वयम्.

Where they disagree

The schools agree on the vow and differ, as in 3.7, on what ‘food’ ultimately is: praised as a means (Śaṅkara), the Lord’s body (Viśiṣṭādvaita), or the Lord in a form (Madhva).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • आपो वा अन्नम् āpo vā annam → आपः + वै + अन्नम्

    visarga; ai before a vowel

    āpaḥ before the voiced v: aḥ before a voiced consonant becomes o: the visarga (ru) becomes u, P. 6.1.114 (हशि च), and a + u gives o, P. 6.1.87 (आद्गुणः). vai + annam: ai before a vowel becomes āy, P. 6.1.78 (एचोऽयवायावः), and the y is dropped, P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य); the hiatus stays.

  • ज्योतिरन्नादम् jyotirannādam → ज्योतिः + अन्नादम्

    visarga

    After i the visarga (from s, P. 8.2.66 (ससजुषो रुँः)) appears as r before a vowel.

  • ज्योतिष्यापः jyotiṣyāpaḥ → ज्योतिषि + आपः

    yaṇ sandhi

    i or u before an unlike vowel becomes y or v: P. 6.1.77 (इको यणचि).

  • महान्भवति mahānbhavati → महान् + भवति

    —

    Written without a space in the base text; no change of sound.

  • तद्व्रतम् tadvratam → तत् + व्रतम्

    consonant sandhi

    Final t before a vowel or a voiced consonant becomes d: P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते).

  • तदेतदन्नमन्ने tadetadannamanne → तत् + एतत् + अन्नम् + अन्ने

    consonant sandhi

    Final t before a vowel or a voiced consonant becomes d: P. 8.2.39 (झलां जशोऽन्ते). Final m before a vowel is simply joined.

  • स य एतदन्नमन्ने sa ya etadannamanne → सः + यः + एतत् + अन्नम् + अन्ने

    visarga

    saḥ and eṣaḥ lose their visarga before a consonant: P. 6.1.132 (एतत्तदोः सुलोपोऽकोरनञ्समासे हलि). yaḥ before e: aḥ before a vowel other than a loses its visarga and the hiatus stays: P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि) makes it y, P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य) drops the y.

    anusvāra

    Final m before a consonant becomes anusvāra: P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः).

Morphology

परिचक्षीत
Optative middle of cakṣ with pari: ‘reject, refuse; speak against’. Śaṅkara glosses ‘not reject’, Govindarāja ‘not make a show of refusing’ (न परिख्यातं कुर्यात्).
आपः
The word for water is plural in Sanskrit (āpaḥ, f.); the participle pratiṣṭhitāḥ agrees with it.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

The same frame as 3.7: an optative injunction with na, named a vow; two identifications (āpo vā annam, jyotir annādam); two sentences of mutual support with locatives and participles; the summary; the relative-correlative sentence of the result and the shared refrain.

Ambiguities

What is it to ‘reject’ food?
Śaṅkara: not to reject it, अन्नं न परिचक्षीत न परिहरेत्, judging it good or bad. Kūranārāyaṇa: not to refuse, with words of blame, whatever is given to oneself — येन केन वा आत्मने दीयमानं निन्दापूर्वकं न निषेधेत्. Raṅgarāmānuja: not to push away the food on one’s plate — पात्रस्थम् अन्नं न निराकुर्यात् इत्यर्थः. Rāmānanda: whatever comes by the rule of life — यदन्नं विधिवशात् प्राप्तं भवेत् तत् न निराकुर्यात्.
Why are water and light food and eater?
Rāmānanda: water is the effect of light, and the cause is present in its effect — आपो हि ज्योतिषः कार्यं कार्ये च कारणं समनुगतं भवति — so each rests in the other. Kūranārāyaṇa connects it with offering: the food offered in the fire becomes water, which in turn feeds the fire — अन्नं हि जलरूपेण परिणमति.
Is the vow absolute?
Kūranārāyaṇa quotes a verse that Prajāpati counted even a wrongdoer’s food as fit to eat (भोज्यं मेने प्रजापतिः) and says the text will itself show that the vow involves no conflict (अविरोधं स्वयमेव वक्ष्यति). Govindarāja’s note appears to refer to scripture requiring bad food to be given up, so the vow is not read as overriding other rules; the OCR text of his note is damaged there, and it is not quoted.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Ethical leadership

Refusing what is offered can be a quiet way of putting oneself above the giver. A leader who accepts help, ideas and hospitality without first judging them signals respect for the people who offer them. The vow is about food, but the attitude behind it — receiving before judging — applies widely. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Is there something you were offered recently that you refused too quickly?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Water comes from light, and light feeds on water, say the commentators. Where in your life does what you give come back to sustain you?

A contemplation exercise For one day, accept what is offered to you — food, help, an opinion — with a simple thanks before deciding what you think of it.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.

Further notes

  • The results (annavān annādo bhavati …) repeat those of 3.6; the commentators say so (‘as before’).
  • Madhva’s digital text gives one comment for 3.8–3.9; it opens with a gap (‘.....’) and a verse that seems to concern the guest-vow of 3.10. His four named forms are earth, breath, space and body; water and light are not among them in the imported text.

Uncertainties recorded

  • review Madhva’s comment as imported is not divided by anuvāka (3.8–3.9 together) and opens with a verse about guests that belongs with 3.10; the division should be checked against a printed edition.
  • uncertain Govindarāja’s note on 3.8 is damaged in the OCR text after its first gloss; the damaged part is described, not quoted.
