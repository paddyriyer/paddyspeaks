Taittirīya Upaniṣad · Śīkṣāvallī · Anuvāka 7 of 12

Everything in fives: the outer world and the body

Three groups of five outside and three groups of five within are set side by side, and a seer’s saying concludes that the whole is fivefold and that the inner fives complete the outer.

5 Faithful English translation

Earth, mid-region, sky, the quarters, the intermediate quarters; fire, wind, sun, moon, stars; waters, plants, trees, space, the self (body): thus [the series] with regard to beings. Now with regard to the self: out-breath, diffused breath, in-breath, up-breath, middle breath; eye, ear, mind, speech, skin; hide, flesh, sinew, bone, marrow. Having set this out, the seer said: ‘Fivefold indeed is this all. By the fivefold, indeed, one fills the fivefold.’

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

Earth, the space between, the sky, the four quarters and the quarters between them; fire, wind, sun, moon and stars; water, plants, trees, space and the body of the whole — that is the outer world. Now the inner: the five breaths; eye, ear, mind, speech and touch; skin, flesh, sinew, bone and marrow. Having arranged all this, the seer said: ‘All this is in fives. With the fives within, one fills out the fives without.’

Key concepts उपासना worship, meditation ऋषि seer आत्मन् self, Self लोक world, realm अग्नि Agni, fire वायु breath, air सूर्य the Sun पाङ्क्त fivefold

7 Explain it to a beginner

This passage is a list, and the list has a pattern. First it names fifteen things in the world outside us, in three groups of five: earth, air-space, sky and the directions; fire, wind, sun, moon and stars; water, plants, trees, space and the whole body of the world. Then it names fifteen things inside the body, again in three groups of five: five kinds of breath; eye, ear, mind, speech and touch; skin, flesh, sinew, bone and marrow. A seer then sums it up: everything comes in fives, and the fives inside us match and complete the fives outside. The commentators say the point is a meditation: to see the world and one’s own body as built on the same plan, and so as not truly separate.

An analogy Like two halves of a torn ticket: each half is incomplete, but the edges match exactly, and fitting them together shows that they were always one piece.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the passage actually say?

It lists three groups of five ‘with regard to beings’ — worlds and quarters; fire, wind, sun, moon, stars; waters, plants, trees, space, self — and three ‘with regard to the self’ — five breaths; eye, ear, mind, speech, skin; hide, flesh, sinew, bone, marrow. It says that a seer, having set this out, declared that all this is fivefold, and that by the fivefold one fills the fivefold. It does not say what is to be done with this; the commentators call it a meditation.

Traditional Interpretation

Why ‘fivefold’?

Śaṅkara connects the number with the paṅkti metre of five pādas and with the sacrifice, which the Veda calls pāṅkta: पाङ्क्तश्च यज्ञः, पञ्चपदा पङ्क्तिः पाङ्क्तो यज्ञः. To see the whole as fivefold is therefore to see it as a sacrifice; by that sacrifice, imagined, one reaches Prajāpati, whose nature is fivefold: तेन यज्ञेन परिकल्पितेन पाङ्क्तात्मकं प्रजापतिमभिसम्पद्यते.

Traditional Interpretation

How are the six groups named?

Śaṅkara names each group: the five of worlds, of deities and of beings (लोकपाङ्क्तम्, देवतापाङ्क्तम्, भूतपाङ्क्तम्) outside; the five of breath, of the senses and of the bodily tissues (वायुपाङ्क्तम्, इन्द्रियपाङ्क्तम्, धातुपाङ्क्तम्) within. The two halves, he says, are all there is: एतावद्धीदं सर्वमध्यात्मं बाह्यं च पाङ्क्तमेव.

Comparative Vedānta

What is the fruit of the meditation?

For Śaṅkara, one who knows ‘all this is fivefold’ becomes one with Prajāpati: एवं पाङ्क्तमिदं सर्वमिति यो वेद, स प्रजापत्यात्मैव भवतीत्यर्थः. The modern commentator Gopālānanda reads spṛṇoti as ‘protects’ and the fruit as sovereignty: पाङ्केन - पाङ्कदृष्ट्योपास्यमानेनानेन पाङ्कं पृथिव्यादि स्पृणोति पालयति, साम्राज्यं लभते इति भावः (his printed text spells pāṅkta as पाङ्क).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Why is ‘adhividhāya ṛṣiḥ’ left unjoined?

Ordinary sandhi would fuse ā and ṛ into ar (adhividhāyarṣiḥ). The recited text keeps the two vowels apart, as Pāṇini allows before ṛ: P. 6.1.128 (ऋत्यकः). Every witness of this edition has the hiatus. The variant avāntaradiśāḥ (long ā), read by the Sāyaṇa-pāṭha and Madhva’s text, is a by-form of the same word.

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Śīkṣāvallī 7

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

The meditation continues the meditation on Brahman as the four calls, now in the form of the fivefold: तस्यैवेदानीं पृथिव्यादिपाङ्क्तस्वरूपेणोपासनमुच्यते. It is an upāsana of Brahman with qualities whose fruit is identity with Prajāpati; Śaṅkara does not present it as the knowledge of the unconditioned.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Kūranārāyaṇa, commentary on Śīkṣāvallī 7 (six-commentary edition)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Kūranārāyaṇa calls the passage a particular meditation taught as a limb of knowledge: इदानीं ध्यानविशेषं ज्ञानाङ्गत्वेनोपदिशति. The seer is the Veda (ऋषिर्वेदात्मा), and by the fives held together in meditation one pervades and protects the universe: पाङ्कं - विश्वं स्पृणोति - व्याप्नोति, पालयति (his printed text spells pāṅkta as पाङ्क).

Dvaita · Madhva, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, on the seventh anuvāka

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Madhva cites a verse he attributes to the Tattvasaṃhitā (इति तत्त्वसंहितायाम्): it is Hari himself, in the form of Nārāyaṇa and his other forms, who strengthens the six fives by the fivefold — पृथिव्याद्यं पाङ्क्तषट्कं पाङ्क्तेनैव स्वयं हरिः. The seer who spoke is Viṣṇu. This is a verse cited by Madhva; this edition cannot verify it as a quotation of an independent work.

Where they disagree

All read a meditation on correspondences. They differ on its fruit (identity with Prajāpati for Śaṅkara; the power to sustain the world for Kūranārāyaṇa and Gopālānanda) and on who does the ‘filling’ (the meditator for Śaṅkara and Kūranārāyaṇa; Hari himself for Madhva).

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • पृथिव्यन्तरिक्षं pṛthivyantarikṣaṃ → पृथिवी + अन्तरिक्षम्

    vowel sandhi (yaṇ)

    ī before a dissimilar vowel becomes y: P. 6.1.77 (इको यणचि).

  • द्यौर्दिशोऽवान्तरदिशः dyaurdiśo'vāntaradiśaḥ → द्यौः + दिशः + अवान्तरदिशः

    r-visarga; aḥ + a

    A visarga after au becomes r before the voiced d. diśaḥ before a becomes diśo, and the a is elided (avagraha): P. 6.1.113 (अतो रोरप्लुतादप्लुते), P. 6.1.109 (एङः पदान्तादति).

  • आप ओषधयो वनस्पतय आकाश आत्मा āpa oṣadhayo vanaspataya ākāśa ātmā → आपः + ओषधयः + वनस्पतयः + आकाशः + आत्मा

    aḥ before a vowel; aḥ before a voiced consonant

    Before a vowel other than a, aḥ loses its visarga and the hiatus stays (P. 8.3.17 (भोभगोअघोअपूर्वस्य योऽशि), P. 8.3.19 (लोपः शाकल्यस्य)); before the voiced v, oṣadhayaḥ becomes oṣadhayo (P. 6.1.114 (हशि च)).

  • मांसंस्नावास्थि māṃsaṃsnāvāsthi → मांसम् + स्नाव + अस्थि

    m before a consonant; savarṇa-dīrgha

    Final m before a consonant becomes anusvāra: P. 8.3.23 (मोऽनुस्वारः). a + a → ā: P. 6.1.101 (अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः). The base prints māṃsaṃ and snāvāsthi without a space.

  • पाङ्क्तेनैव pāṅktenaiva → पाङ्क्तेन + एव

    vowel sandhi (vṛddhi)

    a + e → ai: P. 6.1.88 (वृद्धिरेचि).

Morphology

द्यौः
Nominative singular of div/dyu, ‘sky’, with vṛddhi in the strong stem (dyau-).
पाङ्क्तम्
A secondary derivative of paṅkti, the metre of five pādas (and ‘a row of five’), with vṛddhi of the first syllable: ‘belonging to the paṅkti, fivefold’. Śaṅkara connects it with the sacrifice, which the Veda calls pāṅkta: पञ्चपदा पङ्क्तिः पाङ्क्तो यज्ञः.
अवोचत्
Reduplicated (thematic) aorist of vac, ‘to speak’: ‘he said’.
स्पृणोति
Present of spṛ (fifth class, -ṇo-), ‘to fill, to strengthen, to sustain’. Śaṅkara glosses ‘strengthens, fills, is known as one with’ (एकात्मतयोपलभ्यत इत्येतत्).
स्नाव
Nominative singular of the neuter n-stem snāvan, ‘sinew’.
मज्जा
Nominative singular of majjan, ‘marrow’ (an n-stem; nominative majjā).

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

Six lists of five nouns in the nominative, without verbs: three ‘with regard to beings’ (adhibhūtam) and, after atha, three ‘with regard to the self’ (adhyātmam). An absolutive (adhividhāya, ‘having set out’) introduces the seer’s saying with avocat, ‘said’; the saying is two sentences closed by iti — a nominal sentence (pāṅktaṃ vā idaṃ sarvam) and a verbal one whose subject is unexpressed (‘one fills’).

Ambiguities

What is ‘ātman’ at the end of the outer series?
In a list of beings — waters, plants, trees, space — ātman is unexpected. Śaṅkara takes it as Virāj, the cosmic body, ‘because beings are the subject’ (आत्मेति विराट् , भूताधिकारात्); Gopālānanda similarly. Madhva reads it as the principle of I-making: आत्मा अहङ्कारतत्त्वम्.
Who is the seer?
Śaṅkara allows two readings: the Veda itself, or some seer who had this vision — ऋषिः वेदः एतद्दर्शनसम्पन्नो वा कश्चिदृषिः. The text names no one.
Who fills whom, and how?
The subject of spṛṇoti is not expressed. Śaṅkara: the inner fives, by sameness of number, fill out the outer, so that the two are known as one; the knower becomes Prajāpati. Kūranārāyaṇa and Gopālānanda take spṛṇoti as ‘protects, sustains’ (स्पृणोति - व्याप्नोति, पालयति; स्पृणोति पालयति), the result being the power to sustain the world.
Why only three outer groups are ‘adhibhūta’
Strictly only the third group (waters … ātman) consists of beings. Śaṅkara explains the word as standing for all three outer groups: इत्यधिभूतमिति अधिलोकाधिदैवतपाङ्क्तद्वयोपलक्षणार्थम्.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Ego and self-image

We often picture ourselves as a self set against a world. This passage lays the two side by side and finds the same structure in each: the breath within answers the wind without, the eye answers the sun, the body answers the earth. It does not deny that there is an inside and an outside; it says they match. A self-image built on that correspondence is harder to inflate and harder to isolate. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.
