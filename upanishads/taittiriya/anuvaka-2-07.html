Taittirīya Upaniṣad · Brahmānandavallī · Anuvāka 7 of 9

Rasa: the essence that makes us glad

The verse on being born from non-being, the famous ‘raso vai saḥ’ — Brahman is the essence that gives every joy and every breath — and the teaching that fearlessness comes from resting in it and fear from making the smallest gap.

5 Faithful English translation

‘Non-being indeed was this in the beginning; from that indeed being was born. That made itself by itself; therefore that is called the well-made.’ What indeed is that well-made, he indeed is the essence. For only having obtained the essence does this one become blissful. Who indeed would breathe out, who would breathe in, if this space [were] not bliss? For this alone gives bliss. For when this one finds in this invisible, bodiless, undefinable, supportless [one] a fearless footing, then he has gone to fearlessness. But when this one makes in this even a little gap, then for him there is fear. That itself is fear for the knower who does not reflect. On that also there is this verse.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

‘In the beginning this was non-being; from it being was born. It made itself by itself — and so it is called the self-made.’ That self-made is the essence, the very savour of things: whoever tastes it is filled with joy. Who could breathe out, who could breathe in, if this bliss were not there in the space of the heart? It is this that gives joy. When a person finds a fearless footing in this invisible, bodiless, indefinable, unsupported one, he has reached fearlessness. But the moment he makes the slightest gap in it, fear comes. It is that very reality that becomes fear for one who knows but does not reflect. There is a verse about this too.

7 Explain it to a beginner

This is one of the most loved passages of the Upaniṣads. It begins with a short verse: before the world appeared there was something without name or form, and from it everything with name and form came — it made itself. Then come three famous words: raso vai saḥ, ‘it is the essence’ — the sweetness, the taste in things. Whoever finds it becomes glad. The passage even asks: who could breathe in or out, if this joy were not there? Then it turns to fear. When someone rests in that reality, which no eye sees and no word defines, they become fearless. But the moment they make even a small gap between themselves and it, fear arises. The commentators disagree about the gap — a sense of being separate, or a lapse in remembering — but agree that fear begins there.

An analogy Like sugar dissolved in water: you cannot see it anywhere, but every sip is sweet because of it.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the passage say?

A verse: in the beginning this was non-being; being was born from it; it made itself, and so it is called ‘well-made’. That well-made is the essence (rasa); obtaining it, one becomes blissful. No one could breathe if this space were not bliss; this alone gives bliss. When one finds a fearless footing in the invisible, bodiless, undefinable, supportless, one has gone to fearlessness; when one makes even a little gap in it, fear arises. That itself is fear for the knower who does not reflect.

Traditional Interpretation

Why is Brahman called rasa?

Śaṅkara: rasa in ordinary speech is what satisfies and delights, like sweet or sour — रसो नाम तृप्तिहेतुः आनन्दकरो मधुराम्लादिः प्रसिद्धो लोके. Wise people who have no outer source of pleasure are still seen to be glad, as if they had tasted something; that something must be Brahman: नूनं ब्रह्मैव रसस्तेषाम्. For Śaṅkara this is one of a series of proofs in the anuvāka that Brahman exists (from the world’s being ‘made’, from joy, from breath, from fear and fearlessness).

Traditional Interpretation

What does ‘who could breathe?’ prove?

Śaṅkara: the body’s breathing and other activities are done by organs working together, and such working together needs a conscious being that is not itself part of the combination: को ह्येव लोके अन्यात् अपानचेष्टां कुर्यादित्यर्थः. Kūranārāyaṇa reads anyāt and prāṇyāt as breathing out and in, अन्यात् प्राण्यात् इति उच्छ्वासनिश्वासौ विवक्षितौ, and the bliss as what keeps all beings alive.

Traditional Interpretation

How do fear and fearlessness arise?

Śaṅkara: fear always comes from a second; one who rests in the one without a second has nothing to fear, and one who makes even a little difference fears: भेददर्शनमेव हि भयकारणम्. So a ‘knower’ who still sees difference is no knower: तस्मात् विद्वानप्यविद्वानेवासौ, योऽयमेकमभिन्नमात्मतत्त्वं न पश्यति. Gopālānanda (modern) reads the footing as unbroken remembrance and the gap as its interruption, and quotes a verse he gives from the Garuḍa Purāṇa, यन्मुहूर्तं क्षणं वाऽपि वासुदेवो न चिन्त्यते.

Traditional Interpretation

What does Rāmānanda add?

This passage reads ānandayāti as Vedic for ānandayati (आनन्दयाति इत्यत्र दीर्घश्छान्दसः), takes ‘udaram’ as ut + aram, ‘even a little’, and names the bliss-self as Śrī Rāma: आनन्दमयस्य श्रीरामस्य आनन्दयितृत्वकथनात्. The last point fits a Rāmānandī commentator, which supports the suggested attribution.

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Brahmānandavallī 7

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

The anuvāka continues the proof that Brahman is. Asat is Brahman before name and form unfold, अव्याकृतं ब्रह्म; rasa, breath and fearlessness all point to a real, conscious, blissful ground. Fearlessness is resting in it as one’s own self; fear is the least seeing of difference: भेददर्शनमेव हि भयकारणम्.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Kūranārāyaṇa; Gopālānanda (modern), commentaries on Brahmānandavallī 7 (six-commentary edition)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

The verse states that the world exists beforehand in a subtle state and that Brahman makes itself into the world as its body: Gopālānanda reads it as rejecting the view that effects come from nothing — असत्कार्यवादव्यावर्तनमुखेन सत्कार्यवादं प्रकाशयन्. Brahman is the rasa that the devotee obtains. Fear is entry into the round of births (Kūranārāyaṇa’s udaram), or a lapse in remembering the Lord (Gopālānanda).

Dvaita · Madhva, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, on the seventh anuvāka

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Asat and sat are names of the Lord: the unreachable Nārāyaṇa became the reachable Vāsudeva, and made himself into Saṅkarṣaṇa and the other forms — स आसाद्यश्च सन्नामा वासुदेवोऽभवत् प्रभुः, तस्मात् तत् सुकृतं नाम स आनन्दो रसस्ततः. The released soul rejoices only by reaching him. Fear comes to one who makes a difference in Viṣṇu: यदैतस्मिन् परे विष्णावुदरं जीवगत्वतः । भेदं करोति तेनैव भयमस्य महद् भवेत्.

Where they disagree

All read ‘raso vai saḥ’ as the claim that Brahman is the source of all joy, but they differ on non-being and on fear. For Śaṅkara asat is Brahman unmanifest and fear is seeing any difference from it. For the Viśiṣṭādvaita commentators the world pre-exists subtly in Brahman, and fear is turning away from the Lord. For Madhva asat and sat are the Lord’s names, and fear comes from a wrong difference made in him.

Morphology

अकुरुत
Imperfect middle of kṛ: ‘made for itself’ — the middle voice fits ‘made itself’.
उच्यते
Present passive of vac ‘speak’: ‘is called’.
आनन्दी
ānanda + the possessive suffix -in: ‘one who has bliss’.
अन्यात्
Optative of an ‘breathe’ (second class, an-yāt). Kūranārāyaṇa: अन्यात् प्राण्यात् इति उच्छ्वासनिश्वासौ विवक्षितौ, breathing out and in; Śaṅkara takes anyāt of the downward breath, apāna.
आनन्दयाति
A Vedic form of the causative of nand with ā, ‘causes to rejoice’. Śaṅkara glosses it with ānandayati; Rāmānanda calls the long ā Vedic (आनन्दयाति इत्यत्र दीर्घश्छान्दसः). It can be read as a subjunctive with the augment ā, P. 3.4.94 (लेटोऽडाटौ).
अनात्म्ये
an- + ātmya, ‘belonging to an ātman (body)’: ‘bodiless’, Śaṅkara’s अशरीरे. Rāmānanda: without a body made of matter.
विन्दते
Present middle of vid ‘find’ (sixth class, vind-a-te), distinct from vid ‘know’.
अरम्
An indeclinable ‘a little’ in Śaṅkara’s reading (अरम् अल्पमपि); Rāmānanda: अरशब्दः अल्पार्थकः.
अमन्वानस्य
Genitive of a- + manvāna, the present middle participle of man ‘think’ (eighth class, man-v-āna): ‘not reflecting’. Kūranārāyaṇa and Gopālānanda read manvānasya, ‘reflecting’ (see ambiguities).
विदुषः
Genitive singular of vidvas, ‘one who knows’.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

A four-line verse (the ‘verse’ announced at the end of anuvāka 6) of two sentences, the second giving a result with tasmāt. A relative-correlative sentence follows (yad vai tat sukṛtam, raso vai saḥ), then a sentence with hi stating a reason. A rhetorical double question (ko hy evānyāt kaḥ prāṇyāt) has a conditional with yat in the sense of yadi; then a short assertion with hi. Two parallel yadā … atha sentences contrast fearlessness and fear, and a closing sentence identifies the cause of fear.

Ambiguities

udaram: ‘even a little’ or ‘belly’?
Śaṅkara divides ut aram and glosses ‘even a little gap’: उत अपि, अरम् अल्पमपि, अन्तरं छिद्रं भेददर्शनं कुरुते. Rāmānanda (Ānandabhāṣya) agrees: उत् इत्यस्य अपि शब्द समानार्थता. Kūranārāyaṇa keeps udaram as one word and makes it entry into the round of fear: अतः अत्र भयरूपसंसारप्रवेशः उदरशब्देन उक्तः. Madhva also keeps udaram, explaining it by a derivation of his own (अ इत्युक्तः परो विष्णुरेभ्य उच्च स एव तु). The padaccheda follows Śaṅkara.
amanvānasya or manvānasya?
The base text prints an avagraha (विदुषोऽमन्वानस्य), and Śaṅkara reads ‘for the knower who does not think [Brahman] as one’: एकत्वेन अमन्वानस्य; Madhva’s verse has the same अमन्वानस्य. Kūranārāyaṇa writes तत्त्वेव भयं विदुषो मन्वानस्य and Gopālānanda विदुषो मन्वानस्य मननैकशीलस्य: for one who knows and constantly reflects, the gap itself — a break in remembrance — is fear. The letters allow both.
What is the ‘gap’?
Śaṅkara: seeing difference between oneself and Brahman — भेददर्शनमेव हि भयकारणम्. Gopālānanda (modern) and Rāmānanda: a break in continuous remembrance of Brahman, अल्पमपि अन्तरं स्मृतिविच्छेदं (Gopālānanda). Madhva: making a difference in Viṣṇu.
asat: nothing, or the unmanifest?
Not absolute non-being: Śaṅkara says asat names Brahman before name and form are unfolded — न पुनरत्यन्तमेवासत् — and Rāmānanda says it is called asat because without name and form it is not seen: नामरूपराहित्येन अदृश्यमानत्वात् असत् शब्देन व्यपदेशः. Madhva makes asat and sat names of Nārāyaṇa and Vāsudeva: अनासाद्यस्त्वसन्नामा पूर्वं नारायणाभिधः.
sukṛtam
Śaṅkara: ‘self-made’ — Brahman is called the doer of itself, स्वयं कर्तृ ब्रह्मेति प्रसिद्धं लोके सर्वकारणत्वात्, and also ‘merit’ as the cause of every fruit. Gopālānanda: ‘that whose work is done with ease’, अक्लिष्टकार्यं ब्रह्मेति यावत्.
ākāśa
Śaṅkara: the space of the heart, the ‘highest space, in the cave’ of 2.1. Kūranārāyaṇa: the shining one, since Brahman is light — सम्भवति च परस्य ब्रह्मणः प्रकाशत्वात् आकाशशब्दाभिधेयत्वम्.

Metre

mixed · syllables counted as written: 9 + 8 + 10 + 11 + 6 + 4 + 12 + 7 + 11 + 8 + 28 + 10 + 17 + 9 + 12 + 9

Lines 1–4 are the verse announced at the end of anuvāka 6, but its syllables, counted as written, are 9 + 8 + 10 + 11 (the last with the closing iti): an irregular verse in the manner of the yajus, not a regular anuṣṭubh. The rest is prose.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Wealth and consumption

‘Only having obtained the essence does one become blissful.’ Much of modern life chases the outer carriers of pleasure — things, experiences, upgrades — and finds the pleasure brief. Śaṅkara’s observation is simple and testable: some people with very little are plainly glad. The passage locates gladness in something that cannot be bought because it is not a thing. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin When have you been happy without any particular reason?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry The passage says fear begins with ‘even a little gap’. Is fear always a sense of separation — from others, from safety, from what we love?

A contemplation exercise Notice your breathing for a minute, and with each breath ask the passage’s question: what keeps this going? Do not answer; just notice.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.

  • interpretation The avagraha in विदुषोऽमन्वानस्य (Śaṅkara, Madhva: amanvānasya) is printed by the base text; Kūranārāyaṇa and Gopālānanda read manvānasya. The letters allow both.
  • uncertain Madhva’s derivation of udaram (अ इत्युक्तः परो विष्णुरेभ्य उच्च स एव तु । त उदा जीवसङ्घाः स्युरुदरं तद्गतान्तरम्) is quoted but its exact sense is not settled here.
