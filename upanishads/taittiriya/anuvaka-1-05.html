Taittirīya Upaniṣad · Śīkṣāvallī · Anuvāka 5 of 12

The four calls: bhūḥ, bhuvaḥ, suvar — and a fourth, mahaḥ

The three ancient calls of the ritual are made the limbs of a fourth, mahaḥ, which is named Brahman and the self; each call is matched with a world, a light, a Veda and a breath.

1 Original Sanskrit

भूर्भुवः सुवरिति वा एतास्तिस्रो व्याहृतयः ।तासामु ह स्मैतां चतुर्थीम् ।माहाचमस्यः प्रवेदयते ।मह इति ।तद्ब्रह्म ।स आत्मा ।अङ्गान्यन्या देवताः ।भूरिति वा अयं लोकः ।भुव इत्यन्तरिक्षम् ।सुवरित्यसौ लोकः ॥ १ ॥

मह इत्यादित्यः ।आदित्येन वाव सर्वे लोका महीयन्ते ।भूरिति वा अग्निः ।भुव इति वायुः ।सुवरित्यादित्यः ।मह इति चन्द्रमाः ।चन्द्रमसा वाव सर्वाणि ज्योतींषि महीयन्ते ।भूरिति वा ऋचः ।भुव इति सामानि ।सुवरिति यजूंषि ॥ २ ॥

मह इति ब्रह्म ।ब्रह्मणा वाव सर्वे वेदा महीयन्ते ।भूरिति वै प्राणः ।भुव इत्यपानः ।सुवरिति व्यानः ।मह इत्यन्नम् ।अन्नेन वाव सर्वे प्राणा महीयन्ते ।ता वा एताश्चतस्रश्चतुर्धा ।चतस्रश्चतस्रो व्याहृतयः ।ता यो वेद ।स वेद ब्रह्म ।सर्वेऽस्मै देवा बलिमावहन्ति ॥ ३ ॥

Accented text · Taittirīya Āraṇyaka, Sāyaṇa-pāṭha, as the witness keys it

भूर्भुव॒स्सुव॒रिति॒ वा ए॒तास्ति॒स्रो व्याहृ॑तयः । तासा॑मु ह स्मै॒ तां च॑तु॒र्थीम् । माहा॑चमस्यः॒ प्रवे॑दयते । मह॒ इति॑ । तद्ब्रह्म॑ । स आ॒त्मा । अङ्गा॑न्य॒न्या दे॒वताः॑ । भूरिति॒ वा अ॒यं लो॒कः । भुव॒ इत्य॒न्तरि॑क्षम् । सुव॒रित्य॒सौ लो॒कः ॥

मह॒ इत्या॑दि॒त्यः । आ॒दि॒त्येन॒ वाव सर्वे॑ लो॒का मही॑यन्ते । भूरिति॒ वा अ॒ग्निः । भुव॒ इति॑ वा॒युः । सुव॒रित्या॑दि॒त्यः । मह॒ इति॑ च॒न्द्रमाः॑ । च॒न्द्रम॑सा॒ वाव सर्वा॑णि॒ ज्योतीँ॑षि॒ मही॑यन्ते । भूरिति॒ वा ऋचः॑ । भुव॒ इति॒ सामा॑नि । सुव॒रिति॒ यजूँ॑षि ॥

मह॒ इति॒ ब्रह्म॑ । ब्रह्म॑णा॒ वाव सर्वे॑ वे॒दा मही॑यन्ते । भूरिति॒ वै प्रा॒णः । भुव॒ इत्य॑पा॒नः । सुव॒रिति॑ व्या॒नः । मह॒ इत्यन्न॑म् । अन्ने॑न॒ वाव सर्वे॑ प्रा॒णा मही॑यन्ते । ता वा ए॒ताश्चत॑स्रश्चतु॒र्धा । चत॑स्रश्चतस्रो॒ व्याहृ॑तयः । ता यो वेद॑ । स वे॑द॒ ब्रह्म॑ । सर्वे॑ऽस्मै दे॒वा ब॒लिमाव॑हन्ति ॥

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

1.5.1 Bhūḥ, bhuvaḥ, suvar: these are the three well-known calls. To them Māhācamasya added a fourth, mahaḥ. That is Brahman, the body’s trunk; the other powers are its limbs. Bhūḥ is this world, bhuvaḥ the space between, suvar the world beyond.

1.5.2 Mahaḥ is the sun, for it is the sun that makes all the worlds great. Bhūḥ is fire, bhuvaḥ wind, suvar the sun, mahaḥ the moon, for it is the moon that makes all the lights great. Bhūḥ is the verses of the Ṛgveda, bhuvaḥ the chants of the Sāmaveda, suvar the formulas of the Yajurveda.

1.5.3 Mahaḥ is brahman, the syllable Om, for it is Om that makes all the Vedas great. Bhūḥ is the out-breath, bhuvaḥ the in-breath, suvar the breath that pervades the body, mahaḥ food, for it is food that makes all the breaths great. So these four calls are each fourfold: four sets of four. Whoever knows them knows Brahman; to such a person all the gods bring offerings.

7 Explain it to a beginner

Before many Vedic rites three short words are spoken: bhūḥ, bhuvaḥ, suvar. They are old names for earth, the space above it, and the sky. This passage says that a teacher called Māhācamasya added a fourth word, mahaḥ, ‘greatness’, and that the fourth is the most important: it is the centre, and the other three are like arms and legs attached to it. Then the passage plays a matching game. In each of four groups, three things line up with the first three words, and the fourth thing is the one that makes the others great: the sun for the worlds, the moon for the stars, the sacred syllable for the Vedas, and food for the breaths of the body. Whoever understands these connections, it says, understands the highest reality.

An analogy Like a set of index cards sorted into four piles, each with a heading card on top: the passage shows that every pile — worlds, lights, scriptures, breaths — has the same four-part shape, and that in each the fourth card holds the rest together.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the passage actually say?

It names three calls, bhūḥ, bhuvaḥ and suvar, and reports that the sage Māhācamasya made known a fourth, mahaḥ. It says that mahaḥ is Brahman and the self, and that the other deities are limbs. It then sets out four series: worlds (this world, the mid-region, the world beyond, the sun), lights (fire, wind, sun, moon), Vedas (Ṛg, Sāman, Yajus, brahman), and breaths (out-breath, in-breath, diffused breath, food). It says the four are fourfold, and that one who knows them knows Brahman and receives tribute from the gods. It does not say how the knowing is to be done; the commentators call it a meditation (upāsana).

Traditional Interpretation

Why does the text name the sage who added the fourth call?

Śaṅkara takes the particles u ha sma as reporting an event, and the name as part of the practice: remembering the seer is itself a limb of the meditation, since it is taught here — माहाचमस्यग्रहणमार्षानुस्मरणार्थम्, ऋष्यनुस्मरणमप्युपासनाङ्गमिति गम्यते, इहोपदेशात्. Nothing else is known about Māhācamasya from this edition’s sources.

Traditional Interpretation

Why is the fourth the greatest in each series?

The text gives its own reason, an etymology: mahaḥ, ‘greatness’, is the one by which the others mahīyante, ‘grow great’. Śaṅkara explains the verb as growth and increase (महनं वृद्धिः उपचयः) and the relation as that of a trunk to its limbs: आत्मना ह्यङ्गानि महीयन्ते. He then lists the four series by call: the first call is ‘this world, fire, the Ṛgveda, the out-breath’; the fourth ‘sun, moon, brahman, food’ — इत्येवम् एकैकाश्चतुर्धा भवन्ति.

Traditional Interpretation

Why does the anuvāka end ‘he knows Brahman’ when Brahman has been named?

Śaṅkara raises this objection himself and answers that the text means Brahman with the particular qualities the next anuvāka will give — that it is reached within the heart, made of mind, and so on: तद्विशेषविवक्षुत्वाददोषः, हृदयान्तरुपलभ्यत्वं मनोमयत्वादिश्च. So the two anuvākas form one sentence and one meditation, and the gods’ tribute is the fruit of reaching sovereignty: स्वाराज्यप्राप्तौ सत्यामित्यर्थः.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

How is the word ‘vyāhṛti’ understood?

Vyāhṛti is an action noun from vi-ā-hṛ, ‘to utter’: an utterance, and in ritual the three short calls bhūḥ, bhuvaḥ, suvar. Kūranārāyaṇa gives an etymology for each: bhūḥ as that in which beings come to be (भवन्त्यस्मिन् सर्वाणि भूतानीति भूः), mahaḥ as that by which the universe is made great (महयति विश्वं, महीयते विश्वम् अनेनेति वा - महः). Such etymologies are explanatory, not historical.

Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Śīkṣāvallī 5–6

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

For Śaṅkara the Brahman meditated on through the calls is Hiraṇyagarbha, Brahman as the cosmic person, of whom the other deities are limbs: मह इत्येतस्य हिरण्यगर्भस्य व्याहृत्यात्मनो ब्रह्मणोऽङ्गान्यन्या देवता इत्युक्तम् (opening his comment on the next anuvāka). It is a meditation on the conditioned Brahman with a stated result, sovereignty (svārājya), not the knowledge of the unconditioned Brahman that the second vallī will teach.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Kūranārāyaṇa, commentary on Śīkṣāvallī 5 (six-commentary edition)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Kūranārāyaṇa calls the meditation on the calls a limb of the meditation on the supreme that follows (परोपासनस्याङ्गभूतं व्याहृत्युपासनं). The fourth call denotes the supreme Brahman, the whole; the others denote what is its body: अनया चतुर्थ्या व्याहृत्या अङ्गिभूतं परं ब्रह्म प्रतिपाद्यते, अन्याभिर्व्याहृतिभिः तच्छरीरभूताः. ‘Self’ means the one who supports and rules all things as a soul does its body: यथैकस्य शरीरस्य शरीरि तथा सर्वेषां भूतानाम् आत्मा. In the solar series he takes mahaḥ as Nārāyaṇa within the sun (आदित्यान्तवर्ती नारायणः).

Dvaita · Madhva, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, on the vyāhṛti anuvāka

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Madhva’s comment consists of verses he attributes to a work he calls Vyāhṛtitattva (इति व्याहृतितत्त्वे). In them the four calls name four forms of Viṣṇu: Aniruddha (bhūḥ, the head), Pradyumna (bhuvaḥ, the arms), Saṅkarṣaṇa (suvar, the feet) and Vāsudeva (mahaḥ, the middle of the body) — वासुदेवो महोनामा त्वनिरुद्धश्शिरो मतः. Māhācamasya is the god Brahmā, who knows them: माहाचमस्यस्तज्ज्ञानी ब्रह्मा सम्परिकीर्तितः. These are verses cited by Madhva; this edition cannot verify them as quotations of an independent work.

Where they disagree

All read the four calls as a meditation in which mahaḥ is the whole and the rest its parts. They differ on what that whole is: Hiraṇyagarbha, the conditioned Brahman, for Śaṅkara; the supreme Brahman, Nārāyaṇa, with the rest as its body, for Kūranārāyaṇa; and for Madhva Viṣṇu in four forms, with Māhācamasya as Brahmā.

Morphology

1.5.1 सुवः
The Taittirīya form of svar, an indeclinable whose final visarga goes back to r: pausal suvaḥ, and suvar before a vowel (suvar iti).
1.5.1 प्रवेदयते
Causative of vid, ‘to know’: ‘causes to be known, makes known’. With sma the present reports a past event.
1.5.1 माहाचमस्यः
A patronymic: the descendant of Mahācamasa, with vṛddhi of the first syllable (mahā → māhā) and the suffix -ya. Kūranārāyaṇa names the rule of the Garga group (gargādi, yañ), cf. P. 4.1.105 (गर्गादिभ्यो यञ्).
1.5.2 महीयन्ते
A middle verb from the stem mahīya-, ‘to be great, to be exalted’, related to mahas, the word being explained: the text etymologises the fourth call by the verb (mahaḥ … mahīyante). Śaṅkara: महनं वृद्धिः उपचयः.
1.5.2 ज्योतींषि
Nominative plural of the neuter s-stem jyotis, with the nasal of the plural (jyotīṃṣi).
1.5.3 चतुर्धा
catur, ‘four’, with the suffix -dhā of manner: ‘in four ways’. The four calls are each fourfold, so the anuvāka has set out sixteen equations (4 × 4).
1.5.3 वेद
The perfect of vid, ‘to know’, used with present meaning: ‘knows’.
1.5.3 आवहन्ति
Present of vah, ‘to carry’, with the preverb ā, ‘towards’: ‘they bring’.

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

Nominal sentences throughout, on the pattern ‘X iti Y’ (‘[the call] X [is] Y’), with the verb ‘to be’ understood. The opening sentence names the three calls; a narrative sentence with u ha sma and the present pravedayate (‘made known’) introduces the fourth; three short identifications (tat brahma, sa ātmā, aṅgāny anyā devatāḥ) give its rank. Then four series of four identifications follow, each closed, except the first, by a sentence explaining why the fourth member is the greatest (‘by X, truly, all Ys grow great’). The anuvāka ends with a summary (‘four, fourfold’), a relative clause of knowing (ta yo veda, sa veda brahma) and its reward.

Ambiguities

What is ‘ātman’ beside ‘aṅgāni’?
Paired with aṅgāni, ‘limbs’, ātman may have its old bodily sense, ‘trunk’: mahaḥ is the trunk, the other calls and deities the limbs. Śaṅkara gives it the sense ‘that which pervades’ (आप्नोतेर्व्याप्तिकर्मणः आत्मा), so that mahaḥ pervades the others; Kūranārāyaṇa the sense ‘the self that supports and governs a body’. Both readings keep the contrast of whole and parts.
What is ‘brahman’ in ‘maha iti brahma’?
In the series of Vedas (Ṛg, Sāman, Yajus), brahman stands where a fourth item of speech is expected. Śaṅkara takes it as the syllable Om (ब्रह्मेत्योङ्कारः, शब्दाधिकारे अन्यस्यासम्भवात्), as does Kūranārāyaṇa (प्रणवेन). It is a different use of the word from ‘tad brahma’ in the first unit and ‘sa veda brahma’ at the end.
Does the anuvāka stand alone?
‘Who knows them knows Brahman’ seems complete, yet Brahman was already named. Śaṅkara argues that this anuvāka and the next teach a single meditation: the next describes the Brahman to be known here (उभयोर्ह्यनुवाकयोरेकमुपासनम्).

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Ethical leadership

The passage describes a whole whose centre does not replace its parts but makes them ‘grow great’: the sun does not abolish the worlds, food does not compete with the breaths. Leaders often think of the centre of an organisation as the place where decisions are taken away from the parts. The image here is different: the centre is judged by whether the parts flourish because of it. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

Uncertainties recorded

  • variant Madhva’s text writes svar for the Taittirīya suvar (corrections.json, orthographic); the Sāyaṇa-pāṭha writes a visarga before a sibilant as that sibilant. Neither changes the meaning.
  • interpretation The identification of the Brahman meditated on here (Hiraṇyagarbha, the supreme, or Viṣṇu in four forms) is a matter of school; the text names it only as ‘mahaḥ … Brahman … the self’.
