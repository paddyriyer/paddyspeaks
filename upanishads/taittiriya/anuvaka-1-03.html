Taittirīya Upaniṣad · Śīkṣāvallī · Anuvāka 3 of 12

The great joinings: learning to see the world in the joining of two sounds

The point where two sounds meet in recitation becomes a pattern for meditation: two terms, the junction between them and what joins them, found in the worlds, the lights, learning, family and the speaking body.

‹ Anuvāka 1.2All 12Anuvāka 1.4 ›

1 Original Sanskrit

सह नौ यशः ।सह नौ ब्रह्मवर्चसम् ।अथातः संहिताया उपनिषदं व्याख्यास्यामः ।पञ्चस्वधिकरणेषु ।अधिलोकमधिज्यौतिषमधिविद्यमधिप्रजमध्यात्मम् ।ता महासंहिता इत्याचक्षते ।अथाधिलोकम् ।पृथिवी पूर्वरूपम् ।द्यौरुत्तररूपम् ।आकाशः सन्धिः ॥ १ ॥

वायुः सन्धानम् ।इत्यधिलोकम् ।अथाधिज्यौतिषम् ।अग्निः पूर्वरूपम् ।आदित्य उत्तररूपम् ।आपः सन्धिः ।वैद्युतः सन्धानम् ।इत्यधिज्यौतिषम् ।अथाधिविद्यम् ।आचार्यः पूर्वरूपम् ॥ २ ॥

अन्तेवास्युत्तररूपम् ।विद्या सन्धिः ।प्रवचनं सन्धानम् ।इत्यधिविद्यम् ।अथाधिप्रजम् ।माता पूर्वरूपम् ।पितोत्तररूपम् ।प्रजा सन्धिः ।प्रजननं सन्धानम् ।इत्यधिप्रजम् ॥ ३ ॥

अथाध्यात्मम् ।अधरा हनुः पूर्वरूपम् ।उत्तरा हनुरुत्तररूपम् ।वाक्सन्धिः ।जिह्वा सन्धानम् ।इत्यध्यात्मम् ।इतीमा महासंहिताः ।य एवमेता महासंहिता व्याख्याता वेद ।सन्धीयते प्रजया पशुभिः ।ब्रह्मवर्चसेनान्नाद्येन सुवर्गेण लोकेन ॥ ४ ॥

Accented text · Taittirīya Āraṇyaka, Sāyaṇa-pāṭha, as the witness keys it

स॒ह नौ॒ यशः । स॒ह नौ ब्र॑ह्मव॒र्चसम् । अथातस्सँहिताया उपनिषदं व्या॑ख्यास्या॒मः । पञ्चस्वधिक॑रणे॒षु । अधिलोक मधिज्यौतिष मधिविद्य मधिप्रज॑ मध्या॒त्मम् । ता महासँहिता इ॑त्याच॒क्षते । अथा॑धिलो॒कम् । पृथिवी पू॑र्वरू॒पम् । द्यौरुत्त॑ररू॒पम् । आका॑शस्स॒न्धिः ॥

वायु॑स्सन्धा॒नम् । इत्य॑धिलो॒कम् । अथा॑धिज्यौ॒तिषम् । अग्निः पू॑र्वरू॒पम् । आदित्य उत्त॑ररू॒पम् । आ॑पस्स॒न्धिः । वैद्युत॑स्सन्धा॒नम् । इत्य॑धिज्यौ॒तिषम् । अथा॑धिवि॒द्यम् । आचार्यः पू॑र्वरू॒पम् ॥

अन्तेवास्युत्त॑ररू॒पम् । वि॑द्या स॒न्धिः । प्रवचनँ॑ सन्धा॒नम् । इत्य॑धिवि॒द्यम् । अथाधि॒प्रजम् । माता पू॑र्वरू॒पम् । पितोत्त॑ररू॒पम् । प्र॑जा स॒न्धिः । प्रजननँ॑ सन्धा॒नम् । इत्यधि॒प्रजम् ॥

अथाध्या॒त्मम् । अधरा हनुः पू॑र्वरू॒पम् । उत्तरा हनुरुत्त॑ररू॒पम् । वाक्स॒न्धिः । जिह्वा॑ सन्धा॒नम् । इत्यध्या॒त्मम् । इतीमा म॑हासँ॒हिताः । य एवमेता महासँहिता व्याख्या॑ता वे॒द । सन्धीयते प्रज॑या प॒शुभिः । ब्रह्मवर्चसेनान्नाद्येन सुवर्ग्येण॑ लोके॒न ॥

Svara is shown only from this witness and only as it is keyed; it is not proof-read or reconstructed. This witness reads differently at this anuvāka; see section 14.

6 Readable English an interpretive rendering, not a literal translation

1.3.1 May renown come to both of us together; may the lustre of sacred learning come to both of us together. Now, then, we will explain the hidden teaching of the joining, in five areas: the worlds, the lights, learning, offspring and the person. These are called the great joinings. First, the worlds: the earth is the first sound, the sky the second, the space between them the junction,

1.3.2 and the wind is what joins them. So much for the worlds. Next, the lights: fire is the first sound, the sun the second, the waters the junction, lightning what joins them. So much for the lights. Next, learning: the teacher is the first sound,

1.3.3 the pupil the second, learning the junction, teaching what joins them. So much for learning. Next, offspring: the mother is the first sound, the father the second, the child the junction, procreation what joins them. So much for offspring.

1.3.4 Last, the person: the lower jaw is the first sound, the upper jaw the second, speech the junction, the tongue what joins them. These are the great joinings. Whoever meditates on them as they have been explained is joined with children and cattle, with the radiance of learning, with food, and with the heavenly world.

7 Explain it to a beginner

When we speak quickly, words run together: two sounds meet and make a third. Teachers of recitation call that meeting a joining. This passage turns the joining into a way of looking at the world. Every joining has four parts: a first sound, a second sound, the place where they meet, and what brings them together. Now look at the earth and the sky: between them is space, and the wind connects them. Look at a teacher and a student: between them is learning, and teaching connects them. Look at a mother and a father: between them is a child, joined through birth. Even in your own mouth, the lower and upper jaw meet in speech, moved by the tongue. Practising this way of seeing, the passage promises, brings family, plenty and a good life.

An analogy Like a bridge: two banks, the river between them, and the span that joins them. Once you see that shape you start to see it everywhere — between people, between ideas, between generations.

8 Traditional philosophical commentary
Direct Textual Meaning

What does the passage actually say?

After a wish that renown and the lustre of sacred knowledge come to ‘us two’ together, it announces the ‘upaniṣad of the saṃhitā’ in five domains and sets out each in four terms: in the worlds, earth, sky, space, wind; in the lights, fire, sun, waters, lightning; in learning, teacher, pupil, knowledge, teaching; in offspring, mother, father, child, procreation; in the person, lower jaw, upper jaw, speech, tongue. It calls these the great saṃhitās and promises that whoever ‘knows’ them is joined with offspring, cattle, lustre, food and the heavenly world. It does not say what one is to do with the correspondences; that is the commentators’ question.

Comparative Vedānta

Why do student and teacher pray for renown together?

Śaṅkara takes the wish as the student’s: the teacher has already reached his goal, so a prayer from him would make no sense (कृतार्थो ह्याचार्यो नाम भवति). Kūranārāyaṇa makes it the teacher’s, with an image: when a daughter is given in marriage, the children belong to the bridegroom’s family alone — यथा कस्मैचिद्वराय प्रतिपादितायां कन्यायां पुत्रादिकं फलं तस्यैव वरस्य भवति — and the teacher asks that it not be so with knowledge. Rāmānanda distinguishes this prayer from the opening ‘śaṃ no mitraḥ’: that belonged to the whole Upaniṣad, this one to the meditation on the saṃhitā (सम्प्रति संहितोपनिषदङ्गभूता प्रार्थना क्रियते).

Traditional Interpretation

Why begin with meditations on sound rather than on Brahman?

Śaṅkara: a mind long shaped by memorising the text cannot be brought all at once to the knowledge of meaning, so the Upaniṣad first gives a way of seeing that stays close to the text — यतोऽत्यर्थं ग्रन्थभाविता बुद्धिर्न शक्यते सहसार्थज्ञानविषयेऽवतारयितुमित्यतः. Rāmānanda gives the same reason in his own words: the mind first takes hold of the form of the words and only then passes to their meaning (शब्दस्वरूपे सङ्क्रान्ता बुद्धिरर्थस्वरूपे क्रमेणैव सङ्क्रामति). The anuvāka is a bridge from recitation to reflection.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

What exactly is meditated on?

Śaṅkara: on the prior sound of a joining one is to place the view ‘earth’ — संहितायाः पूर्वे वर्णे पृथिवीदृष्टिः कर्तव्येत्युक्तं भवति — and so on for each term. Rāmānanda makes the pattern concrete with the sandhi a + u → o: तत्राकारः पूर्वरूपम्, the u the latter form, the o the junction ओकारः सन्धिर्द्वयोर्वर्णयोः सन्निकर्षरूपत्वादोकारस्य, and the effort that produces it the connector. Kūranārāyaṇa identifies that effort with breath: the wind is ‘the effort for pronouncing the prior and latter together’ (पूर्वोत्तरयोः सहोच्चारणार्थो यो यत्नः स वायुरिति वेदितव्यः). Rāmānanda adds that the saṃhitā meant is that of one’s own branch of the Veda (सेयं संहिता स्वशाखासम्बद्धैव ग्राह्या).

Traditional Interpretation

What does ‘upāsanā’ mean for Śaṅkara?

Śaṅkara uses the word veda in the last sentence to define meditation exactly: a continuous flow of the same idea, as scripture prescribes, unmixed with other ideas, resting on a support the scripture gives — उपासनं च यथाशास्त्रं तुल्यप्रत्ययसन्ततिरसङ्कीर्णा च अतत्प्रत्ययैः शास्त्रोक्तालम्बनविषया च. He compares the everyday sense: one ‘attends on’ a teacher or a king by serving them continually, and gains the fruit of that service.

Traditional Interpretation

Is the promised fruit the point?

The text promises offspring, cattle, lustre, food and heaven, and Śaṅkara simply restates it: प्रजादिफलं प्राप्नोतीत्यर्थः. Rāmānanda distinguishes two practitioners: one who meditates without desire attains the knowledge of Brahman, one who meditates with desire gets the stated fruit — एतासां पञ्चानां संहितोपनिषदामनुष्ठानेन निष्कामस्य उपासितुः ब्रह्मविद्यानिष्पत्तिः भवति. Kūranārāyaṇa reasons that since knowing the joinings has no visible result of its own, the explanation is meant for meditation (अत्र संहिताज्ञाने दृष्टान्यफलादर्शनात् संहिताव्याख्यानमिदम् उपासनार्थम्).

9 Comparative Vedānta
Comparative Vedānta

Advaita · Śaṅkara, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Śīkṣāvallī 3

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

For Śaṅkara the anuvāka belongs to the region of meditation and its fruits, not of liberating knowledge. It is a graded step: the mind trained on the text is led to a way of seeing ‘close to the text’ (ग्रन्थसन्निकृष्टामेव व्याख्यास्यामः), and the word ‘knows’ is read as meditation. Its fruits — offspring to heaven — are stated without reinterpretation.

Viśiṣṭādvaita · Kūranārāyaṇa; Rāmānanda, commentaries on Śīkṣāvallī 3 (six-commentary edition)

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Kūranārāyaṇa reads the opening wish as the teacher’s and the five joinings as meditations. Rāmānanda reads the fruit two ways: for one who meditates without desire it is the knowledge of Brahman (निष्कामस्य उपासितुः ब्रह्मविद्यानिष्पत्तिः भवति), for one with desire the fruit the text names. Kūranārāyaṇa’s opening verse (printed before 1.1) says that in the Sāṃhitī too the Lord of all is taught as ‘the one who joins’: सांहितीष्वपि सन्धाता सर्वेशः प्रतिपाद्यते. The word sandhātā echoes this anuvāka’s sandhāna; it suggests that for him the connector of every joining is finally the Lord. That is an inference from the verse — his comment on this anuvāka does not say it.

Dvaita · Madhva, Taittirīyopaniṣad-bhāṣya, Śīkṣāvallī 3

From the school’s commentary on this anuvāka

Madhva’s comment on this anuvāka is a single line: in the five, the worlds and the rest, are the forms of Nārāyaṇa and others — नारायणादिरूपाणि लोकादिषु च पञ्चसु. For him the terms of each joining are to be meditated on as forms of the Lord present in them. The line is too short to say how each correspondence is read.

Where they disagree

All agree that the anuvāka teaches meditations on the joining of sounds and that its fruits are worldly as stated. They differ on who speaks the opening wish (the student for Śaṅkara, the teacher for Kūranārāyaṇa), and on what the terms of the joinings ultimately are: mental supports placed on the sounds (Śaṅkara), meditations that can ripen into knowledge of Brahman when done without desire (Rāmānanda), or forms of Nārāyaṇa (Madhva).

Morphology

1.3.1 नौ
The enclitic dual of the first-person pronoun, ‘of us two’: the wish is made for a pair, student and teacher.
1.3.1 अधिलोकम्
The five headings are avyayībhāva compounds of adhi with a noun, ‘in respect of …’. Kūranārāyaṇa and Rāmānanda name the rule, P. 2.1.6 (अव्ययं विभक्तिसमीपसमृद्धिव्यृद्ध्यर्थाभावात्ययासम्प्रतिशब्दप्रादुर्भावपश्चाद्यथाऽऽनुपूर्व्ययौगपद्यसादृश्यसम्पत्तिसाकल्यान्तवचनेषु), the indeclinable used ‘in the sense of a case ending’. Rāmānanda adds that adhyātmam takes the suffix ṭac by P. 5.4.108 (अनश्च).
1.3.1 आचक्षते
Present middle of cakṣ with ā, ‘they call’. The subject is unexpressed: ‘people, those who know’.
1.3.1 द्यौः
Nominative singular of div / dyo, ‘sky’, feminine here.
1.3.2 आपः
ap, ‘water’, is plural in use: āpaḥ, ‘the waters’.
1.3.2 वैद्युतः
A vṛddhi derivative of vidyut, ‘lightning’: ‘belonging to lightning’; Śaṅkara does not gloss it.
1.3.3 अन्तेवासी
Nominative of antevāsin; the first member keeps its locative ending (ante, ‘near’).
1.3.3 प्रजननम्
Action noun from jan with pra, ‘begetting’. Kūranārāyaṇa takes it concretely.
1.3.4 हनुः
hanu, ‘jaw’, is feminine here, as adharā and uttarā show.
1.3.4 वेद
Perfect of vid with present meaning, ‘knows’. Śaṅkara argues that here it means upāsanā, meditation: वेदेत्युपासनं स्यात्.
1.3.4 सन्धीयते
Passive of dhā with sam, the very verb of the five joinings (sandhi, sandhāna): one who meditates on the joinings ‘is joined’ with their fruits.
1.3.4 सुवर्गेण
suvarga is the Taittirīya form of svarga, ‘heaven’. The Sāyaṇa-pāṭha and Madhva’s text read suvargyeṇa, ‘leading to heaven’ (corrections.json).

Anvaya

Prose already in prose order; no anvaya is given.

Syntax

The anuvāka opens with a two-line wish without a verb (‘together for us two [be] renown … lustre’). Then a programmatic sentence with the future vyākhyāsyāmaḥ names the topic and lists five domains as indeclinable headings. Each domain is then set out in the same frame: ‘atha’ + heading; four nominal sentences of the form ‘X [is] the prior form / the latter form / the junction / the connector’; ‘iti’ + heading. The close is a relative sentence of fruit, ‘whoever knows (veda) … is joined (sandhīyate) with …’, with five instrumentals.

Ambiguities

Who speaks the opening wish?
Śaṅkara: the student, because only the student still has something to gain — शिष्यस्य हि अकृतार्थत्वात्प्रार्थनोपपद्यते. Kūranārāyaṇa: the teacher, who, about to give the knowledge, wishes its fruit to belong to both — अथ शिष्याय विद्यां प्रदित्सुराचार्यो विद्याफलं शिष्यस्य स्वस्य च साधारणं भवत्वित्याशास्ते. The dual nau allows either.
What is a ‘saṃhitā’ here?
Not the collection of Vedic hymns but the joining of sounds in continuous recitation (P. 1.4.109 (परः संनिकर्षः संहिता), which Gopālānanda cites). Rāmānanda’s example is the joining a + u = o: the a is the prior form, the u the latter, the o the junction, and the effort that produces it the connector. The ‘great saṃhitās’ apply this fourfold pattern to larger things.
‘knows’ or ‘meditates’?
veda ordinarily means ‘knows’. Śaṅkara argues that here it must mean meditation (upāsanā), since the context is one of practice and a parallel passage says ‘meditate’ (वेदेत्युपासनं स्यात्). Kūranārāyaṇa agrees that the explanation is for the sake of meditation.
suvargeṇa or suvargyeṇa
The base text and Śaṅkara’s mūla read suvargeṇa lokena, ‘with the heavenly world’; the Sāyaṇa-pāṭha and Madhva’s text read suvargyeṇa, ‘[with the world] leading to heaven’ (corrections.json, class variant). The sense differs little.

12 Contemporary relevance
Contemporary Reflection

Theme Ethical leadership

The passage places the teacher and the pupil on the same footing as earth and sky: two terms that only make sense with the space between them and the act that connects them. Leaders often think of themselves as the first term and others as the second. The passage asks about the junction and the connector — what actually passes between people, and what makes it pass. In a team, that is shared knowledge and the work of explaining. This is a modern reflection on the passage, not part of its traditional meaning.

13 Reflect and contemplate

A question to begin Pick two people you know well. What is the ‘space between’ you, and what keeps you connected?

Go deeper

A philosophical inquiry Why might a text use the joining of two sounds as a pattern for the worlds, learning and family? What does that say about how it regards speech?

A contemplation exercise Say a phrase slowly where two words run together. Notice the first sound, the second, where they meet, and the movement of the tongue that joins them.

An invitation to attend, not a treatment; no medical or psychological claim is made.
