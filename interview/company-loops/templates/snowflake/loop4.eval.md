# Snowflake · Loop 4 — evaluator notes (hidden from candidates)

Product rules quoted in these scenarios (in-region sharing without copies,
replication for other regions, micro-partition sizes, result reuse windows,
time-travel and fail-safe periods) are this exercise's rules, stated from public
documentation in general terms; candidates should not be marked down for not
knowing them, and the notes below never describe how any company builds its
systems.

## SNO-L4-01

**Reference architecture (one defensible design; score others on the criteria).**
- **Grant model**: a share is a named object in the provider account holding grants (usage on a database and schema, select on specific tables and secure views) plus a list of consumer accounts. Consumers mount it as a read-only database. Grants and the consumer list live in a control-plane catalogue; queries resolve them at compile time on every query, so a change applies to the next query without touching data.
- **Row entitlements**: one secure view per shared dataset that joins the fact tables to an entitlement table (`consumer_account_id, market, tier, valid_from, valid_to`) on the querying account's immutable identifier (a function such as "current account" evaluated in the consumer's session), filtering to rows the account is entitled to on the current date. The view definition is hidden from consumers, and the optimiser must not push consumer predicates below the filter in a way that leaks rows through errors or timing (the reason secure views exist). Contract amendments are rows in the entitlement table with effective dates, not new shares.
- **Remote regions**: the provider keeps a provider-owned account per region; the shared database is replicated there on a schedule that meets the freshness promise (a refresh every few minutes, so lag stays under {{refresh}}), and shared locally from that account. Replication copies only files added since the last refresh plus metadata. Consumers in that region never read across regions.
- **Revocation**: the authoritative consumer list and entitlement table are control-plane state. Revocation must not wait for data replication: either the replica region's share lists are managed directly (an API call per region from a provider-side entitlement service), or entitlement data is replicated on its own fast path with a deny list checked at compile time in every region. Running queries finish (they started under valid access) or are cancelled, a stated choice; result caches must re-check privileges on every reuse.
- **Audit**: grant changes are an append-only log (who, what, when, old and new). Access history records per query: consumer account, objects and columns read, time, row counts, never query text visible to the provider. Kept two years; "could read" is reconstructed from the grant log and entitlement history as of a date; "did read" from access history.
- **Cost**: in-region sharing costs the provider nothing extra beyond storage; replication costs compute and transfer proportional to changed bytes per refresh times number of regions. Rewrite-heavy operations (re-clustering, large updates, full reloads) multiply transfer and must be planned per region.

**Competing designs to accept.** Per-consumer shares with table-level grants for a small number of consumers (simple, does not scale to tens of thousands, and entitlement changes become share changes); per-tier shares where tiers are few; a marketplace listing with automatic fulfilment to regions instead of hand-managed replicas; physically separate per-consumer extracts where the contract demands isolation, with honest discussion of the copy and revocation consequences.

**Bottlenecks and failure modes.** Replication lag after rewrites; grants that travel only with replicated data; entitlement keys that are mutable (names); optimiser leaks through non-secure views; result caches that skip privilege checks; audit that cannot reconstruct "could read" because the grant log was not kept; transfer cost growing with regions times churn.

**Incident guidance.**
- *Revocation lags {{refresh}} in a replica region*: confirm the window (access history in that region for the revoked account after the revocation time), remove the account from the replica's share directly now, and tell the provider's legal contact what was read in the window. Prevention: revocation as control-plane state applied to every region independently of data refresh, with a check that queries each region's share lists after any revocation and alerts on mismatch.
- *Re-cluster rewrites every file*: the change set for the next refresh is the whole table. Pause or slow automatic replication for that database, or replicate the rewrite in planned stages per region, and tell remote consumers their data is stale while it catches up. Prevention: a pre-flight estimate of changed bytes for large maintenance operations, run them in a window, budget alerts on transfer, and consider whether the re-cluster is worth N regions' transfer.
- *Entitlement keyed by renamed account*: restore access by updating the entitlement rows to the account's stable identifier; audit every other entitlement row for name keys. Prevention: key entitlements by an immutable account locator or identifier, a test that renames a test account and expects unchanged results, and a monitor on consumers who suddenly see zero rows.

**Follow-up guidance.** For the first-query walk-through, expect: consumer mounts the database, compile resolves grants and the secure view, the new table is visible only after the next refresh in that region, so it either does not exist yet or is empty for up to {{refresh}}. For midnight revocation, push for exact behaviour per region and for running queries. For the auditor, the difference between "could read" (grant and entitlement history as of a date) and "did read" (access history).

**Rubric anchors.** 2: copies data per consumer or treats sharing as an export; revocation by deleting copies; no entitlement model. 3: in-region sharing without copies, secure view with an entitlement table, per-region replicas from changed files, revocation applied centrally, access history without query text. 4: also revocation independent of replication with verification in every region, immutable account keys, a changed-bytes cost model with plans for rewrites, running-query and cache semantics stated, and crisp incident handling.

**Strong signals.** Asks what "access stops" means for replicas and caches before designing; separates "could read" from "did read". **Red flags.** Revocation that waits for the next data refresh without comment; entitlements enforced in the consumer's application; giving providers consumer query text "for analytics".

## SNO-L4-02

**Reference architecture (one defensible design; score others on the criteria).**
- **Statistics per file and column**: minimum, maximum, null count, row count, optional distinct-count sketch for planning, and for strings a truncated minimum and a truncated-then-rounded-up maximum (or a flag that the maximum is unbounded). Written in the same commit as the file: the commit record lists added and removed files with their statistics, so a reader never sees a file without statistics.
- **Storage and access**: the metadata service stores file statistics column-wise per table, partitioned by table and ordered by the clustering key's minimum, with an interval structure (sorted by min, plus the running maximum of max, or an interval tree) so the compiler finds overlapping files for a range in logarithmic time plus output. Compile reads only the columns in the predicate. Hot tables' statistics are cached by compile nodes in compressed form; very large tables use a two-level scheme: summaries over groups of files (min of mins, max of maxes) prune groups before individual files are read.
- **Pruning rules**: a range or equality predicate on a column prunes a file when the predicate's interval does not intersect [min, max]; IS NULL uses null count; prefix LIKE becomes a range; monotonic functions (cast to date, date truncation) are mapped to the column's range; non-monotonic expressions do not prune. NULLs are excluded from min and max and handled by null count. Truncated maximum: store the prefix with its last byte incremented (with carry), so the stored max is ≥ every value; truncated minimum: the prefix itself is ≤ every value. Joins: runtime pruning with the build side's key range or a filter pushed into the probe side's scan.
- **Clustering metric**: overlap depth at a point is the number of files whose [min, max] for the key contains it; average depth over the range's breakpoints (or a histogram of depth) measures clustering. Maintained incrementally: inserting a file adds +1 over its interval, removing it adds −1 (a segment structure or bucketed counts over the key domain).
- **Reclustering service**: periodically finds key ranges with the highest depth weighted by how often queries filter there (from query history), picks the files overlapping those ranges, sorts and rewrites them into non-overlapping files, and commits with optimistic concurrency (if a concurrent DML removed an input, the commit fails and is retried or dropped). Budget: estimate benefit as bytes scanned saved per day by queries on that range, cost as bytes rewritten; run only where benefit exceeds cost, stop at {{recluster_budget}} of the tenant's query compute. Leave the newest range alone until loads in it settle, since recent data keeps changing.

**Competing designs to accept.** Requiring an explicit clustering key and sorting within each load batch only (cheaper, less adaptive); zone maps plus per-file bloom filters for high-cardinality equality lookups such as {{filter_col}} = constant; a separate search-optimisation-style index for point lookups at extra storage cost, if the candidate prices it; hierarchical summaries stored in the file storage itself instead of a metadata service, if compile latency is addressed.

**Bottlenecks and failure modes.** Metadata reads that scale with total files rather than overlapping files; statistics not committed atomically; incorrect truncation; arbitrary-order loads destroying clustering; reclustering thrash on recent ranges; rewrites inflating time travel, replication and caches; concurrency conflicts between DML and reclustering.

**Incident guidance.**
- *Arbitrary-order loads collapse pruning*: depth monitoring per table should alarm (depth on {{filter_col}} rising week over week, pruning ratio falling). Recover by reclustering the affected range (one-off cost, tell the tenant), and fix the loader to sort within each batch or by the key. Prevention: per-table pruning-ratio and depth dashboards, alerts to the tenant, and load-time light sorting by the clustering key.
- *Truncated maximum stored as-is*: this is a correctness incident, not a performance one. Disable string pruning on affected columns immediately (correct but slower), recompute statistics for files written since the release, identify affected queries from history (string predicates on columns with values longer than 16 bytes), and tell customers which results may have been incomplete. Prevention: a property test that compares pruned and unpruned results on randomised data, including long shared prefixes, as a release gate.
- *Recluster thrash on a trickle*: the service keeps reclustering the newest range because new files keep raising its depth. Exclude ranges still receiving loads (or a time window), use a hysteresis threshold, and cap rewrites per range per day. Show the tenant the cost and benefit; refund or credit if the policy was at fault. Prevention: benefit-per-byte scoring and a budget circuit breaker.

**Follow-up guidance.** On the walk-through, a strong answer touches only the statistics of {{filter_col}} for files overlapping the day (interval lookup), not every file. On depth, expect a definition, not "how sorted it is". On the join follow-up, expect dynamic pruning from the build side's key range or values.

**Rubric anchors.** 2: "add an index" or "partition by date" with no per-file statistics; truncation not considered. 3: per-file min/max/null count committed with files, correct pruning rules for ranges and NULLs, a depth metric, a background reclustering service. 4: also correct truncated-string bounds, scalable metadata access (interval structure, summaries, caching), incremental depth maintenance, a cost-benefit budget with recent-range policy, dynamic join pruning, and crisp incidents.

**Strong signals.** States the no-false-negative invariant first; prices reclustering in rewritten bytes including history and replicas. **Red flags.** Pruning that can skip a matching file "rarely"; a global sort of the table on every load; ignoring that statistics must be committed with the files.

## SNO-L4-03

**Reference architecture (one defensible design; score others on the criteria).**
- **Result cache key**: tenant id; normalised query text (whitespace and comments removed outside literals; keyword case folded; literals and identifiers with quoting rules preserved); the version id of every table, view and function read (after view expansion); the effective security context: the set of row access and masking policies applied and their versions, and the role's privileges on each object (or a hash of the resolved security context); session settings that change semantics (time zone, date and timestamp formats, case-sensitivity settings, current database and schema used for name resolution). Queries containing non-deterministic or context functions (current time, random, current user unless part of the key, external functions) are marked uncacheable at compile.
- **Validity**: a cached entry is valid while every version in its key is still current; lookup computes the key from current metadata and finds an exact match. No invalidation messages are needed; stale entries simply stop matching and are evicted. Shared data from another account carries the provider's table versions, so the consumer's key changes when the provider commits.
- **Security on reuse**: privileges are re-checked at reuse even when the key matches (the key includes the security context, and a revocation changes it).
- **Storage**: results stored in object storage per tenant, encrypted per tenant, with an index (key → result location, size, last used). Eviction by the 24-hour-after-last-use rule (up to 31 days from first run, this exercise's rule) and a per-tenant byte budget with least-recently-used eviction.
- **Materialised views**: each view records the base table version it reflects. A maintenance service consumes the base table's change sets (added and removed files since that version) and applies them incrementally where the aggregate allows (SUM, COUNT; MIN and MAX on inserts; deletes force recomputation of the affected groups for MIN, MAX and COUNT(DISTINCT) unless auxiliary state is kept). It schedules refreshes by lag and query demand, at the tenant's cost.
- **Query rewrite**: the optimiser matches a base-table query to a view whose definition subsumes it. Then, per query: if the view is current, answer from it; if behind but within the staleness bound, answer from it and report the lag; if outside, either combine the view with an aggregate over the base table's unprocessed changes (when the aggregate is mergeable and there are no deletes) or run on the base table.

**Competing designs to accept.** Timed expiry with explicit invalidation on commit for systems without version ids (accept if they handle the race between commit and cache write); caching at the dashboard tool's layer in addition, with the security caveat; semantic caching of partial results (per-partition aggregates) for higher hit rates under frequent loads, if correctness is argued; refusing to answer from a stale view at all when the bound is "always current" and using base-plus-delta only.

**Bottlenecks and failure modes.** Missing security or session context in the key; non-deterministic functions not detected; low hit rate with frequent loads; per-tenant storage growth; views that fall behind after bulk changes and push all traffic onto base tables; MIN/MAX/COUNT(DISTINCT) under deletes; maintenance cost exceeding savings.

**Incident guidance.**
- *Row access policy tightened, cached rows still served*: a security incident. Purge the tenant's cached results for affected tables immediately (or bump a tenant-wide cache epoch), identify from the cache index which users received results computed under the old policy, and support the tenant's notification. Prevention: policy and privilege versions in the key plus a privilege re-check on reuse, and a release test that changes a policy and asserts a cache miss.
- *Time zone not in the key*: add semantic session settings to the key; purge affected entries; find queries using date functions served across differing time zones. Prevention: a list of semantics-changing session parameters maintained with the parser, and a test per parameter.
- *View falls behind after a 30% correction*: traffic at {{qps}} moves to the base table. Short term: scale the warehouse or queue non-critical dashboards, let the view fully rebuild (often cheaper than incremental for 30% churn), and serve within-bound stale results where the product promise allows. Prevention: maintenance that switches to full rebuild when change volume exceeds a threshold, a lag alarm, and scheduling large corrections with view maintenance in mind.

**Follow-up guidance.** For two analysts with different roles, the key differs when their security contexts differ, so each gets its own entry unless the effective policies are identical. For hit rate with loads every {{load_interval}}, expect an estimate (queries per table version) and options: align dashboard refresh to load cadence, cache partial aggregates per immutable file group, or offer a stated staleness mode.

**Rubric anchors.** 2: cache keyed by query text with a timed expiry; security not considered. 3: key with table versions and security context, version-based validity, non-determinism handled, incremental view maintenance for simple aggregates, staleness-aware rewrite. 4: also semantic session settings, deletes and non-mergeable aggregates handled, base-plus-delta answers, per-tenant storage and eviction, cost accounting of views against savings, and crisp incidents.

**Strong signals.** Starts from "what does the result depend on"; treats the cache as a security boundary. **Red flags.** Sharing results across tenants or roles by text alone; "invalidate the whole cache on any write"; views that silently serve stale data beyond the bound.

## SNO-L4-04

**Reference architecture (one defensible design; score others on the criteria).**
- **Versions**: each commit writes a version record (table id, version number, commit time, statement id, files added, files removed). The current file set is reconstructed from a periodic checkpoint plus subsequent deltas. AT a timestamp or BEFORE a statement resolves to a version by binary search on commit time or a lookup by statement id, then the file set for that version from the nearest checkpoint.
- **Drop and UNDROP**: dropping marks the object dropped with a time and keeps its version chain; the name is freed. UNDROP restores the most recent dropped object with that name if the name is free; if a new object has the name, UNDROP fails with a clear error and the user renames the new object first (state the rule). Schema and database UNDROP restore the container and the children dropped with it, in one metadata transaction.
- **Clones**: a clone gets a new table id whose first version references the source's files at the chosen point; afterwards the two have independent version chains. Files are shared, not copied.
- **Garbage collection**: a file is deletable when, for every table (source and clones) whose version chain contains it, the version that removed it is older than retention plus fail-safe, and no current version contains it. Implement as reachability: periodically compute the set of files reachable from any version inside any window across all tables in the account (clones can cross databases), and delete unreachable files that also passed a grace period. Alternatively crash-safe reference counts updated in the same transaction as the version record, with a periodic reachability audit that never deletes a reachable file. Deletions are logged.
- **Fail-safe**: after retention, files and version records move to a fail-safe state that users cannot query; operator recovery restores them. Transient and temporary tables skip fail-safe.
- **Storage accounting**: per table per day, bytes of files in the current version (active), files only reachable from versions inside retention (time travel), and files past retention in fail-safe. Shared files between a source and its clones are charged to the table that owns them (for example the source while it references them; then transferred to the remaining clone), with a stated rule so totals never double count.
- **Customer controls**: per-object retention, transient tables for staging and reload targets, alerts when time-travel plus fail-safe bytes exceed a multiple of active bytes, and a per-table report showing which jobs cause churn.

**Competing designs to accept.** Snapshot-based retention (daily snapshots as file lists) instead of every version, if time travel granularity is stated; log-structured metadata with periodic compaction; per-table GC with an explicit cross-table reference registry for clones (only if clones are registered and checked).

**Bottlenecks and failure modes.** Version metadata growing with churn (checkpoints needed); GC that misses cross-table references; GC races with clone creation (a clone of a version that is just leaving retention); truncate-and-reload multiplying history; UNDROP name conflicts; retention changes that surprise customers; erasure requests against immutable history.

**Incident guidance.**
- *Truncate-and-reload bill jump*: with full daily rewrites, each day's files stay for {{retention_days}} days plus fail-safe, so history reaches roughly retention plus fail-safe days' worth of the table for permanent tables. Show the customer the per-table breakdown, recommend transient tables or shorter retention for reload targets, or incremental loads. Prevention: proactive alerts on the history-to-active ratio and on truncate-and-reload patterns in query history.
- *GC deletes files a clone still uses*: stop GC immediately; recover deleted files from fail-safe or storage-level versioning if available; identify affected clones; tell customers. Prevention: GC must compute reachability across all tables in the account, a dry-run comparison against the previous algorithm before release, a grace period between "unreachable" and "deleted", and a canary that clones, drops and checks.
- *UNDROP blocked by recreated names*: rename the 40 new tables (or restore the dropped ones under new names, if the system allows), then UNDROP; verify row counts against the dropped versions. Prevention: UNDROP with a rename option, warnings when an automated job recreates a recently dropped name, and access controls that make dropping a production schema require a separate role.

**Follow-up guidance.** For the deletion-request follow-up, a strong answer is honest: the rows remain in time travel and fail-safe until those windows pass; options are shorter retention for personal-data tables, crypto-shredding with per-subject or per-partition keys, or rewriting history where the platform supports it; and the customer communicates timelines, without legal conclusions from the candidate.

**Rubric anchors.** 2: "keep backups" or full copies per day; no GC model. 3: versions as file lists, time travel by version lookup, clones as shared references, GC that respects retention and fail-safe, storage split by state. 4: also cross-table reachability with crash safety and grace periods, clear name-conflict and attribution rules, customer-facing cost controls and alerts, an honest erasure answer, and crisp incidents.

**Strong signals.** States the GC invariant before the algorithm; estimates history bytes from churn. **Red flags.** Copying data for clones or time travel; GC per table without clones; promising deletion within days while history remains.

## SNO-L4-05

**Reference architecture (one defensible design; score others on the criteria).**
- **Raw sources**: marketplace events (listing page view, request, get, trial start, conversion, cancellation) with account and organisation ids; access records from consumer queries on shared objects (record id, consumer account, share or listing, objects and columns read, time, rows scanned), with no query text. Both land in an internal ingestion layer keyed by record id.
- **Provider-facing model**:
  - `listing_daily` (listing, day): views, distinct viewing accounts (aggregate only), requests, gets, trials started, conversions — browsing columns suppressed below {{min_group}} distinct accounts.
  - `consumer_usage_daily` (listing, consumer account, day): queries, distinct users count bucketed or omitted, objects and columns accessed, rows scanned — identified, because the consumer obtained the listing.
  - `object_usage_daily` (listing, object, column, day): query counts and distinct consumer accounts.
  - Browsing breakdowns (region, industry) only on a fixed set of published cuts, each suppressed below {{min_group}}, with complementary suppression so a hidden cell cannot be recovered from a total minus visible cells.
- **Privacy rules**: no query text, predicates, literals or joined consumer tables ever enter the model (schema-level allowlist, plus a scanner that fails the pipeline on unexpected long-text fields). Browsing data has no free filtering: providers get pre-aggregated tables at fixed grain, not raw events. Day-level grain only; no sub-day times for browsing. Optionally noise on browsing counts that are never used for billing; usage counts that providers bill on are exact and identified.
- **Delivery**: per provider, a share of secure views over the model filtered to the provider's listings, refreshed within {{freshness}}; providers query with their own compute, so the platform does not see their analysis queries beyond normal operation.
- **Late and replayed data**: idempotent ingestion keyed by record id; daily partitions are versioned; restatements publish a new version with a changelog row and notice to the provider; billing-relevant tables say which version a number came from.

**Competing designs to accept.** Differential privacy for all browsing metrics with a per-provider budget (if usable numbers and budget accounting are discussed); a clean-room style where providers run approved aggregate queries against protected tables, with query templates and thresholds enforced; publishing browsing only at listing level with no breakdowns at all (simpler, less useful).

**Bottlenecks and failure modes.** Differencing across overlapping cuts; free-text fields slipping in through logging changes; double counting on replay; identity changes (account merges and organisation moves) breaking conversion counts; providers using restated numbers for invoices without knowing which version.

**Incident guidance.**
- *Differencing reveals one account*: two overlapping cuts that both clear the threshold reveal a single account. Remove the ability to choose arbitrary exclusions (fixed cuts only), apply complementary suppression or coarser buckets, and review whether the provider retained the derived information. Prevention: a differencing check in the publishing job that compares cuts for small differences, and noise for browsing metrics.
- *Query text leaked into access records*: a privacy incident. Stop the pipeline, delete the field from provider-facing tables and their history (including shares and any caches), list affected providers and consumers, and support notifications through the privacy process. Prevention: a schema allowlist for provider-facing data, a pipeline check that fails on new fields or on text resembling SQL, and review of logging changes that touch access records.
- *Replay doubled usage*: deduplicate by record id, restate the day as a new version, notify affected providers with the corrected numbers and which invoices used the inflated ones. Prevention: idempotent upserts keyed by record id, a daily reconciliation of record counts against the source, and an anomaly alert on day-over-day jumps.

**Follow-up guidance.** For conversion with account merges, expect a stable organisation-level identity with mapping history and a rule (count per organisation, attribute to first trial). For "most common filter values", a strong candidate declines raw values, offers column-level usage counts, or an aggregated, thresholded and consented feature, and explains why literals are sensitive (they reveal consumer intent).

**Rubric anchors.** 2: gives providers query history or raw events; thresholds on rows not distinct accounts. 3: facts from access records without text, identified versus anonymous separation, distinct-account thresholds, idempotent ingestion, delivery as scoped shares. 4: also differencing defences, data-minimisation gates on new fields, versioned restatements tied to billing, identity-change handling, and crisp incidents.

**Strong signals.** Asks what a provider is already entitled to know before designing; treats query text as consumer data. **Red flags.** "Anonymise the query text" and publish it; thresholds counted in events; no answer for replays.
