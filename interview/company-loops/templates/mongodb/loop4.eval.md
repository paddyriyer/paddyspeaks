# MongoDB · Loop 4 — evaluator notes (hidden from candidates)

## MDB-L4-01

**Reference architecture (one defensible design; score others on the criteria).**
- **Identity and topology**: every sample carries `member_id` (stable for the life of a process host, new when a member is replaced), `cluster_id`, `shard_id` (or none), `role`, `sample_ts`, `agent_seq`. Topology is a separate event table `(member_id, cluster_id, shard_id, valid_from, valid_to)` fed by the control plane, so a chart for a cluster joins samples to the members it had at each moment. Members are never renamed in place.
- **Ingestion**: agents batch samples per {{sample_seconds}}-second tick, compress, and send to a regional ingestion tier (authenticated per agent, which fixes the tenant). A durable log partitioned by `cluster_id` keeps a cluster's members together and ordered. Ingestion computes rates from cumulative counters per (member, counter): `rate = (v − v_prev) / (t − t_prev)`, and when `v < v_prev` or the process start time changed, it treats the interval as a reset (rate from `v / (t − process_start)` or a gap), never a negative rate. Samples carry their own `sample_ts`; late samples are accepted into the right time bucket.
- **Storage**: (a) a hot store with raw samples for a few days, keyed `(tenant, cluster_id, metric, member_id, ts)`, sized for chart reads of one cluster; (b) rollups at 1 minute and 1 hour with `sum, count, min, max` for rates and gauges and a mergeable latency histogram or sketch for percentiles; (c) columnar history partitioned by day and tenant for fleet questions and retention to {{retention_days}} days (raw for days, minutes for weeks, hours beyond). A "latest value per series" table answers "largest replication lag right now" without a scan.
- **Alerting**: a stream evaluator keyed by cluster holds per-rule state (condition true since, last sample time). It evaluates on event time with a small allowed lateness, so a condition held for the rule's duration fires within {{alert_minutes}} minutes. Missing data is its own state ("no data for N intervals"), never zero. Rules are indexed by (cluster, metric), so a sample only touches the rules that reference it.
- **Lag semantics**: lag = primary's latest operation time − secondary's latest applied time, but on idle clusters the primary only advances every 10 seconds (this exercise's rule), so lag shows a 0–10 s sawtooth. Report lag as `max(0, lag − idle_allowance)` when the primary had no user writes in the interval, or report "time since the secondary last caught up", and document it.
- **Disk projection**: a fit over several horizons (hours and days) with known transient consumers (index builds, compaction, initial syncs) annotated from control-plane events; alert on the long-horizon projection, warn on the short one.
- **Isolation and cost**: every read API takes the tenant from the authenticated principal, not from the request; fleet views live behind a separate operator API. Per-tenant query quotas and cached chart tiles protect the store from export jobs. Cost per cluster stays flat because storage per series is fixed by retention and rollup policy, and series count scales with members, not with time.

**Competing designs to accept.** A general-purpose time-series database (with cardinality limits addressed) instead of a custom store; computing rates at query time if resets are detected there correctly; regional stores with a federated fleet view; pulling metrics from members instead of agent push, if the candidate covers scale and member churn.

**Bottlenecks and failure modes.** Series cardinality explosion from labels (per-database or per-collection metrics); negative or giant rates after restarts; late or out-of-order batches mis-evaluated by wall-clock alerting; idle-primary lag artefacts; one tenant's export saturating the store; projection alerts that cry wolf.

**Incident guidance.**
- *Rolling upgrade negative spikes*: rates computed across a counter reset. Recompute affected windows with reset detection (process start time in every sample), mark the restart on charts, and rebuild the fleet "busiest" table. Prevention: reset handling at ingestion plus a test that replays a restart.
- *Idle-cluster lag 0–10 s paging*: the idle primary's 10-second no-op makes lag a sawtooth. Change the lag definition for idle intervals (or compare against the last no-op), suppress the page, and tell the customer what changed. Prevention: documented lag semantics and a synthetic idle-cluster test.
- *40-minute regional backlog*: wall-clock evaluation either dropped late samples or fired late. Evaluate on event time; for samples older than allowed lateness, evaluate retrospectively and send "condition held from … to …" notices rather than live pages; alert on agent connectivity separately. Prevention: per-region ingestion health and "no data" as a first-class state.
- *Index-build disk projections*: short-horizon linear fit sees a transient. Annotate known transient operations, require the long-horizon projection to agree, and cap alert volume per run. Prevention: backtest projections against real disk-full events and track precision.

**Follow-up guidance.** Push on counter resets, the meaning of lag on an idle cluster, alert evaluation with late data, and cardinality at the top of {{clusters}}. A strong candidate quantifies: e.g. members × metrics × samples per day, and bytes per sample after compression.

**Rubric anchors.** 2: "send everything to a time-series database and alert on thresholds"; no resets, no late data, no isolation. 3: stable member identity, reset-aware rates, hot store plus rollups, stream alert evaluation with no-data states, tenant isolation. 4: also lag semantics for idle primaries, event-time alerting with retrospective notices, histogram rollups for percentiles, quotas, cost per cluster reasoning, and crisp handling of every incident.

**Strong signals.** Asks what the metrics are used for before choosing stores; treats topology as history. **Red flags.** Treats missing samples as zero; computes percentiles by averaging per-minute percentiles; customer ids taken from the request body.

## MDB-L4-02

**Reference architecture (one defensible design; score others on the criteria).**
- **Plan**: compute the new key's initial ranges from a sample of documents (so the new layout starts balanced), assign ranges to receiving shards, check free disk on each receiver against its share of {{collection_tb}} TB, and check every source shard's oplog window against the estimated copy time with margin. If the window is too short, enlarge the oplog first (it is the customer's cluster; say so in the plan) or add capacity to speed the copy.
- **Copy (clone)**: record a start cluster time T0. Each receiver reads from every source shard the documents whose new key falls into its ranges, as of T0 (a snapshot read), writes them into a temporary collection, then builds the collection's indexes. Throttle to keep ordinary p99 under twice normal.
- **Catch-up**: each receiver tails every source shard's oplog from T0, filters entries for the collection, maps each changed document to its new owner and applies it idempotently (upserts and deletes by `_id`, replaying entries in order per source). Transactions are applied as units. Each receiver tracks its applied position per source; the coordinator tracks the estimated time to apply what remains.
- **Commit gate and switch**: when the remaining lag is small enough to apply in less than {{block_seconds}} seconds at the measured rate (and the source rate is not at peak), the coordinator blocks writes to the collection on the sources, waits for receivers to apply up to the final position, verifies, then commits a single metadata change on the config servers (new layout, new collection version). Routers refresh and route by the new key; writes resume. If the gate is not met within a time budget, wait or defer to a quieter window; never start the block hoping to catch up.
- **Retried writes and transactions**: the retry history (session id and transaction number → result) of writes that touched the collection is copied to the new owners during catch-up and at commit, so a retried write after the switch is recognised and not applied twice. Transactions in progress at the block are aborted and retried by the driver.
- **Verification**: at a chosen cluster time (the commit point), per-range document counts and order-independent hashes of documents (e.g. sum of hashes) computed on old and new layouts and compared; sampled full-document comparison during catch-up. After commit, old copies are deleted in the background, and reports filter documents a shard does not own until cleanup finishes.
- **Abort**: before commit, aborting drops the temporary collections on receivers and stops catch-up; the old layout was never modified. After commit there is no automatic rollback; a reverse reshard is a new operation.
- **Change streams**: the collection's change stream continues across the commit with a resharding event; tokens from before the switch remain resumable as long as the oplog covers them (this exercise's rule; check current docs). Consumers should tolerate the event.
- **Customer view**: estimated duration, disk and cost impact, progress, the gate status, a scheduled or approved switch, and the ability to abort.

**Competing designs to accept.** Application-level dual writes with a backfill and read switch (more control, more application work, harder transactions); copying into a new collection and renaming at the end; adding temporary shards to speed the copy; a chunk-by-chunk migration if the candidate shows how the key change is achieved.

**Bottlenecks and failure modes.** Oplog window shorter than the copy; receivers that cannot keep up with peak writes; disk exhaustion on receivers; long index builds after the copy; retried writes and in-flight transactions at the switch; unique constraints that are only enforced per shard; leftover old copies counted by reports.

**Incident guidance.**
- *Oplog overwritten at catch-up*: the operation cannot continue safely; abort and clean up. Next attempt: enlarge the source oplogs (or the window via retention settings) to cover the copy with margin, raise the copy rate during quiet hours, and monitor "time until the oldest needed entry is overwritten" as a first-class gauge that pauses or aborts early.
- *Cannot catch up at peak*: do not start the block. Defer the switch to a low-traffic window, temporarily raise receiver apply parallelism, and alert the customer with the estimate. Prevention: the gate is computed from measured rates, and the plan schedules the switch window up front.
- *Increment applied twice after retry*: retry history (session id and transaction number) was not carried to the new owner, so it could not recognise the retry. Find affected writes by matching session and transaction numbers in the old and new owners' oplogs, correct the counts with the customer, and carry retry history at commit. Also note for the candidate: non-idempotent updates (`$inc`) depend on that history, while a unique index can only be enforced across shards when it is prefixed by the shard key (this exercise's rule; check current docs).
- *0.03% more documents in reports*: the report counted leftover documents on shards that no longer own them. Make reports and counts ownership-aware (filter by the routing table) and track cleanup progress; prevention: a post-commit cleanup monitor and a documented "orphan" state.

**Follow-up guidance.** Push on the oplog window arithmetic, the commit gate, and retried writes across the switch. Strong candidates estimate copy time from {{collection_tb}} TB and a throttled rate, and compare it with the window.

**Rubric anchors.** 2: "copy the data to a new collection and switch the application"; no catch-up or a long write outage. 3: snapshot copy at a cluster time, oplog catch-up with idempotent application, a bounded switch, verification by counts, abort before commit. 4: also oplog-window planning, a measured commit gate, retry history and transactions across the switch, hash verification at one cluster time, ownership-aware cleanup, and crisp handling of every incident.

**Strong signals.** Starts with the arithmetic (size, rate, window); separates reversible from irreversible steps. **Red flags.** Long write outages; no plan for changes during the copy; "verify by counting rows" on a moving collection with no fixed time.

## MDB-L4-03

**Reference architecture (one defensible design; score others on the criteria).**
- **Placement**: with three serving regions, five members as 2-2-1 (the region with one member holds a lower priority), or 2-2-1 with the single member in a third, non-serving site; losing any region leaves at least three of five votes. With two serving regions on one continent, two data-bearing members per region plus a fifth voting member in a third site (a data-bearing member preferred over an arbiter), since without a third site the loss of the region holding the majority stops writes. With two continents and two regions each, five or seven members spread so no single region holds a majority (for example 2-2-2-1 with seven voting members), priorities favouring the continent where most writes originate.
- **Priorities and roles**: highest priority on members in the primary's preferred region, next in the nearest other region, zero priority for a member in a far or tie-breaker site so it never becomes primary; hidden members (priority 0, no reads) for analytics or backup; a delayed member only as protection against bad writes, never counted on for majority writes.
- **Write concern**: orders `w: majority` (with journaling), so an acknowledged order survives any single-region loss; activity feed may use acknowledgement by the primary only if the loss rule allows it. Cost: a majority write waits for the nearest secondary that completes the majority, roughly one inter-region round trip if a majority is not within the primary's region.
- **Reads**: "my orders" after placing an order: read from the primary, or a causally consistent session whose operation time is carried by the application (in a cookie or token) so any server in any region reads at least that point with majority read concern; feed browsing reads `nearest` with a staleness bound.
- **Failure walk-through**: primary region lost: after about 10 seconds an election (this exercise's rule) elects the highest-priority reachable member; drivers see errors on in-flight writes, retry once (retryable writes), rediscover the new primary; majority-acknowledged writes are all present. Partition: the cut-off primary cannot see a majority and steps down; the other side elects a primary; the cut-off region's application servers cannot write (they can only reach the old region's members) and may read stale data from local secondaries; route those customers to another region.

**Competing designs to accept.** Seven voting members for finer placement; an arbiter in the tie-breaker site if the candidate knows its cost with majority writes; region-local writes through sharding by region (zones) as a later step; accepting a manual failover for two-region setups if the candidate states it violates the constraint and proposes a third site.

**Bottlenecks and failure modes.** Majority unavailable after losing the majority region; arbiter layouts that stall majority writes when a data-bearing member is down; w:1 rollbacks; stale reads from lagging secondaries; failover to a far region moving write latency; failback churn when a high-priority member returns.

**Incident guidance.**
- *Default write concern lowered, 4 s rolled back*: the change violated {{loss_rule}}. Recover the rolled-back writes from the rollback files on the old primary and reconcile them against later writes (customers may have retried); restore `w: majority` for orders, and add a guard that rejects weaker defaults for the order collections. Prevention: write concern per collection or operation in code reviews and a test that kills the primary under load.
- *Arbiter layout, secondary down*: with one data-bearing secondary down, majority writes cannot be satisfied; the primary keeps more history in its cache because the majority commit point does not advance. Bring the secondary back or temporarily reconfigure, lower write concern only where allowed, and replace the arbiter with a data-bearing member. Prevention: no arbiter where majority writes are required.
- *Region cut off 12 minutes*: the old primary stepped down correctly; customers in that region saw frozen data. Route their traffic to healthy regions via the global load balancer, show a degraded notice, and ensure reads that need freshness go to the primary. Prevention: health checks that consider the replica set's view, not only the local region.
- *Write latency after failover*: the primary moved away from the application servers. Move write traffic or let the original region's member become primary again in a controlled step once it is caught up. Prevention: plan priorities with latency in mind and test failback.

**Follow-up guidance.** Make the candidate count votes after each regional loss and count round trips per write. Probe read-your-own-writes across application servers in different regions.

**Rubric anchors.** 2: "three members in three regions, failover is automatic" with no vote arithmetic, write concern or read semantics. 3: correct placement and vote counting, majority writes for orders, read preferences per read, correct election behaviour. 4: also causal consistency across servers, arbiter and hidden or delayed member trade-offs, partition behaviour with step-down, latency accounting in inter-region round trips, failback, and crisp handling of every incident.

**Strong signals.** Starts from the loss rule and vote arithmetic. **Red flags.** Believes w:1 writes are durable across failover; puts a majority of votes in one region; reads from secondaries for read-your-own-writes with no session.

## MDB-L4-04

**Reference architecture (one defensible design; score others on the criteria).**
- **Hierarchy**: the customer's key (in their key management service) wraps a per-cluster master key (or per-member keys); the storage engine's data keys are wrapped by that master key; data files are encrypted with data keys. Only the wrapped master key is stored with the cluster. The service calls decrypt to unwrap the master key at member start; plaintext keys live only in member memory.
- **Access**: the customer grants a role or principal used only by the service's key-access component, scoped to encrypt/decrypt on that key. A periodic health check (for example every few minutes) and the customer's own key events (permission or state change notifications where available) feed a key-access state per cluster.
- **Member start and replacement**: a new or replaced member fetches the wrapped master key, calls decrypt with the customer's key, and starts. If the call fails it stays down and the cluster's status shows "key access failed" with the exact error returned.
- **Rotation**: the customer rotates; the service detects the new primary key version and rewraps the master key with it (a decrypt + encrypt call), on every member, in every region, without restarting or rewriting data. Re-encrypting data files with new data keys is a separate, scheduled operation (rolling initial sync or file rewrite), offered for customers whose policy requires it.
- **Backups**: each snapshot stores its data in encrypted form plus the wrapped key it needs, with a record of the customer key version used. A restore unwraps with that version. Before a customer deletes a key version, they can see (in the console and via API) which retained backups depend on it; the service warns at rotation time and fails restores with a precise message. Cross-region snapshot copies are wrapped by the destination region's key (or a linked multi-region key), recorded likewise.
- **Revocation**: state machine `healthy → access_failing → revoked` (or `outage`). Distinguish permission/state errors (access denied, key disabled or scheduled for deletion) from availability errors (timeouts, 5xx, a regional outage of the key service). On confirmed revocation: block new member starts and restores, then perform an orderly shutdown of members within {{revoke_hours}} hours so in-memory keys are discarded, and stop backups. Recovery: once access returns, members start normally; deletion of the key is irreversible and makes data and backups unrecoverable, as the customer intended.
- **Audit**: the key service's own logs in the customer's account record every decrypt/encrypt call by the service, with times; the service adds a per-cluster event log (rotation detected, rewrap done, access lost, shutdown done) the customer can export.

**Competing designs to accept.** Per-member master keys; client-side field-level encryption for the most sensitive fields in addition (the service then never sees those fields in plaintext); re-encrypting data at rotation if the candidate costs it; immediate shutdown at revocation if they handle outages separately.

**Bottlenecks and failure modes.** Members unable to restart after a policy mistake; deleted key versions breaking old backups; replicas or copies in other regions using different keys; outages mistaken for revocation; exports outside the key's protection; throttling of key service calls when thousands of members restart at once.

**Incident guidance.**
- *Policy mistake, member stays down*: the rolling patch must stop at the first member that cannot unwrap its key (a pre-check before restarting each member). Tell the customer the exact permission error; once fixed, the member starts. Prevention: pre-flight key access check before any planned restart, and alerting the customer when the periodic check starts failing.
- *Deleted key version, restore fails*: the snapshot is wrapped by the deleted version; it cannot be restored. Say so plainly. Prevention: per-backup key-version dependency shown before deletion, a warning when a version with dependants is scheduled for deletion, and rewrapping retained backups to the new version at rotation (a cheap operation on the wrapped key) where the policy allows.
- *Revocation, second region still serving, unencrypted export*: the second region's members held unwrapped keys and were not covered by the shutdown sequence, or used a linked key not revoked. Extend revocation to every member and copy wrapped by any version of the customer's key, and tell the customer exactly what remains (the export). Prevention: an inventory of every artefact per key and exports encrypted with the customer's key.
- *KMS outage treated as revocation*: halt the automation, restart any stopped members once the key service returns, and post an incident note. Prevention: classify errors, require sustained permission errors before acting, and cap automated shutdowns per window with a human approval above the cap.

**Follow-up guidance.** Push on the difference between rewrapping and re-encrypting, on which key versions old backups need, and on how revocation reaches a member that never restarts.

**Rubric anchors.** 2: "encrypt the disk with the customer's key", no hierarchy, no backup or revocation story. 3: envelope hierarchy, start-time unwrap, rotation by rewrap, backups with key-version records, revocation detection and shutdown. 4: also outage versus revocation, bounded in-memory key lifetime, cross-region copies and exports, dependency warnings before deletion, auditable evidence from the key service, and crisp handling of every incident.

**Strong signals.** Asks what "unreadable" must mean and for which artefacts. **Red flags.** Storing the customer's key in plaintext; claiming rotation re-encrypts data for free; no thought for old backups after rotation.

## MDB-L4-05

**Reference architecture (one defensible design; score others on the criteria).**
- **Snapshots**: per shard (and config servers), a consistent snapshot from a secondary using a storage-level snapshot of a checkpoint-consistent data directory, or a database backup cursor; each records its shard and the cluster time it represents. Incremental snapshots copy only changed blocks after a full base; chains are bounded (a new full base periodically). Snapshots are taken from secondaries and throttled to keep load within a few percent.
- **Oplog capture**: a capture process per shard tails the oplog from a secondary and writes slices to object storage, each slice `(shard, first_ts, last_ts, checksum)`. Continuity rule: each slice starts right after the previous slice's last entry; any break is a recorded gap. A gauge per shard tracks "headroom" = time between the oldest entry still on the shard and the capture position; alerts at low headroom, and capture is never throttled below the shard's write rate. Captured oplog is kept for {{window}}.
- **Restore point**: the customer picks a second; the system converts it to a cluster time (all entries with seconds ≤ chosen second, inclusive, defined in the console). For each shard: choose the latest snapshot at or before the point, restore it to new storage, start a member in a restore mode, and apply captured oplog entries in order up to the cluster time. A cross-shard transaction commits at one cluster time on all its shards, so stopping every shard at the same cluster time keeps it whole. Config server metadata is restored to the same point.
- **Time budget**: restore time ≈ (bytes to copy / restore throughput) + (oplog hours since snapshot × {{oplog_gb_hour}} GB / replay throughput) + index and validation checks. Snapshots every {{snapshot_hours}} hours bound the replay; for tight {{rto_hours}} hours, keep more frequent incrementals or a continuously applied standby copy for premium tiers. The console shows an estimate before starting.
- **Verification**: the restored cluster starts and passes collection and index validation; per-collection counts and hashes at the restore point compared with values recorded at snapshot time and adjusted by the replayed oplog (counts derived from oplog inserts and deletes); restored cluster time equals the requested point on every shard. Continuous drills: automatically restore a sample of clusters every day, verify, and record restore duration against the promise.
- **Retention**: snapshots carry a policy class (hourly, daily, weekly, monthly) and expire by policy; deletion is reference-counted (active restores, incremental chains, legal holds); a full base is deleted only when no later increment depends on it, or chains are re-based. Retention changes trigger a sweep and the storage report updates when deletion completes.
- **Honesty**: periods with an oplog gap are shown as not restorable to the second; the nearest restorable points are offered.

**Competing designs to accept.** Logical dumps for small clusters; continuous replication to a standby that is snapshotted; restoring only to snapshot times for cheap tiers; capture from primaries with care for load.

**Bottlenecks and failure modes.** Capture falling behind the oplog window; long replay times; inconsistent cross-shard restore points; corrupted increments; retention not reclaiming storage; drills that only count documents.

**Incident guidance.**
- *25-minute gap from a bulk import*: capture fell behind a shard whose oplog filled in 40 minutes. Mark the gap, show the customer the restorable points around it, take a fresh snapshot immediately to restart a continuous range. Prevention: headroom alerting, never throttling capture below the write rate, and enlarging oplogs or raising capture capacity when write bursts are detected.
- *Restore misses the {{rto_hours}}-hour promise*: replay of oplog since the last snapshot dominated. Communicate the estimate; for the future, more frequent incrementals during heavy write periods, parallel replay per shard, and a restore-time estimate per cluster monitored against the promise.
- *Transaction split across shards in a drill*: shards were stopped at their own wall-clock seconds. Restore to a single cluster time on every shard; add a drill check for cross-shard transactions (both sides present or absent).
- *Bill not falling after retention change*: references from a running restore and from incremental chains kept snapshots alive. Show "pending deletion" and why; re-base chains so old bases can go; release references when restores finish. Prevention: storage accounting by reference and a sweep report.

**Follow-up guidance.** Push on the restore-time arithmetic, gap detection, and the cluster-time restore point. Ask what verification proves beyond counts.

**Rubric anchors.** 2: "daily snapshots plus keep the oplog", no consistency across shards or gap handling. 3: snapshots with cluster-time metadata, continuous capture with gap detection, replay to a point, verification and retention. 4: also restore-time budgeting against {{rto_hours}} hours, cross-shard transaction integrity, reference-counted retention with incremental chains, automated drills with measured durations, and crisp handling of every incident.

**Strong signals.** Treats the oplog window as a deadline for capture; defines "to a second" explicitly. **Red flags.** Wall-clock restore points per shard; no drills; claiming every second is restorable without gap detection.
