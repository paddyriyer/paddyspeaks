Part 1

Data-rich, insight-poor

Hypothetical Meet Vikram, 54, a finance director. He knows his portfolio’s year-to-date return to one decimal place. He knows when his car is due for its 60,000-mile service, the annual fee on each of his three credit cards, and which grocery store discounts olive oil on Thursdays. He has worn an Apple Watch for four years. It has quietly logged roughly a million heart-rate samples, more than a thousand nights of sleep and every flight of stairs he has climbed since the pandemic.

Ask him whether his cardiovascular fitness improved or declined over the last twelve months and he will say, with total confidence, “I think it’s fine.” He has not opened the Health app since he set it up. The watch is, in practice, a very expensive way of being told to stand up.

Vikram is not unusual. Most of us run our bodies the way small companies once ran servers: nobody looks until something is on fire.

We built monitoring systems for our servers, but our own bodies are apparently running in production without observability.

In engineering, observability means telling what a system is doing from the signals it emits, so that a slow drift is caught before it becomes an outage. Your watch records plenty of signals. What is missing is the baseline, the trend line and the habit of looking.

The opposite mistake is just as real: treating every orange ring and every dip in heart-rate variability as a crisis. A single reading is not a trend, most alerts are not disease, and some changes that matter never trigger an alert at all.

Collecting health data is not the same as understanding health. Understanding health is not the same as improving it.

This guide covers the ground in between: what your watch measures and how far to trust it; how to get a year of data out, check it and read it against your own baseline; what deserves a new habit and what deserves a call to your doctor; and how to use AI as an analyst without letting it play cardiologist.

Part 2

What can an Apple Watch actually measure?

Five kinds of number share the Health app, all in the same calm typography. Know which one you are looking at:

  • Measured A sensor reading, such as heart rate from the optical sensor or a single-lead ECG trace. It is the closest thing to raw data, and it is still imperfect. Movement, fit, skin and tattoos all affect optical readings.
  • Estimate An algorithm’s inference: VO₂ max, sleep stages, walking steadiness, active calories, breathing disturbances. These can be useful for trends and unreliable as absolutes.
  • Entered Things you type: weight, medications, mood. It is only as good as your diligence.
  • Device Record Readings from a connected blood-pressure cuff, scale or glucose monitor, and records from your doctor’s system. These are often the most clinically meaningful data in the app.
  • Score A proprietary composite, such as a readiness, recovery or sleep score. It is useful as a summary and impossible to audit, because the formula is not published.

Two things the watch does not do. No Apple Watch measures blood glucose; glucose in Health comes from a meter or a continuous glucose monitor. No Apple Watch measures blood pressure either. Hypertension notifications (Series 9 and later) look for patterns in optical-sensor data that suggest high blood pressure, but they produce no systolic or diastolic numbers. Blood-pressure readings in Health come from a cuff or from manual entry Apple docs.

Part 3

Extract your health data: a practical walkthrough

Method A: the complete native export

  1. Open the Health app on your iPhone and tap Summary.
  2. Tap your picture or initials in the top-right corner.
  3. Tap Export All Health Data, confirm, and wait. A decade of data can take several minutes.
  4. Choose where to save it. Save to Files on your own device is the safest choice. Do not email it to yourself.

The result is a zip archive, typically holding export.xml (everything, one line per sample), export_cda.xml (a clinical-document version), and folders of ECG recordings (CSV), workout routes (GPS tracks) and any clinical records Inference. That list comes from exports we have inspected; Apple documents only that the export is XML. Each Record line carries a type (such as HKQuantityTypeIdentifierRestingHeartRate), a sourceName (the device or app that wrote it), a unit, a value and timestamps with the UTC offset at the time, such as 2026-11-19 07:22:10 -0800. That offset matters as soon as you travel.

Limits of the export. It is a snapshot, not a sync. It holds far more than any one question needs, including GPS routes that show where you live. Composite scores such as Readiness may be missing; one developer analysis reports that Apple exposes the inputs but not its scores Unknown.

Method B: an ECG for your doctor

  1. In Health, open Browse (or search), then Heart → Electrocardiograms (ECG).
  2. Tap the recording you want.
  3. Tap Export a PDF for Your Doctor, then share or print it.

The PDF shows the 30-second trace, the classification, the average heart rate and any symptoms you logged. Read the classification in Apple’s own terms. Sinus rhythm means a regular pattern between 50 and 100 beats per minute during that recording. Atrial fibrillation means an irregular pattern consistent with AFib. Inconclusive means it could not be classified Apple docs.

The watch’s ECG is single-lead, similar to lead I of a hospital ECG. A clinical 12-lead ECG views the heart’s electrical activity from twelve angles, which is how it can show signs of a heart attack and many abnormalities one lead cannot see Research. A single lead answers one question well: does the rhythm look like AFib? It is not a smaller version of the hospital test.

Method C: connected services

Apps reach your data through HealthKit, Apple’s permission system: each asks for specific categories, read and write separately, reviewable under Health → profile → Apps. In 2026 the large AI assistants joined the list. OpenAI’s ChatGPT Health and Anthropic’s Claude both offer Apple Health connections in the US through their iPhone apps. Both say connected health data is not used to train their models Vendor docs. See Part 8 for what those promises do and do not cover.

Method D: connected medical devices

Cuffs, scales and glucose monitors usually write to Health through their maker’s app. Some sync on a delay, and some send only a few types: a scale may send weight but keep body composition to itself. Turn on only the types you want, then confirm the readings actually arrive.

Method E: turn the export into tables you can analyse

You do not need to become a programmer: two short scripts, Python 3 and twenty minutes (or a technically minded friend). The first streams the huge XML file into one CSV, a plain table any spreadsheet opens:

# health_to_csv.py: turn Apple Health's export.xml into one tidy CSV.
# Usage: python3 health_to_csv.py export.xml records.csv
import csv, sys
import xml.etree.ElementTree as ET

src, out = sys.argv[1], sys.argv[2]
FIELDS = ["type", "sourceName", "sourceVersion", "unit", "value",
          "creationDate", "startDate", "endDate"]

with open(out, "w", newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
    writer = csv.DictWriter(f, fieldnames=FIELDS)
    writer.writeheader()
    # iterparse streams the file, so a 2 GB export will not fill your memory
    for _, el in ET.iterparse(src, events=("end",)):
        if el.tag == "Record":
            writer.writerow({k: el.get(k, "") for k in FIELDS})
            el.clear()
print("done:", out)

The second script (it needs the pandas library) turns that into one row per day, which is what you actually want to chart:

# daily.py: one row per local day per measure, ready for a spreadsheet or Parquet.
import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv("records.csv")
df["local_day"] = df["startDate"].str[:10]          # the day as YOU lived it
df["value_num"] = pd.to_numeric(df["value"], errors="coerce")

# Steps: keep one source per day so iPhone and Watch are not counted twice
steps = df[df["type"] == "HKQuantityTypeIdentifierStepCount"]
steps = (steps.groupby(["local_day", "sourceName"])["value_num"].sum()
              .groupby("local_day").max().rename("steps"))

rhr = (df[df["type"] == "HKQuantityTypeIdentifierRestingHeartRate"]
         .groupby("local_day")["value_num"].mean().rename("resting_hr"))

daily = pd.concat([steps, rhr], axis=1).sort_index()
daily.to_csv("daily.csv")
# daily.to_parquet("daily.parquet")   # needs: pip install pyarrow
print(daily.tail())

Three decisions in that code are where amateur analyses usually go wrong:

  • Local day, not UTC day. Taking the date from the original timestamp keeps an 11 p.m. walk in Tokyo on the day you lived it.
  • One source for steps. iPhone and watch both count steps, so summing every row double-counts. The larger single-source total per day is a conservative approximation; Apple merges sources minute by minute, so its total may differ slightly.
  • Numbers stay numbers. Sleep records hold words such as HKCategoryValueSleepAnalysisAsleepCore; errors="coerce" turns them into blanks, not nonsense. For sleep, sum the minutes between start and end per stage.

Parquet is a compact file format analysis tools read quickly; use it if your export runs to gigabytes. A year of daily rows fits in a spreadsheet, and it is far less sensitive than the full export, which is the point of Part 8.

An app being connected does not prove its measurements have synced. A green toggle means permission was granted. It says nothing about whether data arrived, how much, or since when.

The sync and quality checklist

  • Permissions: for each app, the exact categories it can read and write, and nothing extra.
  • Latest sample: open each metric’s “Show All Data” and confirm the newest entry is from today or yesterday.
  • Sources: under each metric’s Data Sources & Access, see who is writing, and in what priority order.
  • Coverage: first and last dates per metric, and how many days in the year actually have data.
  • Gaps: charging at night, a dead battery on holiday, a watch left in a drawer. List them before you interpret anything.
  • Hardware and software changes: a new watch model or a major watchOS release can shift a metric overnight. Mark those dates.
  • Duplicates and units: two apps writing the same workout; pounds and kilograms; mmol/L and mg/dL.

And for AI tools: an assistant has accessed your data only if you connected or uploaded it, and only the categories you allowed. If it describes your trends without that, it is making them up. Ask it to list what it can see first.

Part 4

The 365-day personal health audit

Yesterday’s numbers tell you about yesterday. A year tells you about you. Here is a reproducible method to run yourself, hand to a technical friend or give to an AI assistant with the prompt in Part 9.

  1. Inventory every category you have. List every type in the export with its row count. Most people discover thirty to sixty.
  2. Document each one: source devices and apps, unit, first and last measurement date.
  3. Find the problems before the patterns: missing days, duplicate samples, timezone jumps, a device change, irregular sampling (HRV is sampled opportunistically, not on a schedule).
  4. Separate “not enough data” from “normal”. Twelve VO₂ max estimates in a year is a thin series. Eleven nights of sleep tracking says nothing about your sleep. Write “insufficient” where it applies. It is a finding.
  5. Establish your personal baseline from a stable stretch, for example the median and spread of a calm quarter with no illness, travel or medication change.
  6. Look at the right resolution. Daily is for noticing illness. Weekly averages are for behaviour. Monthly and quarterly medians are for fitness and aging. Annual is for comparing like seasons.
  7. Examine variability, sustained shifts and outliers. A 28-day rolling average that stays above your baseline band for weeks is a shift. One spike is an outlier, so check the context before keeping or excluding it.
  8. Compare non-overlapping windows: the last 90 days against the same 90 days a year ago, not against a window that includes them.
  9. Investigate relationships, not just levels: does short sleep precede a higher resting heart rate the next day? Does a hard training week depress HRV?
  10. Consider the explanations before the conclusions: age, new medications, illness, travel, heat, a new job, a new baby, a new watch.

Statistics that are actually appropriate

Use medians and interquartile ranges (the middle half of your values), because health data are skewed and spiky. Use 7-day and 28-day rolling averages to see through daily noise. Draw your personal reference band on every chart and label it as yours; population ranges, if shown at all, get their own labelled line.

Why HRV from different devices does not compare

HRV is a family of statistics. Apple Health stores SDNN (the standard deviation of beat-to-beat intervals) from short readings while you are still Apple docs. Many rings and straps report RMSSD (based on differences between consecutive beats), often only during sleep. They have different typical values Research. A ring saying 45 and a watch saying 38 are not disagreeing; they are answering different questions. Compare a device only with itself, and mark the date you changed hardware.

Association is not causation, and forty metrics will lie to you

If you correlate forty metrics with each other, you are running 780 tests. Some will look “significant” by pure chance. The fix is old-fashioned: decide your two or three questions before looking, prefer relationships with a plausible mechanism and a time order (sleep before heart rate, not after), and treat anything surprising as a hypothesis to test for a month, not a finding to act on. Three setups, and when to stop

  • Minimal (most people): Apple Health and Fitness, Vitals and sleep tracking on, the notifications your watch supports switched on, and an export once a quarter for the dashboard. Total extra cost: zero.
  • Intermediate: add a validated blood-pressure cuff (the most underrated health purchase in this article) and, at most, one specialist app: a history app if you like detail, or a pacing or recovery app if you train and your watch has no Readiness.
  • Advanced: add connected Health Records, the export pipeline from Part 3, and a CGM or nutrition log only for a defined question agreed with a clinician.

Native apps are enough when your question is “am I trending the wrong way?” A specialist app earns its place when it answers a question Apple’s apps do not, such as long-range sleep history or load-paced training. A validated medical device or a professional assessment is the right tool when the question is clinical: blood pressure, a rhythm you can feel, unexplained breathlessness, a fall. No app upgrades a wellness estimate into a diagnosis.

Part 6

Turning observations into practical improvements

First, the three kinds of signal, because they need three different responses:

Symptoms outrank sensors

A normal-looking reading never cancels a worrying symptom. The watch cannot detect a heart attack. Hypertension notifications miss most cases. Sleep apnea notifications miss some. If you feel wrong, act on the feeling, not the dashboard.

Six scenarios follow. All are Hypothetical composites, not real people.

A · Resting heart rate rises slowly while sleep falls

What the data shows
Over ten weeks, the 28-day resting HR average climbs from 58 to 64 bpm, while median sleep falls from 7 h 10 min to 6 h 05 min. Bedtime variability doubles.
Possible interpretations
Accumulated short sleep, work stress, more evening alcohol, a lingering infection, a new medication, less exercise, or a hot season.
What else is needed
Your annotated timeline: medications, illness, travel, alcohol, workload. A week of home blood-pressure readings.
Measurement limits
Resting HR depends on how the watch finds “rest”. Sleep duration is an estimate. Both are good enough for a ten-week trend.
Reasonable actions
A fixed wake time seven days a week; less alcohol in the evening; protect 30 minutes of daylight in the morning. Recheck the averages after four weeks.
When to see a professional
If the rise persists despite better sleep, if it comes with palpitations, breathlessness or chest discomfort, or if home blood pressure is raised.

B · Estimated VO₂ max declines for six months despite regular walking

What the data shows
Eleven estimates over six months, drifting from 34 to 31 mL/kg/min. Steps are unchanged; most walks are at an easy pace.
Possible interpretations
Easy walking maintains but rarely raises fitness. Fewer brisk outdoor walks mean fewer, noisier estimates. A new heart-rate-affecting medication shifts the estimate. Real deconditioning is also possible.
What else is needed
Medication changes; how many estimates came from brisk walks; whether stair climbing feels harder.
Measurement limits
Typical error is around 13% against lab tests. A 3-point change is within the noise of a thin series.
Reasonable actions
Add two sessions a week where you walk briskly enough that talking in full sentences becomes hard, or use hills. Reassess the trend after three months, not three days.
When to see a professional
If exertion now brings chest discomfort, unusual breathlessness, dizziness or ankle swelling, or before starting vigorous exercise if you have heart disease or several risk factors.

C · Irregular sleep and worsening recovery

What the data shows
Bedtimes range across three hours. HRV’s 7-day average is below its baseline band, and Readiness or a recovery app keeps saying “Pace Yourself”.
Possible interpretations
Irregular schedule, shift work, a new baby, evening screens, alcohol, training too hard for the sleep you are getting.
What else is needed
Training load for the same weeks; caffeine and alcohol timing; daytime sleepiness.
Measurement limits
HRV is noisy, and recovery scores are proprietary. The bedtime spread is the most trustworthy number here.
Reasonable actions
Fix the wake time first; reduce training intensity for a week; no caffeine after early afternoon.
When to see a professional
Loud snoring, gasping, morning headaches, falling asleep while driving, or persistent insomnia. Sleep disorders are treatable.

D · Walking speed and mobility measures decline gradually

What the data shows
Over a year, median walking speed falls from 1.25 to 1.10 m/s; double-support time rises; steadiness drops from OK to Low.
Possible interpretations
Joint pain, foot problems, muscle loss, a medication causing dizziness, low mood, vision changes, or simply carrying the phone in a different pocket.
What else is needed
Falls or near-falls; pain; how the phone is carried; new medications.
Measurement limits
Phone-based gait measures depend on placement. The trend is more credible if several measures move together.
Reasonable actions
Strength and balance training two or three times a week; sensible footwear; a home hazard check.
When to see a professional
At your next appointment, with the chart. Sooner after any fall, new weakness or sudden change.

E · The ECG says sinus rhythm, but symptoms continue

What the data shows
Three ECGs over two weeks, all “sinus rhythm”, taken during episodes of fluttering and light-headedness.
Possible interpretations
Many rhythm problems are intermittent or are not AFib. A single lead cannot assess many of them.
What else is needed
A symptom diary with times; the ECG PDFs; whether episodes come with exertion or fainting.
Measurement limits
“Sinus rhythm” describes 30 seconds on one lead. It does not exclude a heart attack or other conditions Apple docs.
Reasonable actions
Keep recording during episodes, because the strips are useful evidence. Do not treat a normal strip as reassurance.
When to see a professional
Now, by appointment, for recurrent symptoms. Immediately, through emergency services, for chest pain, fainting or severe breathlessness.

F · A frightening red recovery score on an ordinary day

What the data shows
A third-party app shows a red “14% recovered”. Resting HR, respiratory rate and temperature are all within your usual range. HRV is somewhat low.
Possible interpretations
One low HRV reading after a late dinner, a glass of wine, or a hard workout yesterday. Composite scores amplify single inputs.
What else is needed
How you actually feel; yesterday’s context; whether this repeats over a week.
Measurement limits
The formula is unpublished; HRV is noisy; the score is not a medical measure.
Reasonable actions
Train lighter if you like. Otherwise, live your day. If scores routinely make you anxious, hide them. Anxiety about sleep has a name, and it does not help sleep.
When to see a professional
Only if you also have symptoms, or Vitals shows several metrics outside your range for several days.

Two lines no scenario crosses: no diagnosis from a wearable trend, and no medication change on consumer data alone. If your data makes you wonder about a dose, bring the chart to the person who prescribed it.

Part 8

Privacy: your most intimate data deserves protection

A full Health export can contain your ECGs, medications, diagnoses, lab results, menstrual and sexual-activity logs, mood entries and years of GPS routes that start and end at your front door. Almost none of it is needed to answer “has my resting heart rate gone up?”

In the US, HIPAA covers health data held by doctors, hospitals, insurers and their contractors. It generally does not cover what you hand a consumer app. Those apps fall mostly to the Federal Trade Commission, whose Health Breach Notification Rule (expanded in 2024) requires many of them to tell you about unauthorised disclosure FTC. That is a duty to notify, not a guarantee of privacy.

  • Minimise HealthKit permissions. Grant categories one by one. A sleep app does not need reproductive health or clinical records.
  • Know where each app stores data and for how long. On your device, its own cloud or a partner’s? Can you delete it, and does deleting the app delete the copy?
  • Read the sharing clauses. Look for “partners”, “research”, “de-identified” and “advertising” in the privacy policy and the App Store privacy label.
  • Prefer local processing. The scripts in Part 3 run entirely on your computer. A spreadsheet on your own laptop never leaves your laptop.
  • Protect the source. Use a device passcode and two-factor authentication on your Apple Account. Apple lists Health data among the categories that are end-to-end encrypted in iCloud. Do not leave export.zip in your Downloads folder, a shared drive or your email.
  • Strip identifiers before sharing. Remove your name, date of birth (give an age), device names, record IDs and all GPS routes. Send a daily summary table, not the raw export.
  • Limit sensitive categories. Leave out reproductive, sexual and mental-health data unless they are the actual question.
  • Check the AI service’s own terms. OpenAI says data connected to ChatGPT Health is kept in a separate space and not used to train its models. Anthropic says the same of health data connected to Claude Vendor docs. These are the companies’ own statements, which this article cannot audit. Files uploaded into an ordinary chat may follow ordinary settings, so check them.

Permission to access is not permission to redistribute. An app that may read your heart rate has not thereby earned the right to share it, and neither has an assistant you gave a file to, or a family member you let see your dashboard.

Part 9

The generative AI health analyst

Used well, an AI assistant is an excellent junior analyst: it parses the export, builds the daily table, charts trends, finds gaps and drafts questions for your doctor. Used badly, it is a confident stranger guessing at your heart. The prompt below asks for the first and forbids the second. Share only what you have decided to share, and fill in the brackets.

The AI should act as an analytical assistant, not a virtual cardiologist.

ROLE
You are my personal health-data analyst. You are not a physician and must not act like one. Your job: organise, validate and describe my own health data; separate what is observed from what is inferred and what is unknown; and help me prepare useful questions for a qualified clinician. You never diagnose, never tell me to start, stop or change any medication, and never tell me a symptom is nothing.

SAFETY FIRST (check before anything else)
If I mention chest pain or pressure, fainting, severe shortness of breath, signs of stroke (face drooping, arm weakness, speech difficulty), confusion, or a very fast or very slow heart rate with symptoms: stop the analysis and tell me to contact emergency services now. A normal-looking wearable reading never overrides symptoms. Say so whenever it is relevant: a "sinus rhythm" ECG covers only that 30-second single-lead strip and cannot rule out a heart attack; hypertension and sleep-apnea notifications miss many cases, so no alert is not reassurance.

STEP 1 - ACCESS (do not skip)
List exactly which data you can actually see in this conversation: connected sources, uploaded files, and categories. Do not claim, imply or guess access to anything else. If more data would help, name it and ask for my explicit permission first. Do not request reproductive, sexual-health, mental-health, location or clinical-record data unless my question needs it.

STEP 2 - INVENTORY AND COVERAGE REPORT
For every data type present: name, source device or app, unit, first and last date, number of days with data in the analysis period, and data type (sensor measurement / algorithmic estimate / user-entered / connected device / medical record / proprietary score). Present this as a table.

STEP 3 - VALIDATE AND NORMALISE before interpreting
Check for: missing days and long gaps; duplicate samples (for example steps from both iPhone and Watch - never sum sources blindly); timezone shifts (group by the local date in the original timestamp); unit mixes (lb/kg, mg/dL/mmol/L); changes of device or software version; implausible values. Report each issue and what you did about it. Where data are too sparse, write "insufficient data" - never treat absence of data as normal.

STEP 4 - ANALYSIS PERIOD
Analyse [START DATE] to [END DATE] (default: the last 365 days). Establish my personal baseline from [BASELINE WINDOW, e.g. a stable quarter] using medians and interquartile ranges. Compare the most recent 90 days with the same, non-overlapping 90 days a year earlier. Use 7-day and 28-day rolling averages; show daily values only for spotting acute changes.

STEP 5 - TRENDS AGAINST MY OWN BASELINE
For cardiovascular (resting HR, walking HR, HRV from ONE device only, VO2 max estimate, home blood pressure if present), sleep and recovery (duration, bedtime regularity, respiratory rate, wrist-temperature deviation), activity, mobility (walking speed, step length, double support, steadiness) and any metabolic data present: describe direction, size and persistence of change, with sample counts. Distinguish sustained shifts from single outliers. Do not compare my HRV with population norms or with another device's HRV.

STEP 6 - RELATIONSHIPS (limited and honest)
Examine at most three pre-specified relationships: [e.g. sleep duration vs next-day resting HR; training load vs HRV; alcohol days vs sleep]. Respect time order. State that associations are not causes. Do not mine dozens of metrics for correlations.

STEP 7 - CONTEXT BEFORE CONCLUSIONS
Ask me about, or use if provided: age, medications and start dates, illnesses, travel, major life events, new devices. Explain how each could account for the patterns.

STEP 8 - ECG DOCUMENTS (if I upload them)
Report the device's own classification, date, average heart rate and logged symptoms. Explain in plain words what a single-lead consumer ECG can and cannot show compared with a clinical 12-lead ECG. Do not offer your own rhythm diagnosis.

STEP 9 - LABEL EVERY STATEMENT
Tag each finding OBSERVED (directly in the data), POSSIBLE EXPLANATION (one of several), or UNKNOWN (cannot be assessed, and why).

STEP 10 - PRIORITISED ACTION PLAN
At most three lifestyle actions, each tied to a specific finding, each with a process goal I control and an outcome indicator to recheck in 4-12 weeks. Cite evidence for each recommendation. No supplements or medication suggestions.

STEP 11 - QUESTIONS FOR MY CLINICIAN
Up to five specific, neutral questions, each with the chart or numbers that prompted it.

STEP 12 - OUTPUTS
(a) A one-page dashboard summary with sections: Cardiovascular; Sleep and recovery; Activity and fitness; Mobility; Metabolic (if available); Context; Missing or unreliable data; Questions for a professional. No overall "health score".
(b) A physician-facing summary of no more than 250 words: data sources and coverage, key observed changes with dates and numbers, relevant context, my questions. Neutral language, no diagnosis.
(c) A list of what could NOT be assessed, and why.

CITATIONS
Cite a source for every medical recommendation and every claim about what a device can measure - preferably the manufacturer's own documentation, a professional society guideline or a peer-reviewed study - with a working link. If you cannot cite it, label it as your own reasoning or leave it out. Note that device features change by model, software version and region.

STYLE
Plain English. Explain each technical term once. Be calm. Be specific about uncertainty. Do not reassure me beyond the data, and do not alarm me beyond it either.

Two practical notes. First, give the assistant the daily summary table from Part 3 rather than the full export whenever you can. It works better and you share less. Second, the physician-facing summary is a draft for you to check before it goes anywhere. A clinician will take a 250-word summary with dated numbers far more seriously than “my app was red”.

The reader checklist

Your 12-month audit, on one card

  • I know which of my numbers are measured, estimated, entered, device or record, and which are scores.
  • Sleep tracking and Vitals are on, and I wear the watch at night at least five nights a week.
  • I turned on the notifications my watch supports, and I know that none of them can clear me of anything.
  • I checked permissions, sources and the latest sample date for every connected app and device.
  • I exported my data, stored it securely and built a daily table, myself or with help.
  • I wrote down my baselines and my gaps, including the metrics with insufficient data.
  • I compared the last 90 days with the same period last year, using medians and rolling averages.
  • I annotated medications, illnesses, travel and device changes on the timeline.
  • I chose at most two habits for the next 60 days, with process goals.
  • If I own, or have reason to buy, a validated blood-pressure cuff, I take a week of readings every quarter.
  • I wrote no more than three questions for my clinician, each with its chart.
  • I shared only a minimised summary with any app or AI, and I know its data terms.
  • I know the emergency symptoms, and I will never take an ECG instead of calling for help.

Part 10

Make technology serve your health

Nobody needs to turn retirement, or a career, into a full-time job of studying HRV charts, counting steps at midnight to close a ring, or consulting a recovery score before agreeing to a walk with the grandchildren. If your watch has made you more anxious and no healthier, the watch is winning.

The goal is smaller and more useful. Once a quarter, look at a year of your own data honestly. Know which numbers to trust. Notice the slow drifts that no alert will ever announce. Change one or two things on purpose. And walk into your doctor’s office with a dated chart and three good questions instead of “I think it’s fine.”

Vikram, our hypothetical finance director, would never let a portfolio go unreviewed for four years. Your body has better returns, and nobody else is managing it.

So run the audit. Twelve months, one export, one honest dashboard and one conversation with someone qualified to read it.

Your watch may be smart. Your health decisions still need to be smarter.
