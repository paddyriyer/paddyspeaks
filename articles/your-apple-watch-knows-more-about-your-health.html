Read this first: one article, three depths

This is an educational guide to understanding your own data. It is not medical advice, and nothing here diagnoses anything. A watch can notice a change. A clinician decides what the change means.

Three ways to read it. The main text is written for everyone. Under the heart and sleep measures you will find two optional layers, closed until you open them: For the data-minded (sensors, sampling, uncertainty, reproducible analysis) and For the clinically curious (validation studies, guidelines and the limits of consumer measurements, informational only). Everything exhaustive lives in the reference.

Labels: Docs the manufacturer’s, FDA’s or a regulator’s own document. Guideline a professional-society guideline. Research a published study. Inference the author’s reasoning. Unknown not published by anyone. Synthetic invented data or people; never a real patient.

Features, models and regions were checked on 24 November 2026; Apple changes them every September and between releases. The feature-status table separates what is available from what is announced, in beta, or limited by region or model.

1

Data-rich, insight-poor

Synthetic Meet Vikram, 54, a finance director. He knows his portfolio’s year-to-date return to one decimal place. He knows when his car is due for its 60,000-mile service, the annual fee on each of his three credit cards, and which grocery store discounts olive oil on Thursdays. He has worn an Apple Watch for four years. It has quietly logged roughly a million heart-rate samples, more than a thousand nights of sleep and every flight of stairs he has climbed since the pandemic.

Ask him whether his cardiovascular fitness improved or declined over the last twelve months and he will say, with total confidence, “I think it’s fine.” He has not opened the Health app since he set it up. The watch is, in practice, a very expensive piece of wrist jewelry that occasionally tells him to stand up.

We have dashboards for server uptime and stock-market volatility. But when our own resting heart rate changes for three months, our monitoring strategy is apparently hope.

Engineers call it observability: reading a system’s signals so a slow drift is caught before it becomes an outage. Your watch records the signals. What is missing is the baseline, the trend line and the habit of looking. The opposite mistake is just as real: treating every orange ring as a crisis. A single reading is not a trend, most alerts are not disease, and some changes that matter never trigger an alert at all.

Collecting health data is not the same as understanding health. Understanding health is not the same as improving it.

2

Start here: your first 15 minutes

No Python, no export, no paid app. Just the Health app on your iPhone and a pen.

  1. 1Open Health on your iPhone. Apple is redesigning it (iOS 27.3, in beta), so names may move; search finds any measure by name.
  2. 2Resting heart rate, one year. Search “Resting Heart Rate”, tap it, choose Y. Note your usual range and whether the last three months sit higher, lower or the same.
  3. 3Sleep: length and regularity. Open Sleep, look at M and 6M. Is the time asleep steady? Are bedtimes scattered?
  4. 4Cardio fitness, if you have it. Look only at the direction over 6–12 months.
  5. 5Heart notifications and ECG. In Heart, check that Irregular Rhythm Notifications are on (if offered for your age, model and country) and whether you have any ECG recordings.
  6. 6Is the data really there, and recent? Tap Show All Data on each measure: the newest entry should be from today or yesterday, and the year should not be mostly blank. No → fix the plumbing before reading anything: wear the watch at night, check Health → your profile → Devices and Apps. A gap is a gap, not a normal result.
  7. 7Write one question. Not ten. For example: “My resting heart rate has been 5 beats higher since June. Why?”
  8. 8Know when to stop analyzing. Chest pain, fainting, severe breathlessness, stroke signs → call emergency services now (911 in the US). Do not take an ECG first. A notification, or a new symptom → book an appointment and bring the one-page summary. Just curious → carry on to the next section.

That is the whole beginner path. Everything below makes it better; nothing below is required to start.

3

The numbers worth knowing

Five kinds of number share the Health app, all in the same calm typography. Know which kind you are looking at.

Measured a sensor reading: heart rate, a single-lead ECG. Still imperfect.
Estimated an algorithm’s inference: VO₂ max, sleep stages. Good for trends, poor as absolutes.
Entered / Device / Record what you type, what a cuff or scale sends, what your records hold.
Score a proprietary composite (Readiness, recovery, sleep score). A summary you cannot audit.
Not measured blood pressure numbers, glucose, cholesterol. Not by the watch, whatever the marketing implies.

Each measure answers the same five questions; optional layers sit underneath. The full inventory is in the reference.

Resting heart rate Estimated

What it means
Your heart rate while you are still and calm, estimated by the watch from readings taken during quiet moments.
Why care
It moves with sleep, stress, illness and fitness; in large studies a higher resting rate goes with poorer long-term outcomes Research.
What changes it
Short sleep, alcohol, illness, heat, dehydration, stress, less exercise, and medicines: beta-blockers lower it; some decongestants and thyroid medicines raise it.
What to do
Read the 28-day average against your usual range, never a friend’s. Context before conclusions.
When to ask
A rise that lasts weeks without an obvious reason, or any change that comes with palpitations, dizziness, breathlessness or chest discomfort.
For the data-minded

Apple does not publish exactly how resting heart rate is derived Unknown; the export holds one value per day, as a whole number of beats per minute. Against a chest strap, Series 9 and Ultra 2 resting rates were relatively accurate (O’Grady et al., 2024) Research. Because values are integers, medians move in steps of 1 bpm: a “1 bpm rise” may be rounding. Require a minimum wear time per day before a value counts (this guide uses 12 hours), and never compare a period that includes a new watch with one that does not without checking the step at the change.

For the clinically curious

Rate is not rhythm. Heart rate counts beats per minute; rhythm is their pattern and origin. A normal rate can sit on an abnormal rhythm, and the resting-rate figure says nothing about rhythm. The watch derives rate optically (photoplethysmography, PPG: light reflected by pulsing blood in the wrist), which is reliable at rest and less so during vigorous or irregular movement; validity falls as exercise intensity rises (Wang et al., 2017; Bent et al., 2020) Research. Persistent resting tachycardia or bradycardia, particularly with symptoms, is something a clinician interprets alongside history, medications and a standard ECG; the wearable value is context, not a measurement of record.

Heart rhythm: notifications and the ECG app Measured Classified

What it means
Irregular rhythm notifications watch your pulse pattern optically while you are still. The ECG app records a 30-second electrical tracing from your fingertip and labels it: sinus rhythm, atrial fibrillation (AFib), high or low heart rate, or inconclusive Docs.
Why care
AFib is common, often silent, and raises the risk of stroke. These tools can prompt a timely conversation.
What changes it
Recording quality: moving, talking, a loose band, a wrist not resting on a table, cold hands, poor contact.
What to do
If notified, or the ECG says AFib: contact your clinician and export the PDF (Health → Heart → Electrocardiograms → Export a PDF for Your Doctor). Record an ECG during palpitations.
When to ask
Any AFib classification or irregular-rhythm notification; recurrent palpitations, fainting or breathlessness. Apple’s own page is blunt: the ECG app cannot detect a heart attack, blood clots or a stroke Docs.
For the data-minded

The notification feature analyzes PPG “tachograms” (series of pulse intervals) captured only while you are still, so its coverage depends on how much of the day you sit quietly Docs. The ECG app’s classification covers set heart-rate ranges (50–120 bpm for version 1, 50–150 for version 2); outside them, or with noise, the result is inconclusive Docs. In the export, ECG recordings arrive as CSV voltage samples in their own folder; the PDF is what a clinician wants. Treat an inconclusive result as missing data, not as normal.

For the clinically curious
  • Electrical versus optical. The ECG app records electrical activity (a single channel similar to lead I); the notification feature infers rhythm from optical pulse timing. They fail differently: PPG with motion and poor perfusion, the single lead with poor contact and low amplitude.
  • Single lead versus 12-lead. A 12-lead ECG views the heart from twelve angles, which is how it shows ischemia, infarction, conduction disease and many structural clues that one lead cannot. A single lead is a rhythm strip.
  • Sinus rhythm versus an AFib classification. In Apple’s clearance study (about 600 participants, compared with physician-read 12-lead ECGs), AFib sensitivity was 98.3% and sinus-rhythm specificity 99.6%, counting only classifiable recordings Docs. Independent work is less flattering in real cardiology patients: in the BASEL Wearable Study, 18% of Apple Watch 6 tracings were inconclusive, which lowered effective sensitivity and specificity (Mannhart et al., 2023) Research; a 2025 meta-analysis pooled Apple Watch sensitivity at 94% and specificity at 97% across studies Research.
  • Notifications. In the Apple Heart Study (419,297 participants), 0.52% received a notification; among those with a simultaneous patch ECG, AFib was present 84% of the time (Perez et al., 2019) Research. The FDA’s De Novo summary records that the sub-study’s pre-specified goal for spot-tachogram positive predictive value was not met Docs. The feature is not intended for people already diagnosed with AFib.
  • What the guideline says. The 2023 ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS AF guideline accepts that AF can be documented by wearables, but such documentation always requires visual confirmation that the diagnosis is accurate Guideline. That review, a standard 12-lead ECG, ambulatory monitoring for intermittent symptoms (a Holter, patch or event monitor), laboratory tests and an assessment of stroke risk are among the things a clinician might consider. Which, if any, is a clinical judgment.

Heart-rate variability (HRV) Measured, derived

What it means
How unevenly your heartbeats are spaced. More variation at rest generally reflects a more relaxed, recovered nervous system.
Why care
Your own trend can show strain from poor sleep, illness, alcohol or hard training.
What changes it
Almost everything: time of day, breathing, posture, a late meal, a glass of wine, a cold, a hard workout, age.
What to do
Use 7-day and 28-day averages from one device. Never compare your number with someone else’s, or with a ring or strap.
When to ask
HRV alone is not a reason to see a doctor. Symptoms are.
For the data-minded

HRV is a family of statistics. Apple Health stores SDNN (the standard deviation of beat intervals) from short readings taken when you are still, a few times a day on most watches Docs. Many rings and straps report RMSSD (based on differences between consecutive beats), often only during sleep. The two have different typical values and respond differently (Shaffer & Ginsberg, 2017) Research. The Series 12 and Ultra 4 sample far more often and add “Recovery HRV”; Apple has not published which statistic it uses Unknown. Treat a watch change as a break in the series: the synthetic example shows a 6 ms “drop” that is entirely sampling.

For the clinically curious

Against a Polar H10 chest strap analyzed with Kubios, Apple Watch Series 9 and Ultra 2 underestimated SDNN by about 8 ms on average and did not meet the study’s pre-set equivalence margin; the authors concluded watch HRV does not yet meet clinical accuracy standards (O’Grady et al., 2024) Research. Short-term HRV norms depend on recording length, posture, breathing and method, so population reference values do not transfer to ultra-short wrist readings (Shaffer & Ginsberg, 2017) Research. Use it as a within-person trend, not as a diagnostic marker.

Cardio fitness (estimated VO₂ max) and cardio recovery Estimated

What it means
An estimate of your peak oxygen use, from heart rate during brisk outdoor walks, runs or hikes (classified from age 20) Docs. Cardio recovery is how far heart rate drops in the minute after a workout.
Why care
Fitness is one of the strongest predictors of long-term survival in large cohorts (Kodama et al., 2009; Mandsager et al., 2018) Research. It is also one of the most changeable.
What changes it
How often you walk briskly outdoors (no brisk walks, no estimates), heart-rate-limiting medicines, illness, and real fitness.
What to do
Judge the direction over two or more quarters; add two sessions a week where talking in full sentences gets hard.
When to ask
If exertion newly brings chest discomfort, unusual breathlessness, dizziness or ankle swelling; or before starting vigorous exercise if you have heart disease or several risk factors.
For the data-minded

Apple’s estimate covers 14–65 mL/kg/min; heart-rate-limiting medicines can make it read high, which is why Health asks about them Docs. Estimates arrive irregularly (several a week in summer, sometimes none for a month in winter), so a quarterly median with its count is more honest than a line through every point. Cardio recovery depends heavily on how hard and how abruptly you stopped; compare like-for-like workouts only.

For the clinically curious

Against laboratory testing, Apple Watch underestimated VO₂ max by about 6 mL/kg/min on average, a mean absolute percentage error of about 13%, with wide limits of agreement (Lambe et al., 2025) Research; the authors called for refinement before clinical use. The prognostic studies used measured or treadmill-estimated fitness, not wrist estimates. Heart-rate recovery has prognostic value when measured with standardized exercise-test protocols (Cole et al., 1999) Research; a watch’s post-workout figure does not follow such a protocol and is not interchangeable with it. Where fitness or exertional symptoms matter clinically, formal exercise testing is a clinician’s tool.

Sleep: duration, regularity, stages, and apnea notifications Estimated

What it means
Time asleep and bedtimes, estimated from movement and heart rate; stages are a further estimate. Supported watches can flag possible sleep apnea from 30 days of breathing disturbances Docs.
Why care
Regular sleep predicted mortality better than sleep duration in a large UK Biobank analysis (Windred et al., 2024) Research. Untreated moderate-to-severe sleep apnea is common and often undiagnosed.
What changes it
Schedules, caffeine and alcohol, travel, children, stress, pain, a warm bedroom.
What to do
Track bedtime spread, not just hours. A fixed wake time is the most reliable first experiment. Ignore nightly stage swings.
When to ask
Loud snoring or pauses in breathing someone has noticed, morning headaches, dozing while driving, lasting insomnia, or a sleep apnea notification.
For the data-minded

Sleep arrives as category samples (Asleep Core, Deep, REM, Unspecified, In Bed, Awake), often from several apps at once. Merge overlapping “asleep” intervals rather than adding them, exclude “in bed”, and assign each sample to the night it began (noon to noon is a workable rule). The reference scripts do exactly this and are tested on a sample where two sources overlap.

For the clinically curious

Against polysomnography, consumer devices including the Apple Watch Series 8 detect sleep versus wake well, with weaker stage accuracy; in one study the watch underestimated deep sleep by about 43 minutes (Robbins et al., 2024; Schyvens et al., 2025) Research. For sleep apnea notifications, the FDA clearance summary reports 66.3% sensitivity for moderate-to-severe apnea and 98.5% specificity; Apple’s own paper gives 66.6% and 95.9% on its test set Docs. Roughly one in three people with moderate-to-severe apnea in those data were not notified. A notification is a reason to discuss formal sleep testing; its absence does not exclude apnea.

Blood pressure and hypertension notifications Not measured Notification

What it means
The watch does not measure blood pressure. On Series 9 or Ultra 2 and later, hypertension notifications look for patterns in 30 days of optical data, for adults 22 and over, not pregnant, with no existing diagnosis Docs. No numbers are produced.
Why care
High blood pressure is common, usually silent, and one of the most treatable risks for heart attack and stroke.
What changes it
Salt, alcohol, weight, activity, sleep, pain, medicines, and how it is measured (cuff size, posture, talking).
What to do
Measure it properly, with a cuff from a validated-device list (in the US, validatebp.org). The 2025 AHA/ACC guideline encourages home monitoring with validated devices and does not recommend cuffless devices such as smartwatches for measuring blood pressure Guideline.
When to ask
After a notification (take a week of cuff readings first if you can), or whenever home readings are consistently raised.

For the clinically curious

The feature was cleared by the FDA through the 510(k) pathway (K250507, September 2025) Docs. Its pivotal study compared notifications with home cuff readings in more than 2,000 participants. A 2026 JAMA analysis applying those figures to the US population (NHANES) concluded that many people with undiagnosed hypertension would receive no notification, and that wearable notifications should supplement, not replace, standard screening (Cohen et al., 2026) Research. Commentary in Hypertension (2025) notes that clearance does not require validation under an international blood-pressure-measurement protocol. The 2025 guideline’s routine for home readings, as summarized for clinicians, is two readings in the morning before eating and two before bed, with a validated device Guideline. Diagnosis rests on properly measured readings, not on a notification.

Walking speed and steadiness Estimated

Your iPhone, carried near the waist, estimates walking speed, step length and asymmetry, and classifies walking steadiness as OK, Low or Very Low as a fall-risk estimate Docs. Gait speed in older adults is associated with survival (Studenski et al., 2011) Research. Watch the slow drift; a sudden change often means a different pocket. Ask after a fall, new weakness, or a decline over months.

What your watch cannot tell you

This section matters more than any metric above, because the dangerous mistake is not alarm. It is reassurance.

Four columns: what the watch measures, what it estimates, what it does not measure, and what requires clinical assessment.

Measured

  • Pulse rate (optical)
  • A 30-second single-lead ECG, when you take one
  • Movement, steps, stairs
  • Wrist-temperature change from your baseline
  • Sound levels

Estimated

  • Resting heart rate, HRV statistics
  • VO₂ max (about 13% typical error)
  • Sleep and its stages
  • Walking steadiness
  • Calories, breathing disturbances

Not measured

  • Blood pressure numbers
  • Glucose, HbA1c
  • Cholesterol
  • Kidney function
  • Smoking, family history, medicines (unless you enter them)

Requires clinical assessment

  • Chest pain, fainting, new breathlessness
  • Heart attack, coronary or valve disease, heart failure
  • A diagnosis of AFib, hypertension or sleep apnea
  • Any decision about medicines
  • Conditions it does not screen for: coronary disease, heart failure, valve disease, high cholesterol, diabetes. Apple says the ECG app cannot detect a heart attack, blood clots or a stroke Docs.
  • “Sinus rhythm” is a 30-second, one-lead statement. It does not rule out an intermittent rhythm problem, a heart attack, or structural heart disease.
  • No hypertension notification is not normal blood pressure. In the 1,000-person example above, most people with undiagnosed high blood pressure hear nothing.
  • No sleep apnea notification is not a clean sleep study. About a third of moderate-to-severe cases were missed in validation.
  • Estimates are not laboratory tests. VO₂ max, sleep stages and calories are models of you, not measurements of you.
  • Coverage is partial. The watch sees only what happens while it is worn, charged and still enough; a 2025 AHA scientific statement notes that light-based readings may perform differently across skin tones Guideline.
  • Your biggest risks are mostly elsewhere. The AHA’s Life’s Essential 8 lists eight measures of cardiovascular health: diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep, body weight, cholesterol, blood glucose and blood pressure Guideline. The watch speaks to two of them. The PREVENT risk equations (Khan et al., 2024) used in the 2025 blood-pressure guideline need age, sex, cholesterol, blood pressure, diabetes, smoking and kidney function; the watch supplies none of these.

None of this makes the watch useless. It makes it a narrow instrument with a wide reputation. The fix is not fear: it is a cuff, a regular check-up with blood tests, and telling your clinician about symptoms rather than scores.

5

What to do next: a decision tree and six short scenarios

  1. Chest pain or pressure, fainting, severe breathlessness, or stroke signs (face drooping, arm weakness, speech trouble)? Yes → call emergency services now (911 in the US). Not an ECG, not a chatbot. No ↓
  2. New or recurring symptoms: palpitations, breathlessness on exertion, dizziness? Yes → book an appointment; bring the ECG PDFs and the one-page summary. No ↓
  3. A medical notification: irregular rhythm, an AFib ECG, hypertension, or sleep apnea? Yes → book an appointment; do not self-treat or change medicines. No ↓
  4. A sustained change: four weeks or more outside your usual range? Yes → check context, change one habit, recheck in 4–8 weeks; if it persists unexplained, raise it at your next visit. No ↓
  5. A single odd day, or a red score? Note it. Look again in a week. Then go outside.

A · Resting heart rate creeping up while sleep shrinks

Data: 28-day average up 6 bpm over ten weeks; sleep down an hour. Next: fixed wake time, earlier last drink, recheck in four weeks. Professional: if it persists, or with palpitations, breathlessness or raised home blood pressure.

B · Cardio fitness sliding despite daily walks

Data: eleven estimates over six months, 34 → 31: within the estimate’s error. Next: brisker walks; judge over three months. Professional: if exertion brings new chest discomfort, breathlessness or dizziness.

C · Irregular sleep, poor recovery

Data: bedtimes spread over three hours; HRV below its usual band. Next: wake time first, lighter training for a week. Professional: snoring, gasping, daytime sleepiness, persistent insomnia.

D · Walking speed slowly falling

Data: a year of slower walking; steadiness now Low. Next: strength and balance training. Professional: at your next visit with the chart; sooner after a fall.

E · “Sinus rhythm”, but the symptoms continue

Data: three normal-looking ECGs during fluttering and light-headedness. Next: a symptom diary; keep recording. Professional: now, by appointment; immediately, through emergency services, for chest pain or fainting.

F · A red recovery score on an ordinary morning

Data: “14% recovered”, everything else in range. Next: train lighter if you like, otherwise live your day; if scores make you anxious, hide them. Professional: only with symptoms, or several Vitals outliers for days.

All six are Synthetic composites. Two lines no scenario crosses: no diagnosis from a wearable trend, and no medication change on consumer data alone.

6

The 12-month audit, in brief

Yesterday’s numbers tell you about yesterday. A year tells you about you. The full method, code and results are in the reference; here is its shape.

  1. Count before you conclude: days with data, days usable (12 hours of wear for resting heart rate), longest gap.
  2. Fix the known traps: partial days, short wear, irregular sampling, duplicate sources, travel time zones, sleep that spans midnight, new watches and software updates, different HRV statistics.
  3. Choose the baseline by a rule, before looking, for example the first quarter, excluding logged illness and travel.
  4. Compare non-overlapping periods. With one year of data, compare quarters within it. A same-season comparison with last year needs at least two years of coverage. Overlapping windows are never independent comparisons.
  5. Separate noise from change. An outlier is one day far from its recent median; a sustained change is a 28-day median outside your baseline range for weeks.
  6. Write each finding as Observation → Possible explanation → Evidence quality → Next step, and list what could not be assessed. Missing data is never a normal result.

Here it is on one Synthetic year: an invented person with a cold in February, a trip in March, a new job in June, no watch for ten days in August, a new watch in September. Every number is computed from the published data.

Resting heart rate rose in summer, and stayed up

Observation
Median resting heart rate was 58 bpm in the baseline quarter (23 Nov 2025–21 Feb 2026, 81 valid days, illness week excluded) and 60 bpm in 24 May–22 Aug 2026 (79 valid days): a difference of +2.0 bpm, with a resampling interval of +2.0 to +3.0 bpm. In 23 Aug–22 Nov it was 62 bpm (88 valid days). Resting values are whole numbers, so medians move in steps of 1 bpm. The 28-day rolling median stayed above the baseline's upper quartile for 138 consecutive days (08 Jul–22 Nov 2026).
Possible explanation
Sleep fell in the same months (next finding) and the notes record a new job from 01 Jun. Heat, less exercise, alcohol or a medication change could do the same.
Evidence quality
Moderate: enough valid days and a sustained run, but one person's data cannot separate the explanations.
Next step
Compare with sleep and context first. If it persists, or comes with symptoms, take it to a clinician with the chart.

Two routes to your own. No code: the Health app’s Y view, a notebook, four numbers a quarter (resting heart rate, time asleep, bedtime spread, usable days). With code: two tested Python scripts in the reference.

8

Privacy: your most intimate data deserves protection

A full Health export can hold your ECGs, medications, lab results, mood logs and years of GPS routes that start at your front door. Almost none of it is needed to ask “has my resting heart rate gone up?”

In the US, HIPAA covers health data held by doctors, hospitals, insurers and their contractors. It generally does not cover what you hand a consumer app. Those apps fall mostly to the Federal Trade Commission, whose Health Breach Notification Rule (expanded in 2024) requires many of them to tell you about unauthorized disclosure Docs. That is a duty to notify, not a guarantee of privacy.

  • Grant HealthKit permissions one category at a time. A sleep app does not need your clinical records.
  • Share summaries, not exports. Strip your name, date of birth (give an age), device names and every GPS route.
  • Protect the source. Use a passcode and two-factor authentication; Apple lists Health data among the iCloud categories that are end-to-end encrypted. Delete stray copies of export.zip.
  • Read the AI service’s own terms. OpenAI says data connected to ChatGPT Health is kept separately and not used to train its models; Anthropic says the same of health data connected to Claude Docs. These are the companies’ statements, which this article cannot audit; files pasted into an ordinary chat may follow ordinary settings.

Permission to access is not permission to redistribute. An app that may read your heart rate has not thereby earned the right to share it, and neither has an assistant you gave a file to.

The AI analyst prompt

Used well, an AI assistant is an excellent junior analyst. Used badly, it is a confident stranger guessing at your heart. This prompt asks for the first and forbids the second: fifteen steps, from consent to what could not be assessed, with one output for you and one for a specialist.

The AI should act as an analytical assistant, not a virtual cardiologist.

Download it as a text file

PERSONAL HEALTH-DATA ANALYST: MASTER PROMPT (PaddySpeaks, version 2.0, November 2026)
Copy everything from here to the end. Fill in the [BRACKETS]. Share only the data you have decided to share.

ROLE
You are my personal health-data analyst. You are not a physician and must not act like one. You organize, check and describe MY OWN data; separate what is observed from what is inferred and what is unknown; and help me prepare a useful, honest conversation with a qualified clinician. You never diagnose. You never tell me to start, stop or change a medication or its dose. You never tell me that a symptom is nothing. You never invent, estimate or "fill in" a measurement that is not in the data I have given you.

SAFETY CHECK (before anything else, and again whenever I mention a symptom)
If I mention chest pain, pressure or tightness; fainting or near-fainting; severe or sudden shortness of breath; signs of stroke (face drooping, arm or leg weakness, difficulty speaking, sudden confusion or loss of vision); or a very fast or very slow heartbeat with dizziness or breathlessness: stop the analysis and tell me to contact emergency services now. Say plainly that a normal-looking wearable reading never outranks a symptom.

STEP 1 - ACCESS AND EXPLICIT CONSENT
List exactly what you can access in this conversation: each connected source, each uploaded file, and the data categories inside them. If you cannot see any data, say so and stop; do not describe trends you cannot see. Before you read, request or process anything more sensitive (clinical records, medications, reproductive or sexual health, mental health, location or workout routes), name it and ask my permission. Use only what the question needs.

STEP 2 - DATA INVENTORY
Make a table of every data type present: name, unit, source device or app (and model if shown), and what kind of number it is: a sensor measurement, an algorithmic estimate, a user entry, a connected medical device, a medical record, or a proprietary score.

STEP 3 - SOURCE VERIFICATION AND DATE COVERAGE
For each data type: first and last date, number of days with data in the analysis period, the longest gap, and any change of device, watch model or software version (look at source names and versions). Flag partial first and last days. State the analysis period you will use: [START DATE] to [END DATE] (default: the last 365 days).

STEP 4 - VALIDATION AND DEDUPLICATION
Check for duplicate samples, the same measure recorded by two sources (iPhone and Watch both count steps; never add them; use one source per day, or my priority order: [SOURCE PRIORITY]), overlapping sleep records from different apps (merge overlapping time, never add it; "in bed" is not "asleep"), implausible values, and days with too little wear to count (say what rule you used, e.g. at least 12 hours with heart-rate samples). Report every issue and exactly what you did about it.

STEP 5 - UNIT AND TIMESTAMP NORMALIZATION
Keep every value in its original unit unless converting, and say when you convert (lb/kg, mg/dL/mmol/L). Group by the LOCAL calendar date in the original timestamp, not by UTC, and note any time-zone changes (travel). Assign sleep to the night it began. Note daylight-saving changes.

STEP 6 - PERSONAL BASELINE
Choose a baseline window by a rule you state BEFORE looking at the results (for example: the first full quarter with at least 80% usable days, excluding logged illness and travel). Give the median and interquartile range (the middle half) for each measure, with the number of usable days. Never use population averages as my baseline. For HRV, never mix devices or HRV methods (Apple stores SDNN; many rings and straps report RMSSD).

STEP 7 - SHORT- AND LONG-TERM TRENDS
Use 7-day rolling medians for short-term change and 28-day rolling medians for long-term change, and compare NON-OVERLAPPING periods (for example, four quarters of one year). Never describe overlapping windows as independent comparisons. A same-season comparison with last year needs at least two years of data; if I do not have them, say so rather than improvising.

STEP 8 - MEANINGFUL CHANGES
Separate one-off outliers (a day far above its own recent median, often illness, travel or alcohol) from sustained shifts (a rolling median outside my baseline range for weeks). For each change give its size, its direction, its dates and the number of usable days behind it.

STEP 9 - MEASUREMENT UNCERTAINTY
For each finding, state the evidence quality (high, moderate or low) and why: coverage, sampling, wear, device changes, algorithm updates, and the known limits of the measurement (VO2 max is an estimate with errors of roughly 13% in validation studies; sleep stages are estimates; HRV from a watch is noisier than from a chest strap; optical heart rate struggles during vigorous movement). Where a simple interval is possible (for example a bootstrap interval for a difference in medians), give it and explain it in plain words.

STEP 10 - INTERACTIONS BETWEEN MEASURES
Examine at most three relationships I choose in advance: [e.g. sleep duration and next-day resting heart rate; training load and HRV; late bedtimes and sleep duration]. Respect time order. Say clearly that association is not causation and that testing many pairs produces false patterns.

STEP 11 - MEDICAL LIMITS AND SYMPTOM SAFETY
Repeat the safety check if any symptom appears. State what these data cannot show: a "sinus rhythm" ECG covers only one 30-second single-lead recording and cannot detect a heart attack; hypertension notifications miss more cases than they catch; sleep apnea notifications miss some cases; the watch measures neither blood pressure nor blood glucose; cholesterol, diabetes, smoking, family history, kidney function and medications are not in wearable data unless I add them. Absent data is "not assessed", never "normal".

STEP 12 - PRACTICAL LIFESTYLE CONSIDERATIONS
Offer at most three evidence-supported habits, each tied to a specific finding (for example a fixed wake time for irregular sleep, brisk walking for falling cardio-fitness estimates). Cite a source for each. No supplements, no medications, no diets for medical conditions, no universal targets without my clinical context.

STEP 13 - 30/60/90-DAY FRAMEWORK
Days 1-30: fix data quality and measure the baseline. Days 31-60: one or two habit changes, each with a process goal I control (for example "lights out by 23:00 on 5 of 7 nights"). Days 61-90: compare with the baseline using the same rules, report adherence, list confounders, and decide what to keep.

STEP 14 - PHYSICIAN-FACING SUMMARY
Draft "What My Apple Watch Found - Information for My Clinician", at most one page and readable in two minutes: my age and context [AGE, RELEVANT CONDITIONS - only what I volunteer]; the main reason I am seeking advice; symptoms with dates and timing; device model(s) and data sources; measurement coverage and quality; dated, factual trend observations with numbers and usable-day counts; ECG classifications with dates (and note that the PDFs are attached, if I attach them); medications and history only if I have supplied them; the main uncertainties and missing measurements; and up to three specific questions. Neutral language. No diagnosis, no interpretation beyond the data, no treatment suggestions.

STEP 15 - WHAT IS UNAVAILABLE OR UNANSWERED
List every measure you could not assess and why (not collected, too sparse, unreliable, not shared with you), and every question these data cannot answer. Do not soften this list.

CITATIONS
Cite a source for every medical recommendation and every claim about what a device can or cannot measure: the manufacturer's own documentation, an FDA document, a professional-society guideline (ACC/AHA) or a peer-reviewed study, with a working link. If you cannot cite it, label it as your reasoning or leave it out. Note that features differ by watch model, software version and country.

LABELS
Tag every statement OBSERVED (directly in the data), POSSIBLE EXPLANATION (one of several), or UNKNOWN (cannot be assessed, with the reason).

OUTPUT FORMAT A - FOR ME (plain English, under 400 words)
1. What you could see (one sentence per source) and what you could not.
2. Three things that changed, each with dates, size and how sure we can be.
3. One thing that did not change and is worth knowing.
4. Up to three habits to try, each with a measurable process goal.
5. When to talk to a professional, and what to bring.
6. What these data cannot tell me.

OUTPUT FORMAT B - FOR A SPECIALIST (structured, terse)
Data sources and models; analysis period; coverage table (measure, unit, usable days / total, longest gap); validation and deduplication rules applied; baseline window and rule; per-measure median (IQR, n) by non-overlapping period; changes with intervals; outliers with dates and annotations; device or algorithm changes; uncertainty notes; relationships tested; not assessed; questions. Then the one-page clinician summary from Step 14.

STYLE
Plain English; explain each technical term once. Calm and specific. Do not reassure me beyond the data, and do not alarm me beyond it either. If you are unsure, say so.

It has been checked for completeness against the fifteen steps; how well an assistant follows it depends on the assistant, so read its answer with the same care as the data. Give it the daily summary rather than the full export whenever you can.

The checklist

  • I know which of my numbers are measured, estimated, scores, or not measured at all.
  • Sleep tracking is on and I wear the watch at night at least five nights a week.
  • The notifications my watch supports are on, and I know none of them can clear me of anything.
  • I checked that each measure has recent data, and I wrote down my gaps.
  • I chose at most two habits, each with a process goal.
  • I measure blood pressure with a validated cuff, or I have decided I need one.
  • I share summaries, not exports, and I know each app’s terms.
  • I have no more than three questions for my clinician, each with its evidence.
  • I know the emergency signs, and I will never take an ECG instead of calling for help.

Make technology serve your health

Nobody needs to turn retirement into a full-time job of studying HRV charts, counting steps at midnight, or consulting a recovery score before agreeing to a walk with the grandchildren. If your watch has made you more anxious and no healthier, the watch is winning.

The goal is smaller and more useful. Once a quarter, look at a year of your own data honestly. Know which numbers to trust. Notice the slow drifts no alert will ever announce. Change one or two things on purpose. And walk into your doctor’s office with a dated chart and three good questions instead of “I think it’s fine.”

Vikram would never let a portfolio go unreviewed for four years. Your body has better returns, and nobody else is managing it.

Your watch may be smart. Your health decisions still need to be smarter.
