R4

The 12-month audit method

A reproducible method. Every rule is stated before the data are looked at, and every result carries its denominator.

ProblemWhat goes wrongRule used here
Incomplete wear timeA resting value from a day worn for five hours is not comparable with one worn for twenty.Count hours with at least one heart-rate sample; resting heart rate counts only on days with 12 or more.
Irregular samplingHRV arrives a few times a day, VO₂ a few times a month, ECG on demand. Averages hide how few readings stand behind them.Report the number of samples and usable days beside every summary.
Device generationsA new watch samples differently; a level shift appears overnight.Treat a device change as a break; start a new baseline for affected measures (HRV especially).
Algorithm versionsA watchOS update can change an estimate without anything changing in you.Record sourceVersion changes and the update date on the timeline.
Timestamps and time zonesGrouping by UTC moves an evening walk to tomorrow; travel and daylight saving shift days.Group by the local date in the original timestamp; keep the offset; annotate travel.
Duplicates and overlapsiPhone and Watch both count steps; two sleep apps record the same night.One source per day for steps (priority order or largest single source); merge overlapping sleep intervals.
Partial daysThe first and last day of an export are incomplete.Flag them and leave them out of averages.
Different HRV measuresSDNN and RMSSD are different statistics with different typical values.Never mix them; never compare devices.
Sleep-session boundariesA night spans two calendar dates; naps can be double counted.A sample belongs to the night it began (noon to noon); “in bed” is not “asleep”.
ConfoundingIllness, travel, medicines, heat, a new job: each can move several measures at once.Annotate events; exclude illness and travel from the baseline; state explanations as possibilities.
Random versus sustained changeOne bad week looks like a trend; a slow drift looks like noise.Outlier: a day at least 6 bpm above its own trailing 28-day median. Sustained: the 28-day median above the baseline’s upper quartile for 28 days or more.

Comparison windows (corrected in this edition)

Edition 1 suggested comparing “the last 90 days with the same 90 days a year ago”. With only 365 days of data that is impossible: those two windows cannot both exist inside one year. The corrected rule:

  • With 365 days: compare non-overlapping periods inside the year, for example four quarters, against a baseline chosen by a rule stated in advance.
  • For a same-season comparison (this autumn against last autumn): you need at least two full years, 730 days, of coverage of that measure. Otherwise, say it cannot be assessed.
  • Overlapping windows are never independent comparisons. A 28-day rolling median is for seeing shape, not for testing differences.

Statistics, in plain words

Median and interquartile range (IQR): the middle value and the middle half of your days; robust to the odd extreme day. Bootstrap interval: resample your days thousands of times to see how much a difference in medians could move by chance alone; it describes uncertainty in your own data, not a clinical threshold. Whole numbers: resting heart rate is recorded in whole beats, so medians move in steps of 1 bpm and intervals can look blocky.

Coverage comes first

Observation
Resting heart rate exists on 355 of 365 days; 344 pass the valid-day rule (worn at least 12 hours, not a partial day). The watch was not worn for 10 days in August, and 9 days carry a resting value despite less than 12 hours of wear.
Possible explanation
Holiday, charging overnight, forgotten mornings. None of it is physiology.
Evidence quality
High: wear hours and sample dates are recorded directly.
Next step
Report the denominator with every number. Treat the August gap as missing, never as normal.

Resting heart rate rose in summer, and stayed up

Observation
Median resting heart rate was 58 bpm in the baseline quarter (23 Nov 2025–21 Feb 2026, 81 valid days, illness week excluded) and 60 bpm in 24 May–22 Aug 2026 (79 valid days): a difference of +2.0 bpm, with a resampling interval of +2.0 to +3.0 bpm. In 23 Aug–22 Nov it was 62 bpm (88 valid days). Resting values are whole numbers, so medians move in steps of 1 bpm. The 28-day rolling median stayed above the baseline's upper quartile for 138 consecutive days (08 Jul–22 Nov 2026).
Possible explanation
Sleep fell in the same months (next finding) and the notes record a new job from 01 Jun. Heat, less exercise, alcohol or a medication change could do the same.
Evidence quality
Moderate: enough valid days and a sustained run, but one person's data cannot separate the explanations.
Next step
Compare with sleep and context first. If it persists, or comes with symptoms, take it to a clinician with the chart.

Sleep got shorter and less regular

Observation
Median time asleep fell from 7 h 12 min (baseline) to 6 h 28 min in Q3 (−44 min; interval −58 to −31 min). The spread of bedtimes (standard deviation) widened from 25 to 45 minutes, and the median bedtime moved from 22:39 to 23:10.
Possible explanation
A later, more irregular schedule. Sleep stages are not used: they are estimates.
Evidence quality
Moderate: duration from the watch is reasonable for trends; bedtime is reliable.
Next step
A fixed wake time is the first experiment. Recheck the 28-day medians after a month.

The illness week is an outlier, not a trend

Observation
5 days sat at least 6 bpm above the previous day's 28-day median; 5 of them fall in the logged illness or travel days.
Possible explanation
A cold, and jet lag on the first days in London.
Evidence quality
High for 'something happened'; it says nothing about what.
Next step
Keep the days, annotate them, and leave them out of the baseline. Nothing to act on now.

HRV cannot be compared across the watch change

Observation
On the Series 9, median HRV (SDNN) was 40.0 ms over 236 days with a median of 3 readings a day; on the Series 12 from 18 Sep, 34.4 ms over 65 days with 47 readings a day. Within the Series 9 alone, the median fell from 42.0 ms (Q1) to 36.8 ms (Q3).
Possible explanation
Across devices: a different sensor sampling far more often, across the whole day, so the drop is mostly measurement. Within the Series 9: consistent with the shorter, later sleep.
Evidence quality
Low for any cross-device comparison.
Next step
Start a new HRV baseline on the new watch. Compare a device only with itself.

Cardio fitness: a small dip, inside the estimate's error

Observation
Estimates per quarter: Q1 9, Q2 12, Q3 13, Q4 21. Quarterly medians: Q1 33.4, Q2 34.8, Q3 33.1, Q4 32.6 mL/kg/min: a range of 2.2.
Possible explanation
Possibly less fitness in a busy year; possibly just which walks were brisk enough to count.
Evidence quality
Low: the quarterly medians differ by less than the estimate's typical error (about 13%, roughly 4.3 mL/kg/min at this level), and winter produced few estimates.
Next step
Keep up brisk outdoor walks so estimates keep coming; judge the direction over two or more quarters.

What this year cannot tell you

Observation
There is no earlier year, so there is no same-season comparison: that needs at least two years (730 days) of coverage. There is no blood pressure, no laboratory data, no symptoms log and no medication list in this dataset.
Possible explanation
The data were never collected.
Evidence quality
Not applicable: absence is a finding, not a normal result.
Next step
Note it on the clinician summary. Add a validated cuff if blood pressure matters to you.

R6

Scripts, sample output and the no-code route

The no-code route

  1. In Health, open each measure you care about and choose Y (a year) and 6M.
  2. Once a quarter, write four numbers in a notebook: typical resting heart rate, typical time asleep, how scattered bedtimes were, and how many days had data.
  3. Compare this quarter with the previous ones. Look for changes that last, not for single days.
  4. Copy the numbers onto the clinician summary if something persists.

That is enough for most people. The scripts below are for those who want the whole year in a table.

The Python route

Two short, standard-library scripts (Python 3.8 or later, nothing to install). They are teaching tools, not a validated analysis: they show the decisions a careful analysis must make, on your own computer, without uploading anything.

  • health_to_csv.py streams export.xml into records.csv. Memory stays flat for multi-gigabyte exports, because every finished element is discarded from the tree. It checks that the file really is an Apple Health export, skips damaged records and says which, and keeps original timestamps, the UTC offset and units.
  • daily_summary.py makes one row per day: steps from one source (never summed; optional --priority), resting heart rate, HRV with its sample count, sleep merged across overlapping sources and assigned to the night it began, wear hours, and partial-day flags. It stops with a clear message if a measure arrives in mixed units.

When HealthKit statistics are better. Apple’s own statistics (the Health app’s charts, or apps built on HKStatisticsCollectionQuery) merge sources minute by minute using your source priority. For steps, distance and energy they are more accurate than any per-day choice of a single source. The export does not include those merged totals, which is why the script has to choose.

Sample input and its real output

The sample export is synthetic and small, built to contain the hard cases: two sources counting the same steps, two apps recording the same night, a flight from New York to London, partial first and last days, and one damaged record. Corrected in this edition

  • Comparison windows. Edition 1 compared “the last 90 days with the same 90 days a year ago”, which a single year cannot support. Now: non-overlapping quarters within the year; a same-season comparison requires two years.
  • The AI prompt looked truncated. It was complete (twelve steps), but a fixed-height scroll box hid everything after Step 2. Now it is shown in full, with fifteen steps and two output formats.
  • The Python examples. Edition 1’s converter cleared each record but kept the emptied elements and every workout attached to the root, so memory still grew; it did not check the input. The daily script ignored sleep, partial days and units. All fixed, tested and published with real output.
  • Image captions. Schematic cards are now labeled “schematic, not data”; mock-ups of phone and watch screens are labeled “not a real Apple screen; every value is invented”, with two factual corrections in captions (walking steadiness classes; which measures come from the iPhone).
  • Labels. “Apple docs” became “Docs” (any manufacturer, FDA or regulator document), and “Guideline” was added for professional-society guidance.

Not verified, or deliberately left out

  • App prices for Athlytic, Bevel and Gentler Streak come from third-party comparisons; AutoSleep’s from the developer’s site; HeartWatch and HealthFit prices were not found.
  • How Apple computes resting heart rate, Readiness, Health Age, time in daylight and Recovery HRV: not published.
  • Apple Watch 6 sensitivity and specificity from BASEL could not be confirmed from the paper and are not quoted.
  • A “30-second single-lead” criterion in the 2023 AF guideline could not be confirmed and is not used.

  • The prompt was checked for completeness against its fifteen steps; it has not been benchmarked against a particular AI assistant.
