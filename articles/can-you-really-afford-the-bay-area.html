Can You Really Afford the Bay Area?

The House, the Children, the Career — and Everything Life Forgot to Put in the Budget

By Paddy Iyer · 5 September 2026 · 15 min read · Seventeen slides

There is a house I want you to picture. Four bedrooms, two baths, a two-car garage, a lawn that needs more water than it deserves. It sits on a flat street in a Santa Clara County suburb. It was built in the late 1950s by a developer who put up four hundred like it in eighteen months.

It is not a special house. That is the point. Between 1960 and 2026 it changed hands perhaps six times, and each time a family stood in the empty living room and did the same arithmetic in their heads.

This essay asks a harder question than the usual one. Not can you buy it — but could an ordinary professional family realistically build, maintain, and protect a middle-class life around it, in each decade from 1960 to now?

Notice the third verb. Almost every affordability analysis stops at the first two: can you close, can you make the payment. That is the easy half. The hard half is the thirty years afterwards, when the roof fails and a parent gets sick and the industry restructures — usually not one at a time.

The answer is not that one generation had it easy. Read the last slide of the deck and you will find every decade carrying its own catastrophe. The question is narrower and more useful: how did the shape of the risk change?

How to read the numbers

Every figure here is one of three things, and the slides mark which. Historical a published figure from a named source. Calculated arithmetic on those figures. Modelled an assumption I made, flagged with an asterisk on the slide.

The house itself is modelled: an illustrative four-bedroom, not the county median. It is deliberately measured against the countywide median household — two different things, compared on purpose. Every major dollar figure appears twice, in the money of its day and in 2026 dollars.

Part I

The House Through Time

Eight buyers, one house, the same twenty per cent down every time — so what moves in the table is the world, not the financing.

Two numbers on that slide do most of the work. 110× is what the price did in nominal terms, and it is the number people quote. 9.75× is what it did after inflation, and it is the number that means something. A dollar in 1960 bought about eleven times what a dollar buys now, so most of the first figure is simply the currency losing weight. What is left over — the real, nearly tenfold gain — is the part that changed who gets to live there.

That column says something people find surprising, and it is worth stating without flinching: this house has never been within reach of the median Santa Clara household. Not in 1980, not in 2000, not now. It is an above-median house, and the median household is not who buys it.

So the story is not a fall from some lost age of affordability. It is about the size of a gap that always existed — and, more importantly, about how much of that gap survives a bad year. In 1980 the gap was covered by a household with two incomes and low fixed costs. In 2026 it is covered by equity compensation, a prior house, or family money. Same gap, different bridge.

The quietest line on the slide is the deposit. In 1980 the twenty per cent down payment was $38,000, about a year and a half of median income — assemblable, with discipline. In 2026 it is $440,000, about two and a half years of a median household’s entire gross income. Nobody assembles that out of salary. Which is a hint about where Bay Area down payments actually come from.

Part II

Don’t Forget Proposition 13

Why two identical houses on the same street can carry tax bills that differ by a factor of five.

In plain English, Proposition 13 does four things. It caps the basic rate at one per cent of assessed value. It replaces market value with acquisition value — you are taxed on what you paid. It caps annual increases in that assessed value at two per cent, whatever the market does. And the clock resets on change of ownership. Historical

The mechanism is simple; the consequence is not. Proposition 13 did something real and defensible: in the mid-1970s Californians, including retirees on fixed incomes, were being reassessed into bills that rose faster than they could pay. People lost houses they had already bought, to a tax on a gain they could not spend. Prop 13 made the property tax predictable, and almost nothing else in your life has that property.

It also created a strong financial reason never to move, and shifted the funding of schools and roads progressively onto whoever arrived most recently. I am not going to settle that here, and I would distrust anyone who could in a paragraph. What matters for a buyer is narrower:

The point for a buyer in 2026

Your property tax is not a market rate. It is a fixed toll on your entry date — and at roughly $24,000 a year on the modelled house, it is one of the largest lines in your budget. It also grows at a capped two per cent, which over thirty years makes it the friendliest line you have. Everything else compounds faster.

Part III

13.74% versus $2.2 Million

“We paid fourteen per cent in the eighties. Young people have it easy.” The first half of that sentence is true, and it deserves better than an eye-roll.

Freddie Mac put the thirty-year fixed at an annual average of 13.74% in 1980 and 16.63% in 1981 — the highest in the survey’s history. Historical That was the going rate for a good borrower with a job and a deposit. People bought at those rates because the alternative was not buying.

So take the claim seriously and test it. A mortgage payment has two inputs: the rate and the principal. The 1980 buyer borrowed $152,000 at 13.74% and paid $1,773 a month. The 2026 buyer borrows $1,760,000 at 6.71% and pays $11,369. The rate halved; the payment multiplied by six.

There is an asymmetry underneath that nobody mentions. The 1980 buyer’s 13.74% was a ceiling — rates fell to 10.13% by 1990 and 8.05% by 2000, and every step was refinanceable. Their worst month was their first. The 2026 buyer’s $1,760,000 is a floor. If rates fall to four per cent the payment drops to about $8,400, which is real relief. But the sum borrowed does not move with it. Principal comes down only the slow way: through the amortisation schedule, or by putting extra money against it.

A rate can be refinanced in an afternoon. The amount borrowed has to be earned.

Part IV

The $200,000 Paradox

How a household can be, simultaneously and truthfully, in the top tenth of American earners and unable to buy an ordinary four-bedroom house.

Two hundred thousand dollars is roughly the top tenth of American household incomes. Said out loud almost anywhere else in the country, it ends the conversation about whether you are doing well.

Here it meets a $2.2 million house. Principal and interest alone run about $136,000 a year. Property tax adds $24,200. Insurance and a real maintenance reserve push the total toward $178,000 — against a take-home of roughly $152,000 on a proper bracket model. Calculated The household is short before it has bought a single bag of groceries.

No underwriter would write that loan; a conventional debt-to-income ceiling would allow about half of it. The paradox is not that the family cannot quite afford the house. It is that they are not close, on an income that would be transformative almost anywhere else.

What $200,000 can do is worth saying, because leaving it out would be dishonest. It rents a three-bedroom comfortably and still saves. It buys a condominium, an older townhouse, or a smaller single-family home further out — a real home. What it cannot do is buy the four-bedroom on the good street and fund the life the house is supposed to be for.

Part V

Everything the Mortgage Calculator Forgot

The calculator assumes nobody gets sick, nobody gets laid off, the roof has signed a thirty-year maintenance agreement, and both children plan to attend college for free.

Two lines on that table deserve their own paragraph.

Childcare. Infant and toddler centre care in Santa Clara County runs past $27,000 per child per year — the highest in California. Historical Two children overlapping is more than the entire food, transport and healthcare budget combined. This is the expense that quietly decides whether the second parent keeps working, and it arrives exactly when careers compound fastest.

College. Published cost of attendance at a UC runs to roughly $42,000 a year; a private non-profit sits in the low-to-mid $60,000s. Historical Escalate that at four per cent and funding two children at a public university takes on the order of $1,900 a month for eighteen years. That is a second mortgage, and it is nearly what the 1990 buyer paid for the first one.

Note what the children’s lines are not: frivolous. Swimming, an instrument, a sport, a martial art — this is the standard package professional families in this county consider ordinary. Pricing it honestly is the whole exercise. You may choose fewer. You should know what you are choosing between.

The row worth staring at is not $120,000. That one is obvious, and a family in it finds out within months. It is $300,000 to $400,000 — the household that looks, from the street, like it is doing everything right. They own the house. The children are in their activities. The cars are fine.

They are simply not saving. The retirement contribution is a token, the college fund a gesture, the emergency fund three weeks deep. They will discover this in year eleven, when something breaks — which brings us to the part of the essay that actually matters.

Part VI

The RSU Economy

Salary rich and equity rich are not the same species, and the difference does not appear in a salary survey.

Return to the question left hanging in Part I. Somebody is buying these houses at these prices. Who?

Partly households at $600,000 and above — there are more of them here than almost anywhere. But mostly the answer is not income at all. It is the down payment, and Bay Area down payments come from somewhere other than salary: equity that vests quarterly, bonuses, an acquisition, appreciation on stock granted years ago, the equity in a house bought in 2013, or family money. None of these are shameful. They are simply the actual mechanism, and pretending the mechanism is salary makes people feel like failures for arithmetic reasons that have nothing to do with them.

But the bridge has a failure mode, and it is correlated. Stock falls when the company struggles. Companies restructure when they struggle. Layoffs happen during restructuring. Refresh grants shrink in exactly the quarters when the share price is depressed. The chance that “my equity is worth less” coincides with “my job is less secure” is not zero — it is close to one.

A conservative framework, not advice

Recurring salary should support recurring obligations. Variable compensation should build resilience and wealth.

This is a risk posture, not a universal rule, and there are people for whom it is too cautious. The point is not that it is always right. It is that the alternative — letting a variable stream service a fixed thirty-year obligation — is a bet you make once and cannot easily unwind.

Part VII

AI and the 30-Year Mortgage

Not an apocalypse. A duration problem.

Careful here, because this is where essays like this usually go wrong in one of two directions. So, plainly: there is no evidence of widespread, economy-wide displacement from AI. The Stanford Digital Economy Lab, working with ADP payroll records covering roughly one-sixth of American workers, finds no such thing. Historical

What they do find is concentrated. Employment among workers aged 22–25 in highly AI-exposed occupations sits about 19% below where it would be had it tracked their less-exposed peers — a gap that widened from 15% over the preceding year. The mechanism is reduced hiring, not increased firing: companies are not dismissing juniors, they are not opening the requisition. And where AI is used to complement rather than automate, employment is flat or rising.

None of that requires AI to eliminate a single job to matter here. A thirty-year mortgage is one of the very few genuinely long-dated commitments an ordinary person signs, and it is completely inflexible. Against it sits an income whose horizon is honestly shorter than it used to be — and, for a meaningful set of people, partly denominated in a share price.

The problem is not that the career is short. It is that the mortgage is long.

Every generation in this essay faced employment risk; the 1990 buyer watched Bay Area defence and aerospace employment contract sharply. What changed is the ratio. Same uncertainty, far less slack to absorb it.

Part VIII

When Life Hits Hard

Budgets assume that life behaves itself. Life has never signed that agreement.

Read that timeline again and notice what it does not say. It does not say all of these will happen to you, or that any particular one will. Most of them are ordinary. Roofs fail. Parents age. Markets fall. Industries reorganise. If you live long enough and love enough people, some version of this list is simply what a life looks like.

The lesson is narrower, and it is the load-bearing sentence of the whole essay:

You cannot predict which crisis will arrive. You can build enough margin that one crisis does not destroy thirty years of work.

That distinction — between predicting the event and surviving the category of event — is the difference between a plan and a forecast. Forecasts are entertainment. Margin is engineering.

Part IX

The Stress Test

Banks stress-test their balance sheets. Households almost never do, which is odd — a household is a leveraged balance sheet with one concentrated income source and no capital buffer requirement.

The finding that surprises people is that income does not buy resilience. Margin does. A household on $400,000 with five months of cash lands in the same colour as one on $200,000. The variable that separates the outcomes is not what comes in; it is the ratio of accessible reserves to essential burn — and a bigger income mostly buys a bigger house, which raises the burn the reserves have to cover.

Here is the most useful calculation in this essay, and it takes four minutes. Add up your genuinely non-optional annual spending. Divide by twelve. Divide your accessible cash by that number. The answer is usually smaller than expected, and it is the only number that matters when the bad eighteen months arrive.

Part X

Who Is Actually Richer?

Two households. Very different balance sheets. Wealth measured as time, not square feet.

Household A is not doing anything wrong, and there are futures in which they win decisively — if the compensation holds for fifteen more years, if the big house appreciates faster than B’s portfolio, if nothing goes wrong. That is a real scenario and not a rare one here.

The point is that A is running a strategy that requires the next fifteen years to cooperate, and B is running one that does not. Those are different risk positions, and conventional net worth reports them as roughly the same thing. So measure something else:

Years of financial freedom: how long can this household meet its essential costs with no employment income at all?

Accessible assets divided by essential annual burn. Nothing more complicated. It is the number that moves when you make the decisions that actually matter — how much house, how much cash, how much of the compensation is recurring. Net worth goes up when the market goes up. Freedom goes up when you choose it.

Part XI

The Generational Table

Everything in one place — not to award a medal for suffering, but to show that the risk did not simply grow. It changed shape.

Read the two rightmost columns down the page. Every row contains a genuine catastrophe. The 1960 household’s entire retirement depended on one company not failing. The 1980 buyer signed at 13.74% and then watched rates reach 16.63% and two recessions arrive back to back. The 1990 buyer bought into a regional economy about to lose much of its defence employment. The 2000 buyer bought at the top of a bubble and lost their options, their job and their paper wealth inside eighteen months.

And today’s buyer has tools nobody before them had: index funds at three basis points, tax-advantaged accounts that did not exist in 1970, portable healthcare, a global labour market, and information that used to sit behind a brokerage. Those are enormous advantages and waving them away would be dishonest.

The structural change

In 1960 the biggest risk to a family was a single catastrophic event — the plant closes, the pension fails, someone gets sick without insurance.

In 2026 it is a duration mismatch. A 13×-income asset, financed over thirty years, serviced by employment with a shorter visible horizon and partly funded by compensation a market reprices daily. Nothing has to fail catastrophically. The pieces simply have to fall out of alignment for eighteen months.

You cannot insure against a duration mismatch. You can only carry enough slack to survive one.

Part XII

How to Plan for an Uncertain Future

You don’t predict the event. You build resilience.

Three of those deserve a sentence more than the slide can give them.

The emergency fund. The conventional three-to-six months was formed in a labour market where a competent professional replaced a job in weeks, and at burn rates a fraction of today’s. For a specialised senior professional in a narrow function, ask instead how long your replacement takes — not in 2021 when everyone was hiring, but in the market you would actually face if your industry were the one that had contracted.

The opportunity fund. The bucket almost nobody has, and the one that changes the most. An emergency fund exists so a bad thing does not destroy you. An opportunity fund exists so a good thing does not pass you by — nine months to learn something genuinely new, a sabbatical, a company started at thirty-eight rather than never, a role that pays thirty per cent less because it is where the next decade is going. Every one of those is a wealth-creating move that is simply unavailable to a household with no slack. That is the real cost of a maximum mortgage: it does not show up as a loss, it shows up as a choice you never got to make.

Employability, not employment. Employment is a state someone else controls. Employability is a stock you own, and it depreciates unmaintained. Note that the last item on that list is financial: money buys the time to retrain, and retraining protects the money. Career resilience and financial resilience are the same system.

In conclusion

Affordability is the wrong question, asked at the wrong scale

I am not going to tell you not to buy a house. Owning the roof over your children is one of the more sensible things a person can do with money. Nor that AI will take your job — the evidence does not support it. And certainly not that previous generations had it easy; read Slide 13 again.

Affordability is not: can I make next month’s payment? An underwriter answers that in four minutes, and it assumes a world where every month resembles this one.

Affordability is: can my family continue to live with dignity when life does not follow the plan?

A house should provide security. It should not require thirty years of uninterrupted good luck to keep. And if that is right, then wealth is measured wrong almost everywhere — not by salary, not by house value you cannot spend without becoming homeless, not by equity that is a claim on someone else’s future, and not quite by net worth either.

Measure it by how much freedom you have when the paycheck unexpectedly stops.

The real American dream may not be owning the biggest house.

It may be owning your time.

Plan for the house. Plan for the children. Plan for retirement.
But above all, leave room for life.
