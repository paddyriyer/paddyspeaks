Prologue · The promise

Privacy engineering turns promises into mechanisms.

The argument of this guide

A privacy policy is a promise written in human language. Privacy engineering translates that promise into architecture, controls, runtime behaviour and evidence. Every movement, copy, join, inference, retention decision and deletion failure is an engineering decision — whether the organization made it consciously or inherited it by default.

On 3 September, Dana signs up for Northstar, an online shop with a payments wallet, a health app called Pulse and an assistant called Nova. The privacy notice she scrolls past says: “We use your data to fulfil your orders and improve your experience. We never sell your data. Delete your account at any time and we’ll delete your data.” A lawyer wrote it in an afternoon. It is a good promise.

Two months later, when Dana asks to be forgotten, the request has to reach 26 systems. Nobody decided that number. It is what the promise became after the data started moving.

Synthetic data Dana and Northstar are fictional. Northstar’s systems, vendors and numbers are the same synthetic dataset that powers the Privacy Command Center, so the two can be read side by side.

  1. 01Promise“We delete your data when you close your account.”
  2. 02ArchitectureWhich systems hold it, and how they connect.
  3. 03DataThe actual fields, copies and identifiers.
  4. 04ControlThe job, gate or check that enforces it.
  5. 05EvidenceLogs and tests that show the control ran.
  6. 06RuntimeWhat production does at 3 a.m. on a Sunday.

A promise fails at whichever link nobody owns. So the discipline has three jobs, and every scene below names which one it is doing.

Hat 01 · before launch

Reviewer

Reads designs before launch and finds the exposure nobody drew on the whiteboard.

Output: findings, required changes, a risk rating, a signed decision
Hat 02 · in the platform

Builder

Ships the controls: consent propagation, retention, deletion, purpose enforcement, identity isolation, access control, privacy-enhancing technologies, deployment gates.

Output: libraries, platforms, deploy gates
Hat 03 · in production

Auditor

Checks what really runs — logs, joins, retention and runtime enforcement — not what the design said or the policy says.

Output: invariants, dashboards, incident fixes

Lawyers write what must be true. Product decides what users should feel. The privacy engineer makes the system actually behave that way. The job is not saying no. It is finding the design where the product works and the exposure shrinks — and writing down the trade-off when it doesn’t.

01 See · Identity

What could a company know about Dana without her name?

In brief

Five ordinary identifiers, each collected for a good reason, become a profile the moment they can meet: Dana’s routine, her late-night pharmacy visits, a likely pregnancy. The decision is not whether to collect an email; it is which identifiers may join, and for what.

Nobody at Northstar needs Dana’s name. Watch what happens when five ordinary identifiers — each collected for a perfectly reasonable reason — are allowed to meet.

The email arrived at sign-up. The device ID came with the app. The advertising ID came with an SDK. The payment token is how checkout works. The location ping powers “stores near you”. None of them is secret. The harm is not in any one of them. It is in the join.

Collected, derived, inferred.

“We only use what customers give us” is almost never true, because most of what a company holds about a person was never given. It is useful to sort it by where it came from, because each kind fails differently: provided data is what the person typed; observed data is what the system recorded while they used it; derived data is computed from those; inferred data is a guess about them. Inference counts as collection — and it can be wrong.

02 See · Linkability

Two harmless tables name a stranger.

In brief

Removing names does not make data anonymous: ZIP code, birth date and sex single out most people, so a “de-identified” table joined to a public list names them. The review question: can we use a weaker, scoped or rotating identifier — or none?

On the left, a clinic dataset with the names removed — the textbook idea of “de-identified”. On the right, a public list of the kind many places publish. Neither table, alone, tells you who has which diagnosis. Now join them on the three columns they share.

How many columns does it take to find one person?

In 2000, Latanya Sweeney estimated that about 87% of the US population was likely to be unique on just three things: five-digit ZIP code, gender and full date of birth. Sweeney, 2000 Historical example Treat the number with care: it was an estimate of uniqueness from 1990 census data, not a count of people re-identified, and a 2006 re-analysis on 2000 census data put it nearer 63%. Golle, 2006 Either way, most people are one lookup away. Try it on a crowd of 1,000 synthetic people — your target is in there somewhere.

So choose the weakest identifier that still works.

Identifiers are not equal. A global, durable one — an email, a phone number, a government ID — links a person across every system that sees it, and hashing an email does not change that: the same email hashes the same way everywhere. Move right along the spectrum whenever the feature allows.

03 See · History

“Delete” is a word, not a mechanism.

In brief

Tools that remember edits keep what you deleted. In a shared document, permission to edit the present is permission to read the past. Share clean copies, give history a retention clock, and treat anything ever exposed as exposed.

Before Dana’s data, Northstar’s own. On Monday a manager pastes a salary sheet into a planning doc: forty names, forty numbers. On Tuesday she deletes the table. The doc looks safe. On Friday a contractor is added as an Editor. At 4 p.m. the contractor opens File › Version history › Monday. Every name, every number.

Google Docs keeps a version history — being able to rewind is the feature. People with edit access can open it; viewers and commenters cannot. Google Docs Editors Help Scrub the week, then change who is looking.

Builder note Six design rules for engineers who build history — and a one-minute check for everyone else
  1. Separate the permissions. Seeing today’s document shouldn’t automatically mean seeing every past version.
  2. Purge across time. A redaction must reach old versions, backups and indexes — with an audit trail.
  3. Share snapshots, not live files. Send a flattened copy to outsiders; keep the working draft inside.
  4. Give history a retention clock. Old versions are data too: expire them like logs.
  5. Scan the past on share. Before a new person gets access, check old versions for secrets and personal data.
  6. Treat exposure as permanent. A leaked key or password is burned. Rotate it — deleting isn’t enough.

Before you hit share

  • Does this person need Editor, or just Viewer?
  • Is the link set to “anyone”?
  • Was anything sensitive ever in this file?
  • Are resolved comments hiding secrets?
  • Would a clean copy be safer?

04 See · Accuracy

Wrong data hurts as surely as leaked data.

In brief

Wrong data harms as surely as leaked data. Dana’s payment is declined because an identity job merged her with her partner’s device. The more a use decides, the more it needs a merge threshold, human review, an explanation, a correction path and an appeal.

On 22 October Dana tries to pay for her vitamins. Declined. Her account is restricted “for your security”. What happened is ordinary: Northstar’s identity job saw the same home IP and overlapping evenings, merged her profile with her partner’s old tablet at 0.71 confidence, and the fraud model read his chargebacks as hers.

Nobody leaked anything. The data was simply wrong about her, and a system acted on it. Choose where the wrong inference is used, then choose the safeguards.

The severity scale is an illustrative ranking of consequences, not a prediction of harm to any real person.

Field notes When automated inaccuracy became a public failure

Real case Robodebt, Australia. From 2015 to 2019 a government scheme averaged annual income across fortnights to raise welfare debts automatically. A Royal Commission found in 2023 that income averaging, as used, neither produced accurate results nor complied with the law. Royal Commission, 2023

Real case Childcare benefits, the Netherlands. The Dutch data protection authority fined the tax administration €2.75 million in 2021 for processing (dual) nationality in a way it found discriminatory while assessing benefit applications. Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens, 2021

Real case Tenant screening, US. In 2023 the FTC and CFPB settled charges that TransUnion’s rental-screening business failed to ensure the accuracy of eviction records in its reports: $15 million. FTC, Oct 2023

Legal uncertainty Accuracy is a principle in the GDPR (Art. 5(1)(d)) with a right to rectification (Art. 16) and, for decisions based solely on automated processing with legal or similarly significant effects, a right to human intervention and to contest (Art. 22). GDPR In the US, consumer reporting agencies must follow reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy. FCRA §1681e(b) Whether a given model counts as either depends on the facts.

TakeawayAn inference is a claim about a person. The more it decides, the more it must be checkable, correctable and contestable.

05 Decide · The review

Eight questions. Every feature.

In brief

A short, fixed review — value, data, identity, flow, access, time, misuse, reduce — asked the same way for every feature, turns opinion into an instrument. Dana’s reorder reminder ships with four conditions. Tier data by what it reveals, not by column type.

Review outcomeShip — with four conditions.

  1. Sensitive product categories are excluded from reminders by default.
  2. The cadence (“every 28 days”) is stored; raw baskets expire after 90 days.

07 Decide · Purpose

Purpose is checked when data is used — not just when it’s collected.

In brief

Data rarely moves far to be misused; the reason moves. A phone number collected for sign-in codes becomes an ad-matching key. Bind purpose to the data and check it at every read: a purpose written only in a privacy notice cannot stop a query.

Consent screens and privacy notices happen once, at collection. Drift happens at every read. The question a reviewer asks is not “is this data sensitive?” but “does this flow match what Dana expected when she shared it?” — Helen Nissenbaum’s idea of contextual integrity. Nissenbaum, 2004

Builder note A purpose check in the query layer, in twenty lines 
# policy: every field carries purposes; every read declares one
fields:
  users.phone_number:  { purposes: [account_security, fraud_prevention], tier: T2 }
  orders.sku:          { purposes: [fulfilment, reminders], tier: T2, reveals: T4_if_category_in(health) }

# enforcement, at read time, for every query
def authorize(query, principal):
    purpose = query.declared_purpose or DENY("no purpose declared")
    for col in query.columns:
        allowed = policy[col].purposes
        if purpose not in allowed and not exception(principal, col, purpose):
            log_deny(principal, col, purpose)        # evidence
            return DENY(f"{col}: {purpose} not in {allowed}")
    if consent_required(purpose) and not consent.current(query.subjects, purpose):
        return FILTER_OUT(revoked_subjects)           # consent is read now, not at midnight
    return ALLOW

# exceptions expire; nobody gets a permanent pass
exception(principal, col, purpose) := ticket.approved and ticket.expires > now()

08 Decide · Threat modeling

Think like the adversary. Then like the person.

In brief

Privacy threat models ask what anyone — including the company, doing its job — can learn or do that the person wouldn’t expect. Walk every technical issue to a human consequence. Children, biometrics, location, health, employees and people at risk change the default design.

Where the stakes change the design.

Six contexts in which a default that is fine elsewhere becomes harmful. These are field notes for engineers, not a legal encyclopedia; the law differs by place and changes. Legal uncertainty

ChildrenAssume a child can be on the other end.

Why it changes: children can’t meaningfully consent to profiling and are more exposed to manipulation. Default move: separate stores, no ad identifiers, no behavioural advertising, the shortest retention, parental controls that don’t become surveillance. Real case: Epic Games paid a $275M COPPA penalty in 2022 over children’s data and default settings. FTC, Dec 2022 Law: COPPA (under 13 in the US; the FTC’s 2025 amendments took effect that June) FTC; GDPR Art. 8 sets 16 for consent to online services, which member states may lower to 13.

BiometricsYou can change a password, not a face.

Why it changes: biometric identifiers are permanent and link across contexts. Default move: match on the device; store templates, not images; never build a shared face index. Real case: the Dutch data protection authority fined Clearview AI €30.5M in 2024 for scraping faces into a recognition database. AP, Sep 2024 Law: Illinois’s BIPA requires written release before collection and lets individuals sue. 740 ILCS 14

Precise locationA trail of coordinates is a diary.

Why it changes: a few points reveal home, work, clinic, place of worship and who sleeps where. Default move: coarse by default; foreground only; compute on the device; hours, not months, of raw history; no ad IDs alongside. Real case: the FTC’s 2024 X-Mode order was its first ban on selling sensitive location data. FTC, Jan 2024

HealthIncluding health you inferred.

Why it changes: disclosure can cost a job, insurance or safety; inferences from purchases and app screens count. Default move: no third-party pixels or SDKs on health surfaces; per-user encryption; explicit consent for each use. Real case: the FTC found that the Flo Health period tracker shared health data with analytics and marketing firms despite its promises. FTC, Jun 2021 Law: in the US, the FTC’s Health Breach Notification Rule was extended to health apps in 2024. FTC, Apr 2024

Employee dataThe power runs one way.

Why it changes: employees can’t easily refuse, and monitoring data invites misuse in decisions about them. Default move: separate HR data from operational telemetry; aggregate productivity signals; access by case, not by rank; no notes about health or family life. Real case: Hamburg’s data protection authority fined H&M €35.3M in 2020 for keeping detailed profiles of service-centre staff’s health, family and beliefs. EDPB, Oct 2020

Use the field kit

A practical review framework, a checklist to download, and a map into the Privacy Command Center.

The north star

Six questions. For every piece of personal data.

  1. Why do we have it?
  2. Where did it come from?
  3. Where does it go?
  4. Who can see, join or infer from it?
  5. When will it disappear?
  6. Can we prove the first five?

If you cannot answer, the uncertainty is the finding.

Review framework

The review on one page

Ask the eight questions in order, listen for the red flag, and leave with conditions that each name a control and its evidence. Tier the data by what it reveals before debating controls.

Checklist

Before launch, on change, and when it breaks

Before launch

  • Every field tiered by what it reveals; T3+ justified in writing.
  • Identifiers scoped to the purpose; cross-purpose joins listed and approved.
  • Every arrow has a purpose, retention, owner and control.
  • Purpose checked at read; consent read at use, not cached from collection.
  • Retention enforced by a job; raw → derived → aggregate chosen.
  • Deletion, access, correction and objection wired to every store, vendor and AI store.
  • Vendors: fields allow-listed, contract terms mapped to checks, transfer basis recorded.
  • AI: training data, prompts, memory, embeddings and tools inventoried; human approval for irreversible actions.
  • Worst-day estimate written; sensitive contexts (children, health, location, biometrics, employees, people at risk) called out.
  • A decision record with owner, reviewer, approver, expiry and evidence.

On every change

  • New T3+ field, consumer, purpose, join, destination, region, subprocessor or model feature? Reopen the review.
  • Contract updated and the gate green — or a time-boxed, logged exception.
  • Deletion and rights registries updated for any new store.

Every week

  • Privacy SLOs reviewed by their owners; unknowns treated as findings.
  • Canary identities checked: opted-out and deleted canaries absent everywhere.
  • Assumption tests run; failures routed to owners.

When it breaks

  • Stop processing at the source; preserve evidence in place.
  • Scope people and systems by lineage; brief counsel and the accountable executive early.
  • Remediate every copy; verify; add the guard; write the record.
