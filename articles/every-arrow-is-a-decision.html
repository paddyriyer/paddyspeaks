Every arrow
is a decision.

Every product you use moves data along arrows: from your finger to a chip, from the chip to a server, from the server to someone else. Security asks who could get at it. Privacy asks whether it should move at all. QA asks how anyone outside would know it behaves as promised. Data governance asks who holds it, how long it lives, and whether it can really be deleted. Here are eight everyday products, as Apple, Google and others document them, through all four.

01 Four lenses

One arrow, four lenses.

Take the most ordinary arrow in modern life: you glance at your phone, and a website lets you in. Four disciplines look at that one arrow and ask four different questions.

  • Security

    Can the wrong actor get at it?

    Could a look-alike site replay the login? Could someone who steals the cloud copy use the key?

  • Privacy

    Should it exist, move, combine, persist or be inferred?

    Does your face leave the phone? Does the site learn anything that links you to other sites?

  • QA

    How would anyone outside know it works as promised?

    Which test proves a look-alike domain is refused? Who outside the company can check the claim?

  • Data governance

    Who holds it, how long does it live, and can it really go?

    Who can restore the key when every device is lost? When you delete it on the phone, does the site forget it too?

Most reviews use one lens. Security teams ask the first question, privacy teams the second, QA teams test what the specification says, and governance teams keep the register. The failures live in the gaps between them: a login that cannot be phished but can be recovered by anyone who answers a text message; a backup that is encrypted but readable by whoever holds the key; a setting that says “don’t save” with no test that shows nothing was saved.

This edition looks at eight products most people use every day, as three companies each document them, through all four lenses at once. Apple and Google appear in every row; the third column is whoever makes the other product people most often compare them with: Microsoft, Mozilla, Proton, Meta, Samsung or Amazon.

How to read the tables

  • Documented The company says so in its own documentation.
  • Setting It depends on a setting; the default is given where the company documents one.
  • Limit A limitation the company itself documents, in its own terms.
  • Test Ours, not theirs: what a QA engineer should check before trusting the claim.

This is not a ranking. There are no scores and no winners, and a longer column is not a better product. Products change month to month: every claim cites the company’s own page as reviewed on 30 September 2026, and the method lists what could not be confirmed.

Eight products, one lens at a time.

Pick a lens. Each row is a product, each column a company, each cell the headline of what that company documents. The product name takes you to the detail.

03 Sign-in

Who proves you are you?

A passkey replaces the password with a key pair: the private half stays in your devices, the website keeps the public half, and your face or PIN only unlocks the key. All three companies now sync passkeys between your devices. The questions move from “can someone steal the password?” to “who can restore the key when every device is lost?”

  1. Face or PIN
  2. Secure chip
  3. Passkey
  4. Cloud sync
  5. Website

The highlighted hop is the arrow to watch. Compared: Apple Passkeys in iCloud Keychain · Google Passkeys in Google Password Manager · Microsoft Passkeys and Windows Hello.

Where they agree
All three keep the biometric on the device, give each site its own public key, and sync the private keys end-to-end encrypted. The phishing arrow is closed the same way everywhere.
Where they differ
What happens when you lose everything differs: Apple offers a recovery contact or key, Google’s documented fallback is a reset that deletes passkeys, and Microsoft splits deletion across the account, the device and the sync provider.
The arrow to watch
The recovery path. It is where an end-to-end encrypted key is restored or lost for good, and where deleting on the phone and deleting at the site fall out of step.

04 Browser

Who else is on the page?

The page you see is one arrow. The scripts, cookies and requests you do not see are dozens more, each going to a party you did not visit. A browser decides by default which of those arrows fire, and each of these three decides differently.

  1. You
  2. Browser
  3. The site
  4. Embedded third parties

The highlighted hop is the arrow to watch. Compared: Apple Safari · Google Chrome · Mozilla Firefox.

Where they agree
All three isolate or partition something by default and run public bug bounties. All three let organisations manage the browser by policy.
Where they differ
Defaults diverge: Safari restricts known fingerprinting scripts in every tab, Firefox partitions cookies per site, and Chrome keeps third-party cookies on in normal browsing after retiring its replacement plan.
The arrow to watch
Third-party requests in a normal tab. That is where the default decides who else learns what you read.

05 Mail

Does the email read you back?

An email can carry a one-pixel image. When your app fetches it, the sender learns that you opened the message, when, and from which IP address. Mail providers now step between the pixel and the reader, but none end-to-end encrypts the subject line or the envelope of ordinary email.

  1. Sender
  2. Mail server
  3. Your inbox
  4. Remote image
  5. Sender again

The highlighted hop is the arrow to watch. Compared: Apple Mail and iCloud Mail · Google Gmail · Proton Proton Mail.

Where they agree
All three put something between the sender’s remote content and the reader — a relay, a proxy or a blocker. None end-to-end encrypts the subject line or the envelope of ordinary email.
Where they differ
Apple and Google hold the keys to stored mail (Workspace client-side encryption aside); Proton cannot read stored mail. Proton’s apps are open source and audited; Apple’s and Google’s are closed and checked through bounties.
The arrow to watch
The pixel fetch. When it happens and whose IP address it comes from can be tested directly, and it is the whole of the privacy claim.

06 Messages

Who else can read the conversation?

All three encrypt the conversation end to end. The differences live at the edges: whether the protection can switch off, what the servers still see, and who holds the key to the backup.

  1. You
  2. Your phone
  3. Relay server
  4. Their phone
  5. Backup

The highlighted hop is the arrow to watch. Compared: Apple iMessage · Google Google Messages (RCS) · Meta WhatsApp.

Where they agree
All three end-to-end encrypt the content of native conversations, and all three document that servers still see routing metadata. Each offers some way to check keys.
Where they differ
WhatsApp’s encryption cannot be switched off; Google Messages depends on RCS, the other person’s phone and the carrier. On backups, Apple’s and WhatsApp’s strongest options are opt-in, while Android ties backup encryption to the screen lock.
The arrow to watch
The backup. In transit the message is encrypted by default everywhere; the backup copy is where defaults decide who holds the key.

07 Wallet & ID

What does the tap reveal?

Tapping to pay sends a stand-in for your card number. Showing a digital ID can prove you are over 21 without your name or birth date. The token protects you from the merchant; what the wallet maker itself keeps is a separate question.

  1. You
  2. Wallet
  3. Terminal or verifier
  4. Payment network
  5. Wallet maker

The highlighted hop is the arrow to watch. Compared: Apple Apple Wallet and Apple Pay · Google Google Wallet · Samsung Samsung Wallet.

Where they agree
All three replace the card number with a device-bound token, gate payment and ID behind a biometric or PIN, and keep ID credentials on the device.
Where they differ
Apple states it cannot see where an ID is shown and does not tie card transactions to you; Google and Samsung document collecting transaction details. Google alone has published its age-proof cryptography.
The arrow to watch
Transaction data flowing from the wallet to the wallet maker. The token hides the card from the merchant; it says nothing about what the platform keeps.

08 Cloud backup

Who holds the key to your photos?

Every backup is encrypted. The question that separates them is who holds the key: the provider, so it can recover your account and scan the content, or only your devices, so nobody else can — including the provider, and including anyone who can compel it.

  1. Phone
  2. Upload
  3. Provider cloud
  4. Recovery
  5. Anyone who can compel

The highlighted hop is the arrow to watch. Compared: Apple iCloud · Google Android backup, Google Photos and Drive · Microsoft OneDrive.

Where they agree
All three encrypt in transit and at rest under provider-held keys by default, which lets the provider run recovery, search and abuse scanning. All three offer export and a 30-day bin.
Where they differ
Only Apple offers consumers an end-to-end mode for photos and files, opt-in and not for new UK users. Google end-to-end encrypts device backups by default but not Photos or Drive. Microsoft’s vault adds a gate, not provider-blind encryption.
The arrow to watch
Key custody. Whoever holds the key — provider, user or recovery contact — decides who can be compelled to open it.

09 AI assistant

Where does the question go?

An assistant is useful because it reads across your mail, messages, photos and calendar. Where that reading happens — on the phone, in a sealed server, or in an ordinary cloud service — decides who else could see the request and how long it lives.

  1. Your request
  2. On-device model
  3. Private cloud
  4. Retention
  5. Training

The highlighted hop is the arrow to watch. Compared: Apple Apple Intelligence · Google Gemini · Microsoft Copilot and Recall.

Where they agree
All three split work between an on-device tier and a cloud tier, all three say work content is not used to train general models, and all three run bounties that cover their AI.
Where they differ
Apple’s cloud tier is documented as stateless and checkable through a public log. Google keeps consumer chats by default, with human review unless switched off. Microsoft’s most intimate data, Recall, never leaves the PC and is opt-in.
The arrow to watch
The arrow from device to cloud tier, and what is retained after the answer. “Verifiable” and “we promise” are different kinds of control.

10 Home & voice

Who hears the kitchen?

A voice assistant listens for its wake word in a room where anyone can be speaking. What leaves the room, whether the audio is kept, and whether a person may listen to it are settings — and one of these companies removed a local-processing option in 2025.

  1. A voice in the room
  2. Speaker
  3. Cloud
  4. Saved recording
  5. Human review

The highlighted hop is the arrow to watch. Compared: Apple Siri and Apple Home · Google Google Assistant, Gemini for Home and Nest · Amazon Alexa, Echo and Ring.

Where they agree
All three send at least some voice requests to the cloud and offer settings that limit whether recordings are kept or heard by people. All three publish security material for home devices.
Where they differ
Apple keeps no Siri audio unless you opt in, and Google saves none by default. Amazon removed its local-processing option, so audio reaches the cloud and “don’t save” deletes it afterwards.
The arrow to watch
The arrow from the speaker to the cloud, now that generative assistants need it — and whether deletion covers the transcripts made from the audio.

11 The arrow into the future

What must still be possible years from now?

Every product above was read for where data goes, who can read it, how long it lives, whether it can be deleted, who holds the key and whether the promise can be checked. One arrow is missing from that list, because it points forward in time. A system also decides what must still be possible with the data years from now:

  • recover it
  • search it
  • match it
  • produce it
  • prove it existed
  • prove it was deleted
  • preserve it under a legal hold
  • make it unrecoverable on purpose

Those obligations pull in different directions, and each lens owns one of them.

  • Privacy

    Do not keep personal data longer than the purpose needs.

  • Security

    Protect whatever is kept, for as long as it is kept.

  • Data governance

    Know the retention periods, owners, keys, copies and preservation duties.

  • QA

    Prove that restore, deletion, legal hold and key management actually work.

Four ways to protect a value, four different futures

Take one value, an email address such as dana@example.com. There are four common ways to keep it out of plain sight. They look alike on a diagram and behave very differently ten years later.

Encryption

Kept: the value, scrambled. The original comes back for anyone who still holds the key.

  • Who holds the key, and who could obtain it?
  • Is the key kept as long as the data, and what happens when it is rotated?
  • What if the key is destroyed on purpose?

Deleting the key can be equivalent to deleting the data. But if recovery is later required, a destroyed key leaves preserved bytes that nobody can read.

Tokenization

Kept: a stand-in, such as tok_8f3a…, in every system; the real value lives in a separate vault. The original comes back through the vault.

  • Who controls the mapping?
  • Does the vault follow the same retention rule as the tokens?
  • Does a legal hold preserve the token only, or the mapping too? Can the token travel between systems without linking them?

The token may look harmless, but the vault decides whether identity can come back.

Hashing

Kept: a fixed fingerprint of the value. A cryptographic hash is designed to be one-way, and there is no key. If the original is discarded, it cannot be recovered from the hash, by anyone, under any order.

  • If someone may legitimately need the original later, was an irreversible hash the right choice?
  • How small is the set of possible inputs?

One-way does not necessarily mean unlinkable. When the possible inputs are guessable (a known list of email addresses, phone numbers, ZIP codes), anyone can hash each candidate and look for a match.

Keyed pseudonyms

Kept: a repeatable pseudonym made with a secret key (a keyed hash, such as HMAC), so analytics can count the same person without storing the address. It cannot normally be reversed, but a known candidate can be pseudonymised again and matched against old records, as long as the key, the normalisation rules (lower-case? trimmed?) and the algorithm version survive.

  • Where is each historical key kept, and for how long?
  • Are the normalisation rules and versions recorded alongside the data?

Key lifecycle can quietly become data lifecycle. Destroy the old key and the old records still exist, but can never be matched again.

When “delete” meets “preserve”

Most data follows a simple lifecycle:

  1. Collect
  2. Use
  3. Retain
  4. Delete

An authorised legal or regulatory preservation requirement can bend it. A valid hold suspends normal deletion for the relevant records, for as long as the hold lasts:

  1. Collect
  2. Use
  3. Legal hold
  4. Preserve
  5. Release hold
  6. Resume retention and deletion

The engineering question is not only “did we keep the file?” It is “did we keep enough context to make the file usable?” A record may depend on things stored somewhere else:

  • the encrypted record
  • its decryption key
  • the token mapping
  • the historical schema
  • normalisation logic
  • algorithm and version
  • timestamps
  • lineage
  • metadata
  • the audit trail

Preserved bytes are not necessarily preserved evidenceA five-year-old archive that nobody can decrypt, interpret or match may technically exist while being operationally useless.

This describes engineering requirements, not legal advice: whether a hold applies, to what and for how long is for counsel to decide.

The future, through the four lenses

  • Security

    • Who can recover the protected data?
    • Where are the keys, and who can use them?

  • Privacy

    • Should the original remain recoverable at all?
    • Would an irreversible transformation reduce the risk?

  • QA

    • Can we restore an old archive?
    • Can we reproduce a historical pseudonym?
    • Does a legal hold really stop deletion, and does deletion resume when it is released?
    • Do destroyed keys really make the data inaccessible?

  • Data governance

    • What is the retention rule, and what changes under a hold?
    • Which copies, mappings, keys and derivatives does the hold include?

The products above show the same tension: an end-to-end protected backup can be lost for good along with its recovery key, and every 30-day bin is a copy with a clock.

12 Everyday arrows

One morning, many arrows.

The products above were compared one at a time. A person meets most of them before ten in the morning, one ordinary action after another. Follow those actions instead of the products, and every one of them sets off the same chain:

  1. Event
  2. Identifier
  3. System
  4. Derived data
  5. Inference
  • 8:02 unlock the phone
  • 8:04 sign in
  • 8:06 check email
  • 8:15 search “coffee near me”
  • 8:20 directions to a café
  • 8:32 buy a coffee
  • 9:10 share a file
  • 9:30 ask the assistant “When is my flight?”

Two of them, followed to the end:

Directions to a café

Identifiers: the account ID and the device ID. Systems: maps and the account service. Derived: the route, a location history, then a list of repeated places. Inferred: a possible commute, a likely home and workplace.

Only the route was needed to give directions. The history, the repeated places and the commute are further arrows, and each one is a decision.

“When is my flight?”

Identifier: the account. Systems: the assistant, mail and calendar. Derived: a reservation read from a confirmation email, and the schedule. Inferred: the travel itinerary.

Joined with the likely home from the maps trace, the itinerary also says when the home will be empty. Nobody asked that question.

Harmless alone, revealing together

No single event in that morning is sensitive. Joined through one account ID, a search, a route and a purchase say “buys coffee near work on weekdays”. Change the details (a search for strollers, a visit to a baby-goods shop, a purchase of prenatal vitamins) and the same three arrows say “possibly expecting a child”: a health inference that nobody typed in.

The combination is the inferenceOrdinary signals become revealing when they are joined. The join is the decision to review, not only the store.

Why is this connected?

Every line between two of those boxes should be able to answer six plain questions:

  • what it is for
  • required, useful or optional?
  • which identifier it uses
  • how long it is kept
  • who can use it
  • can the contexts be separated again?

A reviewer then has four answers, not one:

  • Keep the connection, because the purpose needs it.
  • Scope it: give the receiving service its own identifier, so two services can no longer recognise the same person.
  • Shorten retention, so the history a pattern needs never builds up.
  • Separate the contexts, so the join never happens.

Each answer removes a particular inference and leaves the rest. Scoping the maps service’s identifier does not stop it seeing a commute; it stops the commute being joined with the itinerary. Shortening the location history to a day stops the commute from forming at all.

One feature, every source

To answer “When is my flight?”, an assistant needs the question and the reservation. The calendar helps, and so does the time zone. Earlier conversations, files and location history were merely within reach. Each source is reasonable on its own. The new question is what happens when one feature is allowed to see all of them at once. Give each answer the sources it needs, ask before the ones that only help, and leave the rest out: the answer still works.

Many devices, many companies

Few people live inside one ecosystem. A phone from one company, a laptop from a second and a tablet from a third each sign in to their own company’s account, and no single account sees every device. That does not stop the linking. It moves it to whatever runs on every device:

  • one browser account
  • one email address to sign up with
  • the same apps on every device
  • a phone-to-computer link
  • one home Wi-Fi address

Mixing platforms moves the join; it does not remove itThe question is no longer which company sees every device, but which of the things you use everywhere can recognise you everywhere.

Watches, cars and televisions join the same way: through the phone they pair with, an account, or the home network.

Privacy problems arrive as changes

Many of these arrows were not in the first design. They arrive later, one change at a time, and each deserves a review of its own:

  • a new service starts receiving location
  • a retention period grows from 30 days to a year
  • a new identifier is added
  • an AI feature begins reading email
  • an outside processor is added
  • a purpose changes

Everyday arrows, through the four lenses

  • Security

    • Which identifiers travel with each event, and who could steal or replay them?
    • Are the recovery links (phone number, recovery email, devices, IP address) kept inside security?

  • Privacy

    • What can be inferred when this event is joined with others?
    • Which joins does the purpose actually need?

  • QA

    • When a connection is scoped, shortened or separated, does the inference it enabled really stop?
    • Does it stop on every device and platform the person uses?

  • Data governance

    • Which connections changed since the last review, and did each change go through one?
    • Who receives each derived record, and for how long?

This section describes patterns, not any company’s products, so it carries no citations. Try it on synthetic data in the Privacy Command Center: follow “Bought coffee”, ask the assistant about the flight, or open devices across platforms and change the phone, laptop and tablet.

13 What the comparison teaches

Nine patterns across eight products.

Read the tables side by side and the same few arrows keep deciding the outcome, whichever company built the product.

  1. The recovery path is the real boundary.

    Passkeys, messages and device backups are end-to-end encrypted by all three companies that offer them. What differs is who can restore the key when every device is gone: a recovery contact, a PIN, the screen lock, or the provider. Whoever can restore it is inside the boundary.

    Seen in sign-in, messages and cloud backup.

  2. Encryption answers who can read it, not why it exists.

    Every mail provider here encrypts stored mail; none end-to-end encrypts the subject line of ordinary email. WhatsApp encrypts every message and still documents collecting who you talk to and when. Encryption is a security answer to a privacy question it does not ask.

    Seen in mail and messages.

  3. The default is the product.

    Safari limits known fingerprinting scripts in every tab; Chrome keeps third-party cookies on. WhatsApp’s encryption cannot be switched off; Apple’s strongest backup protection must be switched on. Google keeps Gemini activity unless you turn it off. Most people never change a default, so the default is what the product does.

    Seen in browser, messages, cloud backup and AI assistant.

  4. Metadata is data.

    A tracking pixel reveals when and where you read. Message servers keep lookup logs and connection details. Wallets keep transaction details, or document that they do not. The content can be sealed while the pattern around it stays readable.

    Seen in mail, messages and wallet.

  5. A claim you can verify is a control. One you cannot is a promise.

    Apple publishes measurements of the software its private cloud runs; Proton publishes its apps’ source and audits; Google published its age-proof cryptography; WhatsApp publishes key transparency. Everywhere else the evidence is a bug bounty and a policy page. QA’s job is to turn each promise into a test someone can run.

    Seen in AI assistant, mail, wallet and messages.

  6. Deletion has to follow every copy.

    A passkey deleted on the phone can live on at the site. A Gemini chat reviewed by a person is kept for up to three years after you delete your activity. A voice recording can be deleted while its transcript survives. Every 30-day bin is a copy with a clock. Governance is counting the copies.

    Seen in sign-in, AI assistant, home & voice and cloud backup.

  7. A setting can be withdrawn.

    Amazon removed a local-processing option from some Echo devices in 2025. Apple stopped offering Advanced Data Protection to new users in the United Kingdom. Google retired most of the technology it had built to replace third-party cookies. A control that the vendor or a government can remove needs a date on it, and a test that notices when it goes.

    Seen in home & voice, cloud backup and browser.

  8. Recoverability is a design decision.

    Encryption, tokenization, hashing, keyed pseudonyms and deletion are not interchangeable. Some keep the original recoverable for whoever holds a key or a vault. Some keep only the ability to match. Some destroy recoverability on purpose. Choosing between them is not a security detail to settle later: it fixes, years in advance, what the organisation can recover, search, produce or prove, including under a legal hold. So the future obligations have to be decided before the transformation is chosen, not discovered after the key is gone. The privacy question is not simply whether data is protected today. It is what the system must still be able, or unable, to know tomorrow.

    Seen in the arrow into the future, cloud backup and sign-in.

  9. The link moves; it does not disappear.

    A scoped identifier, a separate account or a device from another company each removes one join. The person is then joined again by whatever is shared: an email address, a browser account, an app, a home network, an assistant allowed to read everything. Review the joins as carefully as the stores: a combination of ordinary records is new information that nobody typed in.

    Seen in browser, AI assistant and everyday arrows.

14 The four-lens review

Twenty-eight questions for any product.

Use them on a product you are building, buying or trusting with your family’s data. A question nobody can answer is a finding.

Security

  • What stops a look-alike site, a stolen session or a replayed request?
  • Where does the key live, and what hardware protects it?
  • Who can restore access when every device is lost, and how are guesses limited?
  • What is sent before the person acts: before the wake word, before the tap, before opening?
  • What happens on a compromised or second-hand device?
  • If recovery is required, who controls the key or the mapping?
  • Are the links kept for security (phone number, recovery email, devices, IP address) used for nothing else?

Privacy

  • What leaves the device, and could it have been computed on the device?
  • Which identifier links this activity to other activity, and is it scoped?
  • What metadata remains after the content is encrypted?
  • Is it used for training, ads or personalisation, and what is the default?
  • Could the purpose be met by proving an attribute instead of revealing the data?
  • Should the original remain recoverable, or would a pseudonymous or irreversible representation meet the purpose?
  • What can be inferred when this activity is joined with other activity, on this device and on the person’s other devices?

QA

  • What would an outsider need to verify the claim: source, logs, an audit, a research environment?
  • Is the default state tested, not only the configured one?
  • Is every fallback tested: to SMS, to an unencrypted channel, to a password?
  • Is deletion tested end to end, including derived data and backups?
  • Is the test re-run after every release and every policy change?
  • Can we restore and interpret an old protected record using the documented recovery path?
  • When a connection is scoped, shortened or separated, does the inference it enabled really stop?

Data governance

  • Who holds the key, and who could be compelled to use it?
  • How long does each copy live: active, backup, reviewed, derived?
  • Can the person export it, and in what format?
  • Does deletion in one place reach every other place?
  • Which controls can the vendor or a government withdraw, and who would be told?
  • Does a preservation hold include the keys, mappings, schemas, metadata and derived copies needed to make the record meaningful?
  • Which connections changed since the last review (a new receiver, identifier, processor, purpose or retention period), and did each go through review?

15 How this was made

What was checked, and what was not.

Every claim in the tables comes from the company’s own documentation: support pages, security guides, legal notices, newsroom and engineering posts. Four exceptions are labelled in the sources: an IETF presentation, a published third-party audit, a regulator’s press release and one news report of a dated change. Sources were reviewed on 30 September 2026. Several vendor pages could be confirmed only through their search listings, so the wording here is a paraphrase, not a quotation; check the current page before you rely on one.

The companies are compared on what they document, not on how well they meet it. A documented limitation is not a failure, and a missing row is not a strength. The Test entries are recommendations, not findings: none of them has been run for this essay.

What could not be confirmed

  • The default of Apple’s Protect Mail Activity setting, and whether Safari’s broader tracking-protection setting is on outside Private Browsing.
  • Whether Gmail’s image proxy hides the reader’s IP address, in Google’s own words.
  • Whether Firefox’s newest fingerprinting defences are now on in Standard mode.
  • The default of Google Wallet’s personalisation setting.
  • Whether Nest camera video is end-to-end encrypted, and whether Private AI Compute has a public transparency log.
  • The current default of Amazon’s setting for human review of voice recordings.

Corrections are welcome, with a link to the company’s page: contact.

Every product is a bundle of arrows. Security, privacy, QA and data governance are four ways of asking whether each one was decided on purpose.

A person does not live inside one application. They sign in, browse, write, pay, back up, ask and speak, through products built by different companies with different defaults, on devices from different companies. Privacy engineering must follow them across all of those boundaries.
