The Invisible Map · A field guide in twelve parts

From Tap to Trace

NFC, AirTags, cloud identity, and the quantum future of human privacy

How tiny electronic identifiers, billions of smartphones, cryptographic keys and cloud accounts are reshaping the boundaries between convenience, surveillance and personal freedom.

Paddy Iyer October 22, 2026 Research cutoff: October 21, 2026 About 90 minutes, with appendices

Executive abstract

A coin-sized tracker with no GPS, no cellular radio and no Wi-Fi can be located across a continent because it borrows other people's phones. The largest networks are built so that the company running them does not learn where a tag is or whose it is. That is a real engineering achievement, and it solves a narrower problem than most people assume. Encryption decides who can read a location. It does not decide whether the person reading it should be allowed to follow someone, whether the reported location is true, or whether the person carrying the tag knows.

This guide separates three problems that are constantly blurred: identification (recognising an object), localization (knowing where it was seen) and authorization (deciding who may see that). It follows a location report from the tag's radio to the owner's screen, marks every trust boundary on the way, compares Apple, Google, Samsung and Tile on what each has actually documented, explains why anti-stalking alerts help but are never foolproof, sorts the quantum threat into what is broken in theory, what is broken in practice (nothing here, today) and what needs migrating, and ends with what a CISO can and cannot govern about an endpoint nobody in the company owns.

Part IWhen a keychain became a network participant

Three stories, one architecture.

Scenario A

The forgotten suitcase

A traveller lands, waits at the carousel, and the bag never comes. Inside it, wedged between two shirts, is a tracker the size of a large coin. It has no GPS receiver, no SIM card and no Wi-Fi. It cannot tell anyone where it is, because it does not know.

What it can do is whisper. Every so often it sends a short Bluetooth Low Energy advertisement into the baggage hall. A baggage handler's phone, which the handler has never paired with anything, hears it. That phone knows where it is. It wraps its own position in a sealed envelope that only the tag's owner can open, and sends the envelope to its maker's servers. Twenty minutes later, on a phone at the arrivals hall, a dot appears on a map: the bag is in a holding area at the far end of the terminal. Under a newer feature the traveller can even send the airline a temporary link to that dot.

Nobody at the airport chose to help. The handler will never know. The company running the network, if it built the system as it documents, does not know whose bag it was either.

Scenario B

The invisible passenger

A woman walks to her car after work, and her phone shows a notification she has never seen before: an unknown tracker appears to be moving with her. She checks her bag, her coat, the car. Nothing. She drives home. The alert comes back the next morning.

The tracker in her car belongs to someone else. To that person it is an object on a map. To her it is a proxy for her body: wherever the tag goes, she went. Her home, her gym, her doctor, her sister's house. The same encryption that protected the traveller's suitcase from the cloud provider protects this location stream too, all the way to the person who planted the tag.

Her phone noticed because two of the largest phone platforms agreed on a common way to recognise trackers separated from their owners. Had she been carrying an older phone, a phone with Bluetooth off, or a tracker that does not follow that specification, the alert might have come later, or not at all.

Scenario C

The compromised digital household

A couple separate. One of them moves out, changes their account password, and believes the matter is closed. Weeks later the other still seems to know where they have been.

The password was never the whole story. The location stream can still be reachable through a trusted device that was never removed from the account; a sharing invitation accepted years ago; a family arrangement that shares locations by default; or a tag that was simply never theirs alone. Changing a password does not by itself sign out every device already trusted, and it does not withdraw sharing that was granted on purpose. Conversely, knowing a password usually does not hand an attacker the end-to-end encrypted keys: on the major platforms those are released only to devices that pass additional checks, such as a trusted-device approval or the passcode of a device already in the account.

Each of those paths is a separate lock. Which lock was open matters, because each is closed differently, and closing one can be visible to the person who used it.

These are the same system seen from three seats. In the first, the system works exactly as intended and the beneficiary is the owner. In the second, it works exactly as intended and the beneficiary is the wrong person. In the third, the cryptography may be flawless and the failure is in who holds the right to read.

The rest of this guide separates three ideas that ordinary conversation melts together:

IdentificationRecognising an object, credential or device. An access badge identifies; a serial number identifies; a Bluetooth advertisement may or may not.
LocalizationKnowing where an object is, or was observed. A tag does not localize itself; a passing phone localizes itself and reports that it was near the tag.
AuthorizationDeciding who may see the result. This is an account, key and sharing problem, not a radio problem, and it is where most real harm is decided.

Which brings the introduction to the question the rest of the guide tries to answer:

What happens when physical ownership, cryptographic ownership and cloud-account ownership are no longer the same thing?

Part IIThe radio technologies people constantly confuse

How can a tiny tracker with no GPS, no cellular radio and no Wi-Fi be located across a country?

The short answer: it is not located by its own radio. It is noticed by other people's radios, each of which already knows where it is. To see why, we need to pull apart six technologies that marketing tends to describe in one breath: RFID, NFC, Bluetooth Low Energy, Ultra Wideband, satellite positioning, and Wi-Fi or cellular positioning. Each does a different job, and only some of them can tell anyone where anything is.

The clearest way to keep them apart is to ask which of four questions each one can answer:

  • What is this? Identification. A badge, a sticker, a serial number.
  • Is it near me? Proximity. A yes, or a rough "warmer, colder".
  • How far, and which way? Ranging and direction. Centimetres to metres, with an arrow.
  • Where on Earth is it? Geographic position. Coordinates on a map.

RFID: the object that answers when asked

Everyday version. The tag sewn into a shirt in a shop, the chip in a pet, the badge that opens the office car park. You hold it near a reader; the reader learns an identifier.

Mechanism. Radio-frequency identification is a family, not one technology. Passive tags have no battery: a reader's field powers them, and they answer by modulating that field (near-field coupling at low and high frequency) or by reflecting part of it back (backscatter at UHF). Low-frequency tags (around 125–134 kHz) are common in animal ID and older access cards; high-frequency tags (13.56 MHz) include most contactless cards and NFC; UHF tags (roughly 860–960 MHz, the "RAIN"/EPC Gen2 family) are used for inventory and can be read at metres under good conditions. Active RFID tags carry a battery and transmit on their own, typically for yard and asset tracking with fixed readers.

Architectural implication. RFID answers "what is this?" for whoever has a reader close enough. Its location value comes entirely from where the reader is: an inventory system knows a pallet passed dock door 4 because the reader is bolted to dock door 4. There is no global crowdsourced RFID network in consumers' pockets.

Attack surface. Cloning of weak legacy credentials (many older 125 kHz access cards broadcast a fixed number with no cryptography), skimming within read range, and relay (Part VI). CISO question: do we still issue access credentials that a cheap reader can copy?

NFC: RFID's careful, close-range cousin

Everyday version. Paying with a phone, tapping a transit card, tapping a poster to open a web page.

Mechanism. Near Field Communication operates at 13.56 MHz using inductive coupling: two loop antennas act like the loosely coupled windings of a transformer. The field falls off very steeply with distance, so practical interactions happen at a few centimetres, by design. NFC devices take three roles: reader/writer (a phone reading a tag or a card), card emulation (a phone or watch behaving like a contactless card, with credentials held in a secure element or, for host card emulation, protected by the operating system), and the largely historical peer-to-peer mode. Passive NFC tags store small records in the NFC Forum's NDEF format, such as a URL, and come in several standard tag types.[26]

Architectural implication. NFC answers "what is this?" and, in a payment or access system, "is this credential genuine?". It does not answer "where on Earth is it?" and it cannot be heard across a street. NFC plays no part in the continuous crowdsourced tracking of an AirTag. Its role there is a close-range courtesy: a person who finds an AirTag can tap it with any NFC-capable phone to open a page about it, including, in Lost Mode, the owner's message.[6]

Attack surface. Malicious tags that send a phone to a phishing page; relay attacks that stretch a few centimetres into many metres; weak tag memory locks that allow rewriting. CISO question: does any of our authorisation rest on the assumption that "only someone standing here could have tapped this"?

Bluetooth Low Energy: the whisper

Everyday version. Your earbuds reconnecting, a fitness band syncing, and, the subject of this guide, a tracker announcing itself.

Mechanism. BLE works in the 2.4 GHz band. A device can advertise: send short packets on three primary advertising channels at an interval its designer chooses. Any listener in range that is scanning can receive those packets without pairing. An advertisement carries an address and a small payload (classically up to 31 bytes in legacy advertising, more with extended advertising). Range depends on transmit power, antennas, bodies and walls: from a metre or two inside a bag to tens of metres in open air, with long-range modes beyond that. Signal strength (RSSI) gives a crude, noisy hint of distance, nothing more.

Architectural implication. BLE answers "is it near me?" for any listener, and that is precisely its privacy problem. A fixed address or a fixed payload is a beacon anyone can follow. The Bluetooth specification therefore supports private addresses that change over time, and finding networks rotate their payload too (Part VII).

Attack surface. Passive tracking of identifiers that do not rotate, or rotate predictably; correlation of rotating identifiers by timing, signal and co-travel; replay and relay of advertisements; implementation bugs in Bluetooth stacks. CISO question: what Bluetooth identifiers do our own devices, badges and vehicles broadcast, and can an outsider follow them?

Ultra Wideband: the tape measure

Everyday version. The arrow on your phone that says "6 m, ahead and to the left" when you search for a tag in a room.

Mechanism. UWB spreads very short pulses over a wide bandwidth (500 MHz or more, in bands around 6.5 and 8 GHz). Short pulses make arrival times precise, so two devices can measure the time of flight of a signal and convert it to distance. Light travels about 30 cm per nanosecond, so timing to a fraction of a nanosecond gives distances to within centimetres to tens of centimetres in good conditions. Multiple antennas also give an angle of arrival. IEEE 802.15.4z added security features, including a scrambled timestamp sequence, designed to make distance measurements harder to manipulate.

d = c · t_ToF t_ToF = (t_round − t_reply) / 2

d distance · c speed of light, about 0.3 m per nanosecond · t_round time from sending a poll to receiving the response, measured by the initiator · t_reply the responder's processing delay, which it reports · t_ToF one-way time of flight. An error of 1 ns in the timing is about 30 cm of error in distance.

Architectural implication. UWB answers "how far, and which way?" and only for two cooperating, compatible devices within short range, typically tens of metres at most. It is the last few metres of a search, not the search. Apple calls this Precision Finding.

Attack surface. Distance manipulation and relay against UWB-based access (for example, car keys), which is why secure ranging matters; and, for privacy, the fact that a UWB-capable tag can be located precisely by whoever is authorised to range to it. CISO question: where do we rely on distance, rather than cryptography alone, to authorise a door, car or device?

GNSS: the sky's clock

Everyday version. "GPS" in your phone, car and watch.

Mechanism. Global navigation satellite systems (GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, BeiDou) broadcast precisely timed signals. A receiver measuring signals from at least four satellites solves for its three position coordinates and its own clock error. The satellites know nothing about the receiver; positioning is passive and happens entirely on the device. But a receiver needs sky visibility, an antenna, and a meaningful power budget for acquisition, which is why a coin-cell tracker meant to last a year does not carry one.

Architectural implication. GNSS answers "where on Earth am I?" for the device holding the receiver. A basic AirTag contains no GNSS receiver and does not determine its own location.[3] When an AirTag appears on a map, the coordinates are the finder phone's estimate of its own position, at the moment it heard the tag. Dedicated GPS trackers with cellular modems do exist; they are a different product class with a SIM, a subscription and a battery that needs regular charging.

Attack surface. Jamming and spoofing of satellite signals, which can mislead a finder phone's own position and therefore any report it makes. CISO question: do we treat a reported location as evidence, or as a claim to be corroborated?

Wi-Fi and cellular positioning: the neighbourhood's fingerprints

Everyday version. Your phone finding itself indoors, where satellites cannot be seen.

Mechanism. A phone hears Wi-Fi access points and cell towers, and compares what it hears against a database of where those transmitters are. It can estimate distance from signal strength or timing and combine several estimates (trilateration or multilateration). The databases are built, unsurprisingly, by crowdsourcing from phones.

Architectural implication. This is also crowdsourced localization, but of a different shape: the phone locates itself using fixed infrastructure. Tag finding is one more step out: a phone that has located itself then vouches for being near a tag it cannot identify.

Secure elements and trusted execution environments: the vault, not the guard

A secure element is a separate tamper-resistant chip that holds keys and runs small trusted applets: the payment credential in a phone wallet, for example. A trusted execution environment (TEE) is an isolated region of the main processor; Apple's Secure Enclave and Android's hardware-backed keystores (with StrongBox on some devices) are the familiar examples.

They guarantee, within their threat model, that a key can be used without being exported, that some operations require a user's presence or passcode, and that a stolen device does not trivially surrender its secrets. They do not guarantee that the app asking for a signature is honest, that the user understood what they approved, that a location fed into them is true, or that the account the device belongs to is controlled by the right person. A vault keeps the key safe. It does not decide who should have been given the key.

Part IIIFrom one device to one account: who actually owns the tracker?

The person holding the tag, the account it is registered to and the keys that read its reports can be three different parties.

Ask "whose AirTag is this?" and you are really asking ten questions at once. Who holds it? Who set it up? Which account is it registered to? Who can sign in to that account? Which devices hold the keys that turn a sealed report into a dot on a map? Who else has been invited to see it? And when any of those changes, what follows and what lags behind?

Take the questions one at a time, using Apple's Find My as the primary case study because it is the most thoroughly documented consumer design, and noting where others differ.

  1. Physical possession. Whoever holds the tag can press it, remove its battery, read its NFC page, or put it in someone else's bag. Possession grants surprisingly little cryptographic power: it does not reveal the owner's keys or past locations. It grants a great deal of physical power: placement, concealment, destruction.
  2. Proximity-based setup. An AirTag is set up by bringing it close to an iPhone or iPad. The physical step proves only that the setting-up device was near this tag at that moment. It is a convenience and a weak authenticator, not an identity check of the person.
  3. Logical registration. Setup associates the tag with an Apple Account. Apple documents that a paired AirTag is associated with an Apple Account and that an AirTag paired to one account cannot simply be set up on another; the current owner has to remove it first.[2][5] Documented This is the anti-theft property: a stolen tag cannot be re-paired by the thief.
  4. Owner identity and authentication. The account is protected by its password and, on current Apple Accounts, two-factor authentication through trusted devices or phone numbers. Account authentication decides who can sign in to the service.
  5. Hardware identifiers. Each AirTag has a serial number. Apple says it can link a serial number to the paired account and provide account details in response to a valid request from law enforcement.[5] Documented The serial is an accountability identifier, held by Apple; it is not what the tag broadcasts over Bluetooth.
  6. Cryptographic identity. At setup, the owner's device generates key material for the tag: in Apple's documented design, an elliptic-curve key pair and a secret from which a sequence of short-lived keys is derived.[1] Documented Whoever holds that private material can decrypt the tag's location reports. Whoever does not, cannot, whatever their account status.
  7. Authorised owner devices. Apple documents that the private key and secret are never sent to Apple and are synchronised among the owner's devices through iCloud Keychain, which is end-to-end encrypted.[1] A device gets those keys by joining the owner's keychain, which requires more than the account password: Apple describes keychain joining and recovery as gated by trusted-device approval or the passcode of an existing device.[11] Documented
  8. Explicit sharing. Since iOS 17 an owner can share an AirTag with up to five other people, who can then see its location, play its sound and use Precision Finding. Apple documents that unwanted-tracking alerts for that tag are suppressed for everyone in the sharing group, because they can already see where it is.[8] Documented A separate feature, Share Item Location, creates a temporary link a third party such as an airline can open after authenticating; the link ends when the item is found, when the owner stops it, or after seven days.[23] Announced
  9. End-to-end key synchronisation. Because keys travel through an end-to-end encrypted keychain rather than an account database, "being signed in" and "being able to decrypt" are different states. Inference A web session in a browser, or a device signed in to some Apple services without being a trusted keychain member, is not automatically a decrypting device. How each surface (web, newly enrolled device, restored backup) obtains decrypting capability is only partly documented, which is itself a point to flag.
  10. Recovery, transfer, reset, revocation. Removing the tag from the account ends the pairing. Losing every trusted device makes recovery depend on the account's recovery mechanisms, which are designed to be hard for an attacker and, therefore, sometimes hard for the owner. Removing a person from a sharing group revokes their future access. Nothing revokes what they already saw.
Account association is not the same thing as unrestricted account-level access to every cryptographic secret.

That sentence cuts both ways. It is good news against the casual attacker: a leaked password, by itself, should not decrypt a tag's history on these designs. It is uncomfortable news for anyone leaving a shared household: the dangerous access is often not the password at all, but a trusted device that was never removed, a sharing grant that was never withdrawn, or a tag that was never theirs to begin with.

Where the exposure really lives

Five patterns account for most real-world misuse of the authorisation layer. None of them requires breaking any cryptography.

  • Shared credentials. Couples and families share passwords and passcodes for good reasons. A shared device passcode is particularly powerful because, on the documented designs, it is part of what unlocks end-to-end encrypted key material.
  • Forgotten authorised devices. An old phone, an iPad in a child's room, a laptop left behind after a breakup. If it is still a trusted, signed-in device, it may still decrypt.
  • Shared item permissions. A sharing invitation accepted in good times is still in force in bad times, and on Apple's design it silences the very alert that would warn the recipient about that tag.
  • Coercive control. Someone who can demand your phone and passcode does not need to attack anything. Technology cannot distinguish a coerced approval from a free one.
  • Weak recovery. Account recovery is the back door every account must have. A recovery phone number or email controlled by the wrong person turns the back door into the front door.

For every tracker, badge and device in our estate: who is the owner of record, which identities can decrypt or read its data, which people have been granted access, and when did anyone last review that list?

Part IVPublic keys, private keys, and the cryptography behind an invisible beacon

Three layers: one for everyone, one for engineers, one for architects and CISOs.

Layer 1 · For everyone: the open padlock

Imagine you hand out thousands of open padlocks. Anyone can take one, put a note in a box, and snap the padlock shut. Only you have the key that opens those padlocks. A stranger can lock a message to you without being able to read anyone else's, and without being able to make a key from the padlock.

That is public-key encryption. The padlock is the public key: it can be shared with the whole world. The key is the private key: it never leaves you. Looking at the padlock, however carefully, does not tell you how to cut the key, at least not with any computer we can build today. (Part IX is about the computer we might build tomorrow.)

A tracker tag hands out padlocks over Bluetooth. A passing phone puts its location in a box, locks it with the tag's padlock, and sends the box to the cloud. The cloud stores boxes it cannot open. The owner's phone holds the key.

One honest caveat belongs even at this level. A padlock tells you nothing about who locked the box or whether the note inside is true. Anyone with a padlock can lock any note. Hold on to that; it matters in Part V.

Layer 2 · For security engineers: the parts list

Symmetric versus asymmetric

Symmetric cryptography uses one shared secret key for both directions: fast, compact, ideal for bulk data, with the problem of getting the key to both parties. Asymmetric (public-key) cryptography uses a key pair: a public key for encrypting to, or verifying from, and a private key for decrypting or signing. It solves key distribution but is slower and its keys are mathematically structured, which is precisely what quantum algorithms attack (Part IX).

Elliptic-curve cryptography

An elliptic curve over a finite field defines a group of points in which "adding" points is easy, and "multiplying" a point by an integer, Q = k·G, is easy too. Going backwards, recovering k from Q and G, is the elliptic-curve discrete-logarithm problem, believed infeasible classically for well-chosen curves of sufficient size. A private key is a random integer d; its public key is the point P = d·G. Elliptic-curve keys are small, which is why they fit in a Bluetooth advertisement. Apple's documented Find My design uses the NIST P-224 curve, whose compressed public keys fit in a single advertisement.[1] Documented

Key agreement and hybrid encryption

Elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman (ECDH) lets two parties who know each other's public keys compute the same shared point: e·P = d·E. A one-way sender, like a finder phone that will never hear back, generates a throwaway (ephemeral) key pair (e, E), computes the shared secret with the recipient's public key, derives a symmetric key from it, encrypts, and sends E along with the ciphertext. The recipient recomputes the same secret from d and E. This pattern is ECIES, the Elliptic Curve Integrated Encryption Scheme.

Authenticated encryption

AES-GCM encrypts and also produces a tag that detects any change to the ciphertext or to associated data. It needs a nonce (number used once) that must never repeat under the same key; reuse is catastrophic. Authenticated encryption gives integrity of the envelope: it proves nobody altered the box after it was sealed. It does not give authenticity of the author when the sealing key is public.

Key derivation, randomness and hashing

A key derivation function (KDF) turns a secret into one or more keys, often with a label so that different uses get unrelated outputs. Apple's design uses the ANSI X9.63 KDF with SHA-256 to turn the ECDH secret into an AES key and nonce.[1] A hash is a one-way fingerprint: easy to compute, infeasible to invert or to collide for good functions. Hashing is not encryption: nothing decrypts a hash. Find My uses a hash of each rotating public key as the lookup index on Apple's servers, so the server can match a request to stored reports without being given the public key itself.[1] All of this depends on good random secret material; a predictable seed collapses everything above it.

Nonces, replay and authenticated metadata

Replay protection means a message accepted once is not accepted again, or not accepted in the wrong context. A Bluetooth advertisement is, by design, a public broadcast with no challenge-response, so it can be recorded and replayed elsewhere. Rotating keys limits how long a recording is useful; it does not make replay impossible within the window.

Rotation is not revocation

Key rotation changes which key is in use on a schedule; the old keys are still valid for what they protected. Revocation declares a key or credential no longer trustworthy. A tag rotating its broadcast key every few minutes is a privacy measure against strangers. It is not a way to remove an owner, a sharing recipient or a stolen device.

Ephemeral identifiers and unlinkability

If each broadcast value is derived from a secret by a one-way function, an observer without the secret cannot tell whether two values came from the same tag. That property is unlinkability, and it is what lets a tag be findable by its owner but not followable by a stranger, in principle. In practice, timing, signal strength, co-travel and implementation details can link values the cryptography leaves unlinked (Part VII).

Secure storage and keychain synchronisation

Private keys live in hardware-backed storage where possible, and move between a user's devices through an end-to-end encrypted sync service, where the service relays ciphertext it cannot read. The security of the whole design reduces, at this point, to the security of joining that sync circle.

Forward secrecy: when it is, and is not, provided

A protocol has forward secrecy if compromising long-term keys later does not expose past sessions. TLS 1.3 provides it through fresh ephemeral key exchange in each session. A Find My-style location report does not have forward secrecy against the owner's keys: the owner must keep the long-term material to decrypt reports at all, so whoever obtains that material can decrypt any reports still stored. Inference What limits the damage is retention, not cryptography: reports are only retrievable for a limited window.

End-to-end encryption versus transport security

Transport-layer security (TLS) protects data between your device and a server; the server sees the plaintext. End-to-end encryption protects data between endpoints, so intermediate servers, including the operator's, handle only ciphertext. A finder phone's upload is both: the report is end-to-end encrypted to the owner, and the upload is additionally wrapped in TLS to the server.

Part VThe complete journey of an encrypted location report

Follow the signal. Follow the trust boundaries. Notice where the key is, and where it is not.

Here is the life of one location report in Apple's documented Find My design, in ten steps.[1] Where Google's Find Hub differs materially, it is noted. The equations are in Appendix A; this Part is about who does what, and who can see what.

  1. The owner registers the tracker. The owner's iPhone sets the tag up over a short-range connection and registers it to the owner's Apple Account.
  2. Key material is provisioned. The owner's device generates an elliptic-curve key pair (d, P) and a 256-bit secret SK₀, shares what the tag needs to derive its rotating public keys, and stores the private material in the owner's end-to-end encrypted keychain. Apple documents that the private key and secret are never sent to Apple. Documented
  3. The tracker broadcasts. When separated from its owner's devices, the tag advertises over Bluetooth LE a public key Pᵢ derived for the current period. Apple documents rotation about every 15 minutes; independent researchers report that a tag separated from its owner keeps one key for much longer, around a day, which trades some unlinkability for battery and for findability.[1][43] Research
  4. A nearby phone observes it. Any participating Apple device in range receives the advertisement without pairing or connecting. Its owner is not asked and is not told.
  5. The finder determines its own location. The phone uses its usual positioning (GNSS, Wi-Fi, cellular). The location in the report is the finder's own estimate of where it was, with its own error, not a measurement of the tag.
  6. The report is sealed. The finder generates an ephemeral key pair, performs ECDH with Pᵢ, derives an AES-GCM key and nonce with ANSI X9.63 KDF over SHA-256, and encrypts its location and a timestamp. Documented
  7. The report is sent. The finder uploads the ciphertext, its ephemeral public key and the hash of Pᵢ to Apple over TLS.
  8. The service stores it. Apple stores the sealed report indexed by the hash. It does not hold d, so it cannot decrypt. It necessarily sees operational metadata such as the time of upload and the network connection that delivered it. Inference
  9. An authorised device requests reports. The owner's device, which holds d and SK₀, recomputes the public keys for the time window it cares about, hashes them, and asks Apple for matching reports through the owner's authenticated session.
  10. The owner decrypts and displays. The owner's device derives each period's private key dᵢ, recomputes the ECDH secret with the finder's ephemeral public key, decrypts, and shows the location on the map.

Google documents the same shape for Find Hub, with two notable differences: location reports are end-to-end encrypted with keys protected by the owner's Android screen lock, and, by default, the network shows an aggregated location only after several Android devices have detected an item, which Google presents as a privacy protection for busy places.[14][15] Documented

Confidentiality is not truthfulness

The envelope is sealed with a public key. Anyone who has heard that public key can seal an envelope too. The service, which by design cannot see inside, cannot check whether the coordinates are real. A finder's own position can be wrong because of poor GNSS reception, indoor positioning error or deliberate spoofing. And a tag's broadcast can be recorded in one place and replayed in another, so that honest phones in the second place truthfully report hearing it there.

In April 2026, Gegenhuber, Liadveikin, Holzbauer and Strobl described exactly this as a practical relay attack on AirTags. Anyone who treats a finding-network location as forensic evidence should corroborate it.

Part VIIIf everything is encrypted, how can a person still be tracked?

Because encryption answers "who may read?", and stalking is a problem of "who should be reading at all?"

Confidentiality is not consent

Everyday version. A sealed letter protects its contents from the postman. It does nothing to stop the letter being about you, sent without your knowledge, to someone who should not have it.

Mechanism. End-to-end encryption guarantees that only holders of the right keys can read. In an unwanted-tracking case, the person reading is the key holder: the tag's legitimate owner in the cryptographic sense. The system is doing its job perfectly, for the wrong principal. End-to-end encryption does not guarantee informed consent, and it was never designed to.

Architectural implication. Consent has to be engineered separately, through mechanisms that reach the person being located: alerts, sounds, discoverability, and rules on what owners may do. Those mechanisms sit outside the cryptographic core, depend on the victim's device, and are inherently probabilistic.

What rotating identifiers protect against, and what they do not

Rotation defends against the passive stranger: someone who sets up receivers and wants to follow a tag, or its owner, across days without having the keys. Because each broadcast value is derived one-way from a secret, the stranger cannot compute the next value, and two values do not, on their face, link.

Rotation does not defend against:

  • The key holder, who can compute every value.
  • Continuity within a period. Within a rotation window, a value is a stable identifier. Long windows, such as the roughly day-long period researchers report for separated AirTags, give a stranger a day of linkage.[43] Research
  • Physical continuity. If one value disappears and another appears at the same instant, in the same place, at the same signal strength, moving in the same direction, the stranger can make a good guess.
  • Radio fingerprints. Tiny manufacturing differences in transmitters can make individual devices recognisable regardless of what they say.
  • Other identifiers. A tag that rotates its payload but not its Bluetooth address, or rotates predictably, leaks anyway. That was the 2025 finding against Tile.[22] Research

Rotating identifiers reduce tracking by passive observers. They cannot eliminate all tracking and correlation risk.

How unwanted-tracking alerts work, conceptually

A phone running detection keeps a short-lived, on-device record of tracker advertisements it hears. It looks for a tracker that is (a) separated from its owner, which compliant trackers signal in their advertisements, and (b) seen repeatedly with this phone over time and distance. When both hold, it alerts. Apple documents this behaviour for AirTags and compatible accessories on iPhone; Google documents it for Android 6.0 and later, alongside a manual scan.[6][16] Documented Separately, a separated AirTag plays a sound when moved after a period away from its owner. Before Android had built-in alerts, researchers reverse-engineered Apple’s iOS protection and shipped an Android app, AirGuard, that warned users of Find My trackers; in their study it often warned sooner than the iOS mechanism.[38] Research

Why detection needs repetition

A single sighting proves nothing: you pass hundreds of strangers' tags on a train. The signal is persistence. That has a simple consequence: detection requires time, and time is exposure.

P(detected by observation n) = 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ

p the chance that any one opportunity (a period of co-travel the phone records) contributes enough evidence · n the number of such opportunities. This is a teaching model with an independence assumption, not a vendor algorithm, but it captures the shape: detection rises with exposure, and is never certain after a fixed time.

The tension is visible right in the formula. Anything that lets the victim's phone link observations, such as slower rotation in separated mode, raises p. The same thing lets a stranger link them too.

False positives, false negatives and delays

  • False positives: a family member's tag in a shared car; a borrowed bag; a tag shared with you (on Apple's design, accepted sharing suppresses alerts precisely to avoid this).
  • False negatives: the victim's phone has Bluetooth off, location services off, an old operating system, or alerts disabled; the tracker does not follow the specification; the tracker is near its owner's device often enough to count as "not separated".
  • Delay: alerts come after a pattern forms, not at placement. Vendors describe timing loosely, and have changed it.
  • Platform compatibility: cross-platform detection depends on both the tracker and the phone implementing the shared specification.

Google is candid about one limit of manual scans: a tracker found by a scan is near you now, but may not have travelled with you, and only trackers separated from their owners appear.[16]

The tension at the heart of the design

Three goals pull against each other:

Owner privacyThe owner's tag should not identify the owner or let strangers follow them.
Third-party unlinkabilityA stranger with receivers should not be able to follow the tag.
Victim detectabilityA person carrying someone else's tag should be able to recognise that it is with them.

The first two say "make broadcasts unlinkable". The third says "let the right observer link them". Design work since 2021 is about letting the right observer, a phone travelling with the tag for long enough, link what a passing stranger cannot. Researchers have proposed cryptographic constructions along exactly these lines, which keep brief encounters unlinkable while letting sustained co-travel become recognisable.[39] Research This is an engineering trade-off, not an impossibility theorem; but every deployed design picks a point on it, and every point has a cost.

DULT: what is standardised, and what is implemented

In May 2024 Apple and Google announced that they had delivered a joint industry specification, Detecting Unwanted Location Trackers, in iOS 17.5 and in Android 6.0 and later, so that each platform can alert about compatible trackers from the other's network and from other makers who adopt it; Chipolo, eufy, Jio, Motorola and Pebblebee were named as committed makers.[9] Documented The work was taken to the IETF, which chartered a DULT working group to standardise a protocol for detecting and interacting with unwanted tracker accessories.[28]

Three distinctions matter. Specified is not standardised: at our research cutoff, we could confirm that the DULT accessory protocol was an IETF Internet-Draft, and could not confirm publication as an RFC.[29] Standardised is not implemented: protection depends on each tracker and each phone. And implemented is not effective: real-world detection depends on settings, operating-system versions, radio conditions and how long a person is exposed.

Part VIIIFour ecosystems, four trust models

Similar goals. Different architectures. Different amounts of public evidence.

It is tempting to treat "item trackers" as one product. They are not. They share a shape (a tag, a crowd of finders, a cloud, an owner), but the custody of keys, what the operator can see, how much has been documented and how much has been independently tested differ a great deal. Part IXQuantum computing: which locks might eventually fail?

A migration roadmap is not a confirmed break. Nothing in this Part says anyone is decrypting tracker reports with a quantum computer today.

Why a quantum computer is not a faster classical computer

Everyday version. A classical computer tries possibilities the way you search a library: one shelf at a time, however fast. A quantum computer does not search faster; for a few special kinds of problem, it arranges for wrong answers to cancel out and right answers to reinforce, so that measuring at the end gives the right one with high probability.

Mechanism. A qubit can be in a superposition of 0 and 1, described by two complex amplitudes. Many qubits can be entangled, so their joint state cannot be described one qubit at a time. A quantum algorithm manipulates amplitudes so that they interfere: paths to wrong answers cancel, paths to right answers add. Measurement collapses the state to one outcome. The art is entirely in the interference; for most problems no useful interference pattern is known, and a quantum computer offers no advantage.

Real qubits are noisy. Running long algorithms needs error correction, which bundles many physical qubits into one reliable logical qubit. The phrase "cryptographically relevant quantum computer" means a fault-tolerant machine large enough to run the algorithms below on real key sizes. No such machine is publicly known to exist.

Shor's algorithm: the problem for public keys

Shor's algorithm finds the period of a function efficiently, which solves both integer factoring (breaking RSA) and discrete logarithms, including on elliptic curves (breaking ECDH, ECDSA and EdDSA). Given a public key P = d·G, a cryptographically relevant quantum computer could recover d. Published resource estimates for 256-bit elliptic curves run to roughly two thousand or more logical qubits and very many operations[44]; for RSA-2048, a 2025 estimate put the requirement below a million noisy physical qubits running for under a week, under stated hardware assumptions.[45] Research estimate Estimates have fallen over time. That is the reason to migrate early, not evidence of a current break.

Grover's algorithm: a smaller problem for symmetric keys and hashes

Grover's algorithm searches an unstructured space of size N in about √N steps. Against a 128-bit key that is, in an idealised count, about 264 sequential quantum operations, each one an error-corrected run of the cipher. Grover's speed-up does not parallelise well (splitting the search across k machines saves only a factor of √k), and the error-correction overhead is enormous. The usual response is to prefer 256-bit symmetric keys for long-lived secrets; it is not to abandon AES. Hash functions are affected similarly: preimage resistance of SHA-256 drops, in the idealised model, to about 2128.

Why the threat models differ

Public-key algorithms rest on structured mathematical problems, and Shor's algorithm exploits exactly that structure: the speed-up is exponential. Symmetric ciphers and hashes are designed to have no exploitable structure, so the best known generic quantum attack is Grover's quadratic speed-up. The practical upshot: elliptic-curve and RSA operations need replacing; symmetric primitives with adequate key sizes need, at most, larger keys.

Harvest now, decrypt later: does it apply to location reports?

"Harvest now, decrypt later" means recording ciphertext today and decrypting it once a quantum computer exists. It matters when three things are true: the ciphertext is retained somewhere an attacker can obtain it, the secret is protected only by quantum-vulnerable public-key cryptography, and the information will still matter when the attack becomes possible.

Apply that test to a Find My-style report, using the documented design: Inference

  • Retention. Reports are retrievable from the service for a limited window (researchers reported about seven days; Apple’s privacy notice separately says a located device’s location is kept for up to 24 hours[10]). An outside attacker would need the service's stored ciphertext or traffic captured in transit, which is additionally inside TLS.
  • What Shor would need. To open one report, the attacker must recover a private key: either the finder's ephemeral key from the ephemeral public key in the report, or the tag's period key from its public key. But the report itself carries only a hash of the tag's public key, and that hash cannot be reversed. The attacker would therefore also need the public key itself, which in practice means having heard the tag's broadcast, or obtained it some other way.
  • Blast radius. Breaking one period's key does not reveal the next: periods are linked by a symmetric key-derivation chain, which Shor does not attack. The owner's master public key is not broadcast.

The conclusion is not "safe forever". It is that the dominant risks to location privacy today are the ones in Parts VI and VII, and the quantum risk to stored location reports is bounded by retention and by the need for radio-side data. The more serious long-term quantum exposure for this ecosystem is elsewhere: in signatures on long-lived, hard-to-update hardware, and in key exchange for the traffic that carries accounts, keychains and backups.

Post-quantum cryptography: the replacements

In August 2024 NIST approved three post-quantum standards: FIPS 203, ML-KEM (module-lattice key encapsulation, for establishing shared keys); FIPS 204, ML-DSA (module-lattice digital signatures); and FIPS 205, SLH-DSA (stateless hash-based signatures, a conservative design whose security rests on hash functions).[30] Documented In March 2025 NIST selected HQC, a code-based key-encapsulation mechanism, as a backup to ML-KEM, with a standard expected later.[31] A further lattice signature, FN-DSA, was in draft at our research cutoff.

Key establishment and signatures are different jobs. A KEM lets two parties agree a secret; it replaces ECDH. A signature proves authorship; it replaces ECDSA. A system usually needs both, and they migrate on different schedules: key establishment first, because recorded traffic can be decrypted later, while a signature only has to be unforgeable at the moment it is checked.

Hybrid transitions run a classical and a post-quantum algorithm together, so that the result is secure if either holds. That is how Apple has deployed it: iMessage's PQ3 protocol since 2024, and, from iOS 26, TLS 1.3 with the hybrid X25519MLKEM768 key exchange on by default for apps using URLSession and the Network framework.[13][12] Documented Whether the specific connections Find My uses benefit from this is not stated in the material we found.

Part XIProtecting real people

Plain guidance, by situation. Safety comes before settings.

Read this first if you think someone is tracking you on purpose

If you are in immediate danger, contact your local emergency number. In the United States, the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233, thehotline.org) can help you plan, and many countries have equivalent services.

Disabling a tracker, removing a device from an account, or changing sharing can tell the person who owns it that you found it, and that can raise the risk to you. Apple's own guidance says to consider your safety before disabling a tracker, because the owner then stops getting its location.[7] It is often safer to make a plan with an advocate, and to keep evidence such as screenshots of alerts, before you change anything.

An ordinary consumer

  • Keep your phone's operating system current; detection and fixes arrive in updates.
  • Leave Bluetooth and the unwanted-tracker alerts on if you can.
  • Use two-factor authentication on your Apple, Google or Samsung account, and know what your recovery contacts are.
  • Once a year, look at three lists: your account's trusted devices, the people you share items or location with, and your recovery contacts.
  • Before giving away or selling a tag, remove it from your account.
  • Treat a tapped NFC link like an email link: do not sign in or pay from it.

A parent or caregiver

  • A tag on a child's bag locates the bag, not the child, and only when phones are nearby. Do not rely on it for safety.
  • Prefer a child's own managed account and family features designed for children over a shared adult account.
  • Age-appropriate honesty matters: tell children what is shared, and with whom, and revisit it as they grow.
  • For an older relative, agree sharing openly, and write down how to turn it off.

You received an unknown-tracker alert

  1. Pause and assess your safety. Are you somewhere safe? Is there someone you trust nearby?
  2. Use the alert's own tools to play a sound, or, on supported iPhones and for compatible tags, to find it more precisely. On Android, the alert and the Scan now feature can help.[6][16]
  3. Consider innocent explanations: a family member's or friend's tag in a shared car or bag.
  4. Record evidence: screenshots of the alert and any map it shows; where and when it appeared. If you find the tag, its serial number can be read by tapping it with an NFC phone, or through the alert.
  5. Decide with help, not alone, whether to disable it. If you believe it was placed deliberately, an advocate or the police can help you choose a safe moment; police may be able to request the owner's account details from the vendor.
  6. If it is in a vehicle and you cannot find it, ask a trusted mechanic, victim-services organisation or the police for help.

Someone who has lost a phone or trusted device

  • Mark it lost through your account's find service; this protects the data on it and helps you find it.
  • If you will not get it back, remove it from your account's trusted devices so it stops receiving synchronised data, then change your password.
  • Check that your recovery contacts and second factors still reach you.

A person leaving a shared digital household

This is where Part III becomes personal. Work through it with an advocate if there is any risk of abuse; otherwise in this order:

  1. List what was shared: accounts, devices, family groups, item sharing, location sharing, passcodes known to the other person.
  2. Use your platform's safety-review tool (Apple's Safety Check is one) to see and stop sharing in one place.[7]
  3. Change your device passcode; then your account password; then review trusted devices and remove any you do not control.
  4. Review recovery contacts and second-factor phone numbers.
  5. For your own tags, remove and re-add them, which generates new keys; for theirs, return them.
  6. Remember that revocation stops future access only. What was seen cannot be unseen.

An enterprise employee

  • If you get an unknown-tracker alert at or near work, tell security or HR as well as anyone else you trust: your employer may be able to help with a safe workplace, travel and parking.
  • Register any company tracker to the company's account, not yours, and hand it back when you leave.
  • Do not scan or search colleagues' belongings yourself.

A privacy-conscious technologist

  • Prefer ecosystems whose report encryption and key custody are documented and independently tested.
  • Look for evidence, not badges: what does the vendor publish about identifier rotation, retention and law-enforcement disclosure?
  • Report vulnerabilities through vendors' disclosure programmes; publish carefully, without enabling stalking.
  • Consider supporting research tools that let people on any platform detect trackers.

Part XIIThe future of ownership and consent

What a finding ecosystem built around the located person, not only the owner, could look like.

Everything below is a proposal, not a description of any shipping product. Several pieces exist somewhere in part; none exists everywhere.

  • Cryptographic agility and post-quantum readiness. Tags and accessories ship with more than one firmware trust anchor, including a post-quantum verification key, and with report formats that carry an algorithm identifier. Retirement dates are published for hardware that cannot migrate.
  • Consent-aware sharing. Long-lived sharing grants expire unless renewed, and both parties are periodically asked whether they still want them. A shared tag shows its sharing to everyone it is shared with.
  • Periodic access reviews. A yearly, gentle prompt to look at trusted devices, sharing and recovery contacts, designed to be safe to dismiss in an abusive situation.
  • Hardware-backed device enrolment. Joining an account's key-sync circle is bound to hardware and to an explicit approval on an existing device, with a clear record of who approved whom.
  • Ownership transparency. A person who finds a tag can learn, safely, whether it is registered, whether it is separated from its owner, and how to get help, on any platform.
  • Privacy-preserving anti-stalking detection. Designs in which brief encounters stay unlinkable but sustained co-travel becomes detectable, implemented across every major platform and tested in public.
  • Secure transfer and revocation. Transferring a tag re-keys it; removing a device revokes its keys, and the system says plainly what revocation cannot undo.
  • Stronger report integrity. Owner-side plausibility checks, short validity for broadcasts, and research into attesting finders without identifying them.
  • Separation of identities. Personal, family and enterprise ownership in distinct, visible compartments, so that a company tag never lives in a personal account and a family member's tag never silently becomes a monitoring tool.
  • Limits on metadata retention. Published retention for ciphertext and for operational metadata such as query logs, not only for decrypted locations.
  • Privacy-preserving auditability. Owners can see a record of which of their devices fetched reports and when, without the operator learning more.
  • Transparent ecosystem governance. Cross-vendor specifications completed as open standards, with independent testing of how quickly and how reliably alerts work in the real world, and with victims' advocates at the table.

A thing is not a person

Every design choice in this guide is, underneath, a choice about whose interests the system serves when they conflict. The owner of a suitcase wants it found. The person carrying a stranger's tag wants to be left alone. The network has no idea which situation it is in: the radio packets are identical. The difference exists only in human relationships the system cannot see.

That is why the strongest cryptography in this story protects the weakest promise. Encryption keeps the operator from knowing where your suitcase is. It cannot know whether the "suitcase" is a person's handbag, whether the "owner" is a stalker, or whether the person who agreed to sharing three years ago would agree today. Those are not technical facts. They are questions of consent, and consent has to be designed for, renewed and, when it is withdrawn, honoured.

The ability to locate an object must never be mistaken for an unrestricted right to monitor the human being carrying it.

Technology may give an object a voice, an account an identity, and a network the ability to remember.

Encryption can decide who reads. It cannot decide who should.

Finding a possession is an engineering question. Following a person is a moral and legal one.

Human privacy must remain a right, not a feature available only when someone remembers to turn it on.

A.1 Elliptic curves and the discrete-logarithm problem

E : y² ≡ x³ + a·x + b (mod p) Q = k·G

p a large prime defining the field (for P-224, p = 2²²⁴ − 2⁹⁶ + 1) · a, b curve constants (P-224 uses a = −3) · G a fixed generator point · n the number of points G generates (about 2²²⁴ for P-224) · k an integer · k·G G added to itself k times, computed fast by double-and-add. Given Q and G, finding k is the elliptic-curve discrete-logarithm problem (ECDLP).

The best known classical attack, Pollard's rho, takes about √n group operations, so P-224 offers about 2112, i.e. 112-bit security. NIST's key-management guidance treats 112-bit security as acceptable through 2030, independently of any quantum consideration; a migration away from P-224 was therefore already on the horizon. A.2 ECDH and ECIES

Recipient: private d, public P = d·G Sender: ephemeral e, E = e·G Shared: S = e·P = e·d·G = d·E Keys: (K, IV) = KDF(S, E) Ciphertext: (C, T) = AES-GCM-Enc(K, IV, message) Sent: E, C, T

KDF a key derivation function (Find My: ANSI X9.63 with SHA-256) · K a symmetric key · IV the nonce · T the authentication tag. The recipient computes S = d·E and repeats the derivation. Anyone who knows P can produce a valid (E, C, T) for any message: ECIES gives confidentiality and integrity of the envelope, not authentication of the sender.

A.3 The Find My rotating-key schedule (as documented)

Setup: d (private), P = d·G, SK₀ (256-bit secret) Step i: SKᵢ = KDF(SKᵢ₋₁, "update") (uᵢ, vᵢ) = KDF(SKᵢ, "diversify") dᵢ = uᵢ·d + vᵢ (mod n) Pᵢ = uᵢ·P + vᵢ·G Check: dᵢ·G = (uᵢ·d + vᵢ)·G = uᵢ·(d·G) + vᵢ·G = uᵢ·P + vᵢ·G = Pᵢ

i the period counter · uᵢ, vᵢ integers derived from the period secret · dᵢ, Pᵢ that period's key pair. The tag needs only P and SKᵢ to compute Pᵢ, and never needs d. The owner, holding d and SK₀, can compute every dᵢ. A stranger who sees Pᵢ cannot compute Pᵢ₊₁ without SKᵢ.[1]

Two consequences follow. The chain is one-way: learning SKᵢ reveals all later keys but not earlier ones. And the owner's long-term material (d, SK₀) unlocks everything, which is why its custody (Part III) is the real security boundary.

A.4 The lookup index

index = SHA-256(Pᵢ)

The service stores reports under the index. An owner proves nothing cryptographic by presenting an index; access to reports is governed by account authentication, and reading them by possession of dᵢ. The hash hides Pᵢ from anyone who sees only the database.

A.5 Distance from radio

UWB: d = c · (t_round − t_reply) / 2 BLE: RSSI(d) ≈ RSSI(d₀) − 10·η·log₁₀(d / d₀) + X_σ

c speed of light · t_round, t_reply as in Part II · RSSI(d₀) received power at a reference distance d₀ · η the path-loss exponent (about 2 in free space, larger indoors) · X_σ random variation from multipath, bodies and orientation, often several decibels. Because X_σ is large, BLE signal strength cannot give dependable distance; UWB timing can.

A.6 Grover and Shor, in one line each

Grover: ≈ (π/4)·√N oracle calls to search N items Shor (ECDLP over an n-bit prime field): ≈ 9n + 2⌈log₂ n⌉ + 10 logical qubits (Roetteler et al.)

For N = 2¹²⁸ (an AES-128 key), Grover needs about 2⁶⁴ sequential calls in the idealised count. For n = 224 the logical-qubit formula gives about 2,042; for n = 256 about 2,330, before error-correction overhead, which multiplies physical qubits by orders of magnitude.[44] These are resource estimates for a machine that does not exist, and they have moved over time.

A.7 The cross-platform detection protocol, conceptually

The DULT approach, as described by Apple, Google and the IETF working group, has three parts. Documented at this level

  1. Advertising state. A compliant accessory indicates in its advertisements whether it is near its owner or separated, so phones need not track tags that are with their owners.
  2. Detection. A phone applies its own heuristics to separated accessories observed with it over time. The specification does not fix a single algorithm.
  3. Interaction. A phone that has detected an accessory can ask it to play a sound and can retrieve identifying information and instructions for disabling it, so that the located person can act.

None of this requires breaking the owner's encryption. That is the elegance and the limit: detection relies on the accessory's cooperation and on the phone being present, on and capable.

  • Remove trusted devices you do not control.
  • Review recovery contacts and second-factor numbers.

  • Photograph in place; record time and location.

Appendix DMyth versus fact

Table 14 · Popular misunderstandings
MythFact
“AirTags have GPS.”A basic AirTag has no GNSS receiver. Its map location is a nearby phone's estimate of its own position.
“NFC lets someone track you from across the street.”NFC works at centimetres and plays no part in crowdsourced tracking. Its risks are phishing tags and relay against access systems.
“If it's end-to-end encrypted, nobody can track me with it.”Encryption stops the operator reading. The owner, the key holder, reads perfectly. Encryption is not consent.
“The stranger whose phone relayed my tag knows where it is.”In the documented designs, the finder learns its own location, which it already knew, and nothing about the owner.
“Changing my password locks everyone out.”Trusted devices, sharing grants and recovery contacts survive a password change unless reviewed.
“Knowing my password lets someone decrypt my tag's history.”On the documented designs, decryption keys are released only through additional device or passcode gates. A password alone should not be enough.
“The location on the map must be right.”Reports are not authenticated as true; finder positioning errs, and relay can inject false positions.
“Rotating IDs make trackers untraceable.”Rotation defeats simple following by strangers; timing, signal, co-travel and long windows can still link.
“Anti-stalking alerts are instant and foolproof.”They need repeated observation, a supported and enabled phone, and a compliant tag. They reduce risk; they do not remove it.
“Quantum computers can already read AirTag reports.”No cryptographically relevant quantum computer is publicly known. The threat is a future one, and bounded for short-retention data.
“Post-quantum migration is just swapping an algorithm.”It involves hardware, firmware trust anchors, protocols, clouds and identities, some of which cannot be upgraded.
“A public key is like a password.”A public key is meant to be shared. It does not reveal the private key and cannot decrypt what was encrypted to it.
