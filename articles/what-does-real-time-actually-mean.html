25 Million Viewers, Two Seconds, and One Dangerous Assumption

When a nation reacts at once, who decides the truth? What “real-time” actually promises, why a mood meter and a payment ledger must never share a definition of failure, and why the technology stack is the last interesting question.

Paddy · PaddySpeaks.com · November 17, 2026 · 12 min read

A Six, and Twenty-Five Million Thumbs

Final over. India need eleven from six balls. The bowler runs in, the batter clears the front leg, and the ball disappears over long-on.

In Mumbai, a software engineer leaps off the sofa and hammers the 🔥 button eleven times. In Coimbatore, a retired schoolteacher who has watched India lose from this exact position before taps 🙏 once and keeps her eyes shut. In a hostel in Kanpur, a student who backed the other side in a group-chat bet throws the remote at the wall. He taps nothing. He is still part of the audience.

Now multiply. Twenty-five million people are watching, and the product team has promised that the collective mood will appear on every screen within two seconds. Somewhere, an engineering manager is already drawing boxes.

The number is not a fantasy. During the India–New Zealand World Cup semi-final on 10 July 2019, Hotstar reported a peak of 25.3 million concurrent viewers, a figure its year-end report says was verified by its CDN, Akamai. Four years later Disney reported 59 million concurrent viewers for the 2023 World Cup final. Indian cricket is one of the few workloads on earth where the traffic spike comes with a scorecard.

Pose this as an interview question and the answer arrives within ninety seconds: Kafka, Spark or Flink, Redis, WebSockets. It is not wrong. It is early. It answers how before anyone has asked what exactly are we promising, and what happens when that promise breaks? Technology selection is the last interesting question here. It is also the only one most whiteboards have room for.

The Architecture Everyone Loves to Draw

The gateway admits traffic. The stream absorbs the burst so a slow aggregator does not drop events on the floor. The aggregator counts per emoji per window, a fast store holds the latest snapshot, and a distribution tier pushes it down millions of open connections.

HistoricalHotstar engineering described something close to this in 2019: a Go service writing reactions to Kafka asynchronously, Spark Streaming aggregating in two-second micro-batches (chosen over Flink, the team wrote, for micro-batching and community support), and results pushed through Hotstar's in-house, MQTT-based PubSub, which its engineers presented as built for 50 million concurrent connections. The write-up reports roughly five billion emojis from 55.8 million users across the 2019 World Cup. LinkedIn met a cousin of the problem with live-video likes, where the hard part was routing each like only to the data centres and viewers subscribed to that video. Everything below is a hypothetical design for today.

Illustrative arithmetic, not a benchmark

AssumptionOne in five of 25 million viewers reacts in the five seconds after a six: five million people, four taps each. That is 20 million taps, or 4 million per second at peak.

If each app coalesces its taps into one small message per second, ingest falls to roughly one to two million messages per second. Now look the other way. If every viewer receives a 200-byte update every two seconds, the platform sends 12.5 million messages per second, about 2.5 GB/s of payload before protocol overhead. The interesting load is not coming in. It is going out.

The aggregate itself is absurdly small: ten emoji types, ten counters. The hard parts are the burst, the fanout and the timing. So the essentials are client-side coalescing, admission control at the edge, a fanout tier for tens of millions of connections, and a freshness timestamp on every snapshot. Pre-aggregation nearly is one: a thousand ingest nodes flushing local counters four times a second send four thousand tiny records per second instead of millions. A durable log is optional; for a mood meter nobody will audit, Kafka may be an expensive diary. So is a distributed stream processor. Summing counters needs discipline about time, not a distributed state machine.

The classic traps still apply. Key the stream by match and every reaction in India lands on one partition, a hot partition with a cricket score. Duplicates appear the moment clients retry. And backpressure needs somewhere to go: “drop the oldest reactions” is a legitimate policy here, which tells you immediately this is not a bank.

The Most Dangerous Word in Technology: Real-Time

“Within two seconds” sounds like a requirement. It is five unanswered questions wearing a stopwatch.

Two seconds from what? The tap, ingestion, the window closing, publication, or pixels changing on a phone on a moving train? And for whom: the median viewer, the 99th percentile, or literally everyone? Google's SRE book writes latency objectives as percentiles because averages hide the users having the worst time, and Dean and Barroso's “The Tail at Scale” explains why the tail worsens as systems fan out. Twenty-five million recipients is a great deal of fanning out.

StageIllustrative budgetWhat it costs

Add the upper bounds and the window alone has spent the entire budget. Either the window shrinks and slides, or the promise is restated as “two seconds from window close at p95”, which is a perfectly honest promise and a very different one.

Then the trap nobody draws: the video is not live either. A live stream can run many seconds behind the stadium, and not every viewer is equally behind. A mood meter that is perfectly fresh against the wall clock will show the celebration of a six to someone whose stream has not yet shown the ball being bowled. You have built a spoiler engine with excellent p99 latency.

The fix is a definition, not a framework. Each reaction carries the stream position its viewer was watching; aggregates are keyed to that media time, the way Flink's event-time model separates when something happened from when it was processed; each screen shows the mood for the moment it is showing. No tuning delivers that if nobody asked the question.

“A pipeline can meet every latency target and still deliver a bad experience. The dashboard was green. The viewer was spoiled.”

Hotstar Versus a Payment Platform: Two Definitions of Failure

For a mood meter, losing half a percent of reactions is invisible, and so is counting a few twice. Now put money in the same boxes.

Payments need a durable record written before anyone hears “success”, and idempotency so a retry after a timeout does not charge twice; Stripe's idempotency keys exist because networks fail and a charge endpoint called twice double-charges a customer. They need a double-entry ledger, reconciliation and an audit trail. Even analytics near money needs care: Uber built exactly-once processing with Flink, Kafka transactions and deduplication for its ads pipeline because double-counted clicks would overcharge advertisers.

Be precise about eventual consistency, because payments use it on purpose. Card authorisation and settlement complete at different times, which is why reconciliation exists. Dashboards, reports, fraud features, notifications and replicated balance views can lag, provided they say so. The decision cannot: the check that funds exist and the debit that consumes them, the uniqueness of a payment, the atomic pair of ledger entries. Eventual consistency is acceptable for knowing about money. It is unacceptable for moving it.

And beware the correct total. Customer A is charged ₹500 twice. Customer B's ₹500 payment is silently dropped. The day's total is perfect, two customers have been wronged, and every dashboard is green. In a mood meter the aggregate is the product. In payments the aggregate is a checksum over the product, and checksums can be fooled.

“The same architecture that makes one business successful can make another business bankrupt.”

The Architecture Nobody Draws: The Human Being

Every box so far treats a tap as a fact. It is not. It is a claim: someone, or something, would like you to believe the crowd feels this way.

A script taps faster than any fan. A fan club can be asked to flood 😡 during a rival's innings. And a flood during the final over is a cheap way to knock over a highly visible feature and see what else falls with it.

The defences are unglamorous: per-account and per-device limits, so one person gets one voice per second however hard they tap; authenticated sessions, with anonymous reactions discounted; coordination signals such as bursts from brand-new accounts or identical device fingerprints; a cap on how far any one account or network can move the needle. All of it runs before aggregation, because a fake reaction once summed cannot be un-summed without the raw events. That is the best argument for the durable log I called optional. Optional, it turns out, depends on whether you will ever need to change your mind.

Even with perfect defences, the meter measures only the people who chose to tap. The schoolteacher with her eyes shut is not in it, nor the student whose remote is in pieces. A mood meter is a weather vane, not a census: it shows which way expressed participation is blowing among self-selected users, not how India feels. Label it accordingly.

And the display changes what it measures. In Salganik, Dodds and Watts' 2006 MusicLab experiment, people who could see what others had downloaded produced more unequal and less predictable outcomes than those who could not. A mood meter broadcasts a social signal back to the crowd it measures. That makes it a feedback loop, and feedback loops deserve as much design attention as partitions.

Intelligence Is Not the Same as Reliability

The fashionable next step is to put an AI agent in charge of the stadium. Some of that is sensible. An agent reads dashboards faster than a sleepy on-call engineer, correlates a latency spike with a deploy, suggests pre-scaling before the toss and flags patterns that look coordinated.

Now the final over. Reactions jump forty-fold in three seconds, perfectly synchronised, from every region. An agent taught that synchronised surges look like attacks concludes, in flawless prose, that the platform is under a bot attack, and throttles ingest. It is not an attack. It is a six. The system has suppressed the most genuine moment of the match, and the post-incident summary will be beautifully written.

Deterministic controls (rate limits, idempotency, schema checks, ledger invariants) give the same answer every time and can be tested. Probabilistic recommendations can be right most of the time and still cannot be proven right for the next case. So agents recommend; bounded deterministic policies act; a human approves anything irreversible or wide in blast radius; every agent action is logged with the evidence it saw; and a kill switch and fallback never depend on the agent. (I have argued this at length in The Supervised Agentic Enterprise.) A system that is usually brilliant and occasionally confidently wrong needs a supervisor, not a promotion.

When the Stadium Goes Silent: Designing for Failure

Each of these will happen. The only choice is whether the response was designed or improvised live.

FailureWhat viewers seeDesigned response
Regional cloud outage in the final overThe meter freezes for a regionRegions aggregate independently; the global view says “partial”; the video never depends on the meter
A consumer falls behindOld excitement shown as liveEvery snapshot carries its event time; screens grey it out past a freshness threshold
Fanout tier overloadedDisconnects, then reconnect stormsShed the meter before the video; jittered reconnects; slower updates under load
Duplicates after client retriesInflated countsClient event IDs and a bounded dedup window; approximate is fine here
A broken aggregation windowSpikes, zeroes, double-counted windowsInvariant checks (totals never exceed admitted events); recompute from the log
A delayed stream replays the pastYesterday's excitement as today's realityReplays go to a separate path, never into the live view

The principles are few. Degrade gracefully: the meter is a garnish and must never take down the meal. Isolate faults so one region or tier fails alone. Keep replay wherever you might need to recount. Set recovery objectives per feature; the meter's honest one may be “lose it and move on”. And rehearse: Netflix built Chaos Monkey in 2011 to terminate production instances on purpose, because the only failure plan you can trust is one that has already met failure. Resilience is designed in. You cannot buy it as a managed service any more than you can buy fitness with a gym membership.

The Architect's Real Job: Making Tradeoffs Explicit

Six questions, asked before anyone names a product:

1

What is the business outcome?

“Make the match feel shared” and “move money exactly once” are different systems: one sells the aggregate, the other the record.

2

What exactly must be correct?

Name the invariant. Meter: no single actor moves the needle; stale is never shown as live. Payments: one charge per intent; the ledger balances.

3

How fresh, and against which clock?

Wall clock, event time or the viewer's place in the stream. Every tighter promise multiplies cost.

4

How much inconsistency or loss is tolerable?

Put a number on it: “up to one per cent loss, five seconds stale, labelled.” A number can be tested. “Minimal” cannot.

5

What happens during failure?

Write the degraded mode before the happy path, or the system will choose one for you, live.

6

What does the guarantee cost?

Exactly-once processing, multi-region state and durable logs cost money and pager hours. Pay where the invariant demands it, nowhere else.

Answered honestly, these questions sometimes delete half the diagram and sometimes double it. For the mood meter they may remove the distributed processor and add a media-time key nobody had thought of. For payments they add a ledger, idempotency storage and reconciliation that no streaming benchmark will ever reward. Either way, the stack is chosen last, for reasons someone can write down.

Back to the Stadium

Twenty-five million people watched the same delivery and did not feel the same thing. The engineer in Mumbai felt triumph, the schoolteacher in Coimbatore a relief she was afraid to trust, the student in Kanpur the cost of a bad bet. Only one of them tapped anything.

The system's job is not to manufacture consensus from those taps. It is to represent participation accurately enough for its stated purpose, and to say plainly what that purpose is.

A good engineer knows how to move 25 million events.

A good architect knows which events matter, how much truth can be sacrificed for speed, and when sacrificing truth is unacceptable.

So here is the question I would leave with every team proud of its latency charts: as our systems get faster, are they becoming more trustworthy, or are we simply getting quicker at being confidently wrong?
