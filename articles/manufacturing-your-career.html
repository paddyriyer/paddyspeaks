Manufacturing Your Career10 Lessons in Human Psychology That Job Seekers Were Never Taught

From Edward Bernays and Noam Chomsky to generative AI, job interviews and workplace success — how influence works, how to spot it being used on you, and how to use the honest half of it.

Paddy Iyer21 November 202625 min read

How to read the labels in this essay

Research marks a claim backed by a named study or official source, listed at the end. Practice marks my recommendation: useful, tested in real careers, but not a controlled experiment. Fictional marks a composite scenario written to illustrate an idea; no real person, company or hiring outcome is described.

Where research is mixed or an analogy is only an analogy, I say so. Nothing here is invented to sound authoritative — which, given the topic, would be rather embarrassing.

Part 1

Who is actually manipulating whom?

Fictional

Two people apply for the same analytics role.

Candidate A has the stronger file: a first-class degree, eleven certifications, and a GitHub profile that looks like a small, well-funded research lab. Interview after interview ends the same way — “we were very impressed, and we’ve decided to move forward with another candidate.”

Candidate B has fewer credentials. But when asked about a past project, B says: “Our weekly report took two analysts a full day. I rebuilt it so it ran in twenty minutes, which freed one of them to work on churn — the thing the director actually lost sleep over.” B gets the offer.

So did competence lose to perception? Or did one candidate simply communicate competence better?

The honest answer is: it depends on what the interview was measuring, and that is the uncomfortable part. Candidate B did not lie. B chose what to put in the listener’s attention, framed it as a problem the listener cared about, and gave evidence. That is persuasion. If B had invented the twenty minutes, it would be fraud. If the interviewer had been dazzled by B’s confidence and ignored a weak answer to a technical question, that would be a measurement failure — and a well-documented one, as we will see.

To untangle this we need five words that people use as if they were interchangeable. They are not.

  • Substance — what you can actually do.
  • Perception — what other people believe you can do.
  • Persuasion — giving people reasons and evidence they can inspect.
  • Influence — changing what people notice, feel or decide, by any means.
  • Manipulation — influence that works by getting around someone’s judgment rather than through it.

That last definition follows the philosophical literature on manipulation, which tends to locate the wrong not in the outcome but in the method: the manipulator bypasses or exploits the target’s rational agency instead of engaging it Research. Hold on to it. It is the line this whole essay walks.

Three people who get confused with one another

Edward Bernays (1891–1995), a nephew of Sigmund Freud, did more than anyone to turn “public relations” into a profession and to bring psychology into it. His 1928 book Propaganda argued, without much embarrassment, that the “conscious and intelligent manipulation” of public habits was an important element of democratic society. His most famous stunt came on Easter Sunday 1929, for the American Tobacco Company: young women marched in New York’s Fifth Avenue parade smoking cigarettes, presented to the press as “torches of freedom” Research. The press reported it without naming the client. Historians still argue about how much the stunt actually changed smoking habits — women were already smoking in public by then — but nobody argues about what it was designed to do: attach a product to an identity.

Edward S. Herman and Noam Chomsky wrote something quite different. Their 1988 book Manufacturing Consent: The Political Economy of the Mass Media proposed a “propaganda model”: news is shaped less by conspiracy than by structure — ownership and profit, dependence on advertising, reliance on official sources, organised “flak” against inconvenient coverage, and a dominant ideology Research. Their point was not that journalists are puppets. It was that filters shape what gets through, even when everyone involved believes they are acting freely.

Sylvain Timsit is the one most people have never heard of — even though they have probably read him. In 2002 the French writer published a short text of ten “strategies of manipulation” on his website, syti.net. Over the following decade it went viral under Noam Chomsky’s name, and it still circulates that way, in many languages. The list is Timsit’s, not Chomsky’s Research. It is a polemic, not a peer-reviewed study — but each of its ten items points at a real, studied mechanism of human attention and judgment. That is why it is useful here.

If psychology can shape what millions believe, buy and discuss, can understanding the same mechanisms help one person navigate an interview, a hierarchy or a career change?

Yes — with one condition that I will repeat until you are sick of it. Persuasion communicates genuine value. Manipulation deliberately distorts judgment. Everything below takes the manipulative version, explains why it works on brains like ours, shows how it may be used on you in hiring, and then turns the underlying psychology into something you can use with a clear conscience.

Part 2

Ten strategies, turned into ten career lessons

Each lesson has the same six parts: the mechanism; how it shows up in hiring; how to defend yourself; how to reverse it honestly; a scene; and one exercise you can do today. Skim the headings first if you like — they are written to be useful on their own.

1

The art of directing attention

Distraction → attention, spent on evidence

The mechanism

Attention is scarce and selective. In the famous “invisible gorilla” study, about half of viewers counting basketball passes failed to notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene Research. Working memory holds only a handful of items at once. Whoever decides where attention goes largely decides what is remembered.

In hiring

Recruiters skim. A résumé listing fifty skills does not read as fifty strengths; it reads as noise, and the reader’s eye lands wherever it lands. Interviews do the same thing in reverse: a long, brilliant tangent about an architecture choice can bury the one question the panel actually needed answered.

Defend yourself

Notice when an interviewer or recruiter talks at length about perks, mission and “family culture” while the questions you asked — about scope, manager, budget, on-call — go unanswered. Write your questions down and return to them politely.

Reverse it honestly

Choose three job-relevant achievements and make them impossible to miss: top third of the résumé, first sentence of your introduction, first screen of your portfolio. Directing attention to real evidence is not hiding weaknesses — as long as you answer directly when asked about them. Practice

Fictional

  • InterviewerTell me about yourself.
  • Before“I started in 2014 at a consultancy, where I learned Java, then Python, then a bit of Scala, and I got my AWS certification, then my Azure one…” (four minutes later, still in 2018)
  • After“I’m a data engineer who makes slow, expensive pipelines fast and boring. Three examples: … Which would be most useful to go into?”

Do this today. Print your résumé, give it to someone for ten seconds, take it back and ask what they remember. If it isn’t your three best achievements, move them.

2

Become the person who solves problems

Problem–reaction–solution → real problems, real solutions

The mechanism

Timsit’s version: create (or exaggerate) a problem, wait for the public to demand action, then offer the solution you wanted all along. It works because people value solutions far more when they feel the pain first.

In hiring

Organisations do not hire degrees; they hire to make a problem go away — a backlog, a churn rate, a migration, a regulator. A job description is a blurry photograph of that problem.

Defend yourself

Be wary when a role is sold to you as a rescue mission (“everything is on fire, only you can save it”) without anyone saying why it caught fire or what authority you will have. Ask who owned it before and what changed.

Reverse it honestly

Never manufacture a crisis. Instead, find the real one: read the job description for verbs and pain, check the company’s public filings or engineering blog, and ask, “What will tell you in six months that hiring for this role worked?” Then tell your experience as problem → evidence-based response → measured outcome. That is STAR with a spine. Practice

Fictional

  • Candidate“Before I answer — what is the hardest thing on this team’s plate right now?”
  • Manager“Honestly? Nobody trusts the revenue numbers.”
  • Candidate“Then the most relevant thing I’ve done is reconcile two revenue definitions at my last job. Here’s what we found, and what I’d check first here.”

Do this today. Take your target job description. Rewrite each bullet as “They are worried that …”. Match each worry to one true story of yours.

3

Build credibility before asking for trust

Gradualism → trust, earned in small deposits

The mechanism

Change that arrives in small steps meets less resistance than change that arrives at once. A related effect: we tend to like what we have seen often — the “mere-exposure” effect Research. Familiarity feels like safety.

In hiring

Referrals, alumni networks and “I’ve seen her name around” all trade on gradual familiarity. Probation periods are gradualism in the employer’s favour: trust is extended in instalments.

Defend yourself

Watch for scope creeping in small steps — an extra on-call rotation here, a second team there — with no change in title or pay. Each step is small; the total is a different job.

Reverse it honestly

Build credibility in observable deposits: a useful comment on someone’s post, a small open-source fix, a two-paragraph write-up of a problem you solved. A large randomised study on LinkedIn found that moderately weak ties — people you know a little — produced the most job mobility Research. But familiarity is not competence. Being seen often only helps if what is seen is good. Practice

Do this today. Pick five people one step removed from you in your target field. Send one of them something genuinely useful — an article, an answer, an introduction — with no request attached.

4

Understand the psychology of future promises

Deferring → promises, tested before accepted

The mechanism

A painful decision is easier to accept if its cost is placed in the future. Humans discount the future steeply and inconsistently — economists call it present bias, or hyperbolic discounting Research. We also over-trust our future selves: “next year” feels like a different, richer country.

In hiring

“We review salaries every year.” “There’s a clear path to senior.” “Equity will be meaningful when we raise.” None of these is a lie by default. None of them is a commitment either.

Defend yourself

Convert every future promise into four questions: When? Decided by whom? Based on what criteria? Can it be in writing? A sincere promise survives all four. Ask how many people in this team were promoted last year, and what they did to get there.

Reverse it honestly

Use the same mechanism in your own negotiation, transparently: if the base salary cannot move, propose a dated, written review — “a compensation review at six months, against these three goals.” You make the future concrete instead of vague. Practice

Fictional

  • Recruiter“The band is fixed, but you’ll be first in line for promotion.”
  • Candidate“That’s encouraging. Could we put the review date and the criteria in the offer letter, so we’re both clear what ‘first in line’ means?”

Do this today. List every promise in your current offer or job that starts with “will”. Mark each one written or verbal. Ask for the important verbal ones in writing.

5

Communicate clearly without being condescending

Treating people like children → clarity that respects the listener

The mechanism

Timsit argued that talking to adults as if they were children lowers their critical guard. The research underneath is about fluency: information that is easy to process feels more true and more likeable Research. In one study with a gloriously ironic title, needlessly long words made writers seem less intelligent, not more Research.

In hiring

Many brilliant technical candidates lose executive interviews not because they lack depth, but because they show all of it. The executive needs the decision, the risk and the cost. The candidate gives the architecture diagram.

Defend yourself

When a pitch to you is suspiciously simple — “it’s basically a startup inside a big company” — ask for the complicated version. Simplicity that hides material facts is not clarity.

Reverse it honestly

Translate, don’t dilute. Lead with the outcome, then the mechanism, then the detail the listener asks for. Clear is not the same as childish; childish is assuming the listener cannot handle the trade-off. Practice

Fictional

  • Before“We migrated from a lambda architecture to a kappa architecture with exactly-once semantics on the stream processor.”
  • After“We stopped computing the same numbers twice in two systems. Reports now agree, they arrive in minutes instead of overnight, and the cloud bill fell. The trade-off was a harder migration — happy to go into how we managed it.”

Do this today. Explain your most technical achievement in three sentences to someone outside your field. Every time they frown, you have found a word to replace.

6

Make your achievements memorable

Appeal to emotion → true stories, carefully told

The mechanism

Stories “transport” listeners: when people are absorbed in a narrative, they scrutinise it less and remember it more Research. That is exactly why emotion can be used to slip weak claims past judgment.

In hiring

“I’m passionate about data” is an emotional claim with no evidence attached. It is also, on a conservative estimate, the most common sentence in every cover letter ever written.

Defend yourself

Notice emotional pressure aimed at you: exploding offers (“this expires Friday”), guilt (“the team was so excited about you”), flattery that substitutes for specifics. Slow down. Real offers can usually wait a few days.

Reverse it honestly

Don’t claim the emotion — show the behaviour that implies it. Curiosity is the weekend you spent tracing why a number was wrong. Resilience is what you did after the launch failed. Never invent hardship; interviewers who have heard a thousand stories can smell a fabricated one, and if they can’t, you still have to live with it. Practice

Fictional

  • Before“I’m very passionate about customer experience.”
  • After“I read every support ticket tagged ‘billing’ for a month because our dashboard said satisfaction was fine and the tickets said otherwise. The tickets were right. We changed the invoice email, and billing tickets roughly halved.”

Do this today. Find every adjective about yourself in your résumé or LinkedIn summary (“passionate”, “driven”, “results-oriented”). Replace each one with a fact that proves it, or delete it.

7

Reverse the strategy through knowledge

Keeping people ignorant → closing the information gap

The mechanism

When one side of a deal knows much more than the other, the better-informed side sets the terms. Economists have studied this information asymmetry since George Akerlof’s 1970 paper on the market for used cars Research.

In hiring

The employer knows the salary band, how many people applied, how interviews are scored, what the last person in the role was paid and why they left. The candidate often knows a job title and a logo.

Defend yourself

Several US jurisdictions — including Colorado, California, Washington and New York City — now require many employers to publish pay ranges in job postings Research. Use them. Read public company filings for strategy and risk; read the engineering or product blog; ask current and former employees (politely, briefly) what the job is really like.

Reverse it honestly

Ask informed questions in the interview: “The annual report mentions moving upmarket — how does this role support that?” Informed questions do two things at once: they get you information, and they signal exactly the curiosity the employer says it wants. Practice

Do this today. For your top target employer, find three facts from primary sources (filings, the company’s own job postings, its own blog) and turn each into one interview question.

8

Resist the culture of superficial credentials

Encouraging mediocrity → proof over badges

The mechanism

Credentials are signals. Michael Spence’s classic work on job-market signalling showed why a credential can be valuable even when it teaches little: because it is costly to obtain and so separates people Research. The catch is obvious — when a signal becomes cheap, it stops separating anyone.

In hiring

A weekend certificate, twenty keywords and a LinkedIn badge collection can create an illusion of expertise. Some employers reward it, because keyword filters are easier than judgment. Many do not, because the best-supported selection methods — structured interviews and work samples — test what you can do, not what you have collected Research.

Defend yourself

Be sceptical of any course or bootcamp that sells a credential as a guarantee of employment. Ask what graduates can do, and ask to see it.

Reverse it honestly

One genuinely difficult project with documented results beats twenty certificates. Build it the way expertise is actually built: deliberate practice on the hardest part, with feedback, repeatedly Research. Write up what broke and how you fixed it. That write-up is the credential. Practice

Fictional

  • Interviewer“I see a lot of certifications. Which one changed how you work?”
  • Candidate“Honestly, none as much as the project where our costs tripled overnight. Can I show you what I found?”

Do this today. For every certificate on your profile, write one sentence: “Because of this, I built/fixed/decided ___.” If you can’t finish the sentence, the certificate is decoration.

9

Learn from rejection without losing perspective

Replacing revolt with self-blame → honest attribution

The mechanism

Timsit’s ninth strategy: persuade people that their misfortune is entirely their own fault, and they stop questioning the system. Psychologists study how we explain outcomes as attributions — internal or external, stable or changeable Research. Attributions affect motivation and self-efficacy, the belief that our effort can change results Research.

In hiring

A candidate receives sixty rejections and concludes they are incompetent. Some of those rejections may have been about preparation. Others were about things the candidate could never see: an internal candidate, a hiring freeze, a requisition kept open “just in case”. Field experiments have also shown that identical résumés can receive different callback rates depending on the name at the top — a structural bias, not a candidate flaw Research.

Defend yourself

Refuse the all-or-nothing story. One rejection is information about one process on one day. Sixty is a pattern — and patterns are analysable.

Reverse it honestly

Keep a simple rejection log: stage reached, role type, what you were asked, what you would change. If you fail at the same stage repeatedly, that is controllable — practise that stage. If you fail at random stages across unrelated roles, the market may be speaking, not you. Practice

Do this today. List your last ten applications in four columns: no reply · screen · interview · final. The column with the most entries is where to spend this week.

10

Understand yourself before algorithms define you

Knowing people better than they know themselves → self-knowledge you can defend

The mechanism

Timsit’s last strategy is the most modern: systems that observe behaviour at scale can predict and steer individuals who have never examined their own patterns. In 2002 that was speculative. In 2026, recommendation feeds and hiring software make it ordinary.

In hiring

Résumé parsing, ranking tools, one-way video interviews and psychometric tests are widely used — but not uniformly, and not by every employer. Their validity varies and some have failed badly: Reuters reported in 2018 that Amazon scrapped an experimental recruiting tool that had learned to penalise résumés associated with women Research. New York City now requires independent bias audits of automated employment decision tools, and the EU’s AI Act treats many hiring systems as high-risk Research.

Defend yourself

Read the privacy notice before a recorded or assessed interview; ask what is evaluated, by whom, and whether a human reviews it. Where the law gives you a right to an alternative process or to information, use it. Do not try to “game” a personality test — inconsistency is often what these tests look for, and a job chosen on a fake profile is a job you will have to fake.

Reverse it honestly

Know your own data first. Build a skills inventory from evidence, not adjectives. Ask three colleagues what they come to you for. Use generative AI as a sparring partner — to rehearse answers, find gaps in your stories, simulate a sceptical interviewer — never to invent experience you do not have. Practice

Do this today. Ask three people who have worked with you: “What do you come to me for?” Compare the answers with your LinkedIn headline.

Part 3

The 2026 career marketplace

Every mechanism above has been automated, at least partly. That is worth stating carefully, because the job-search internet is full of confident claims about “the algorithm” that nobody can verify.

What is reasonably established. Most large employers use an applicant tracking system to collect and route applications. Many use some automated screening or ranking, increasingly with AI. Video interviews, sometimes one-way and recorded, are common. Regulators have noticed: researchers who examined vendors’ public claims found little consistency in how, or whether, they validated and de-biased their tools Research, which is part of why audit laws exist.

What is not. There is no single “ATS algorithm”. Systems differ by vendor and by how each employer configures them. Many still leave the decision to a recruiter skimming a ranked list. Claims that “75% of résumés are never seen by a human” circulate widely without a traceable study behind them; I would not plan a job search around them. And no one — no vendor, no coach, no model — can reliably predict whether you will be hired.

When everybody uses AI to polish their résumé, authenticity may become the rarest differentiator.

Here is the paradox. Generative AI makes average writing free. A thousand candidates now submit fluent, keyword-matched, grammatically spotless applications. Fluency used to signal effort; now it signals a subscription. What remains scarce is what the model cannot supply: your specific numbers, your trade-offs, the detail only someone who did the work would know — the cron job that ran at 2 a.m. in the wrong time zone. Practice

Use AI where it is strong: rehearsal, critique, finding the question you are dreading, turning a ramble into three sentences. Keep it away from where it is dangerous: inventing achievements, padding skills, writing in a voice you cannot reproduce live. An interviewer’s first follow-up question is the fastest lie detector ever built.

Part 4

Three realistic career journeys

Fictional All three are composites. None ends in a miracle; all three end in progress.

The fresh graduate

MistakeMeera lists every course, club and tool, and apologises for having “no real experience”.
MechanismDistraction and self-blame together: attention scattered across forty items, and a frame that invites the interviewer to agree she is inexperienced.
BetterTreat the capstone project as work: a problem, a constraint, a result, a lesson.
RewrittenOur team of four built a scheduling tool for the campus clinic. I owned the data model. The first version double-booked rooms, so I wrote tests for the overlap logic, and in the final month we had none.
Why credibleIt is specific, checkable and modest. It shows learning ability — the thing graduate hiring is really buying. She still fails two final rounds before an offer; the difference is that she now knows which answers to sharpen.

The experienced architect

MistakeDaniel, fifteen years in, answers the CTO’s “why this design?” with twelve minutes of component diagrams.
MechanismHe mistakes depth for value — lesson 5 in reverse. The executive needed risk, cost and decision; he received detail.
BetterLead with the business consequence, offer the depth.
RewrittenWe chose the slower-to-build option because it cut our outage risk during peak season. It cost an extra quarter. I’d make the same call again, and here’s the one thing I’d do differently.
Why credibleIt names a trade-off and admits a cost. Executives trust people who show them the bill. His next panel still rejects him — the role had gone to an internal candidate — but the following one turns into a principal offer.

The career changer

MistakeAna, a nurse moving into product management, buys three PM certificates and hides her clinical background.
MechanismLesson 8: a cheap signal replacing a strong one. Her decade of decisions under pressure, with patients as “users”, is exactly the evidence product interviews look for.
BetterTranslate the experience rather than replace it, and build one small product artefact.
RewrittenI redesigned our ward’s handover checklist after two near-misses. I interviewed twelve nurses, tested three versions over a month, and the one we kept is still in use.
Why credibleIt is discovery, iteration and adoption — product work in all but title. Her first applications to product roles mostly go unanswered; a health-tech company, where the clinical background is an advantage, is where the conversation finally starts.

Part 5

The PaddySpeaks career playbook

Before the interview

  • Research the organisation from primary sources: its filings, its own job pages, its own engineering or product writing. Note one strategic bet and one visible risk.
  • Evaluate the opportunity, not just your chances: scope, manager, team stability, pay range, how success is measured.
  • Pick three achievements that map to the problems in the job description. Write each as problem, action, evidence, result.
  • Build a truthful narrative: why this field, why now, why this company — in under a minute.
  • Rehearse the questions you dread out loud, ideally with a human, otherwise with an AI asked to be sceptical.

During the interview

  • Establish rapport by listening: repeat the interviewer’s problem back before you solve it.
  • Lead with the answer, then the evidence, then stop. Silence is not a failure.
  • Show business understanding: name the cost, the risk, the trade-off.
  • Ask questions that only someone who did the research could ask.
  • Never bluff. “I haven’t used that; here’s how I’d find out, and here’s the closest thing I have done” beats a confident invention every time.

After you get the job

  • Build trust in small deposits: do what you said, when you said, and say so.
  • Deliver one measurable win in the first ninety days, agreed with your manager in advance.
  • Document your accomplishments weekly in a private log; your future self will need it at review time.
  • Handle politics by being legible: write decisions down, credit others in public, escalate with options rather than complaints.
  • Negotiate with evidence, not threats; and build a reputation for reliability rather than theatrics.

Five hard interview questions, answered honestly

Practice These are models of structure, not scripts. Say them in your own words or don’t say them.

“Why should we hire you over someone with more experience?”

“They may well have more years. What I can offer is that I’ve done the specific thing you described — cleaning up a reporting pipeline people had stopped trusting — recently, and at a similar scale. If it helps, I can walk through exactly what I’d check in the first month.”

Why it works: it concedes the true point, then redirects attention to relevant evidence and offers proof.

“Tell me about a time you failed.”

“I pushed a launch date because I was sure the integration was nearly done. It wasn’t; we slipped three weeks and a customer escalated. I told my manager the same day, re-planned with the team, and since then I share a forecast range instead of a single date.”

Why it works: a real cost, clear ownership, and a changed habit. No disguised boast.

“What are your salary expectations?”

“From the posted range and what I’ve seen for similar roles, I’m looking at the upper half of your band, given the scope we discussed. I’m flexible on structure — I care most about the total package and a clear review point.”

Why it works: it uses public information instead of guessing, and makes the future concrete (lessons 4 and 7).

“There’s a gap in your résumé. What happened?”

“I took eight months to care for a family member. During that time I kept my skills current with a project I can show you. I’m ready to commit fully now.”

Why it works: brief, true, and forward-looking. You owe no more detail than that.

“What don’t you know yet that this role will require?”

“I haven’t worked with your billing system’s scale, and I’ve never run on-call for a payments product. For the first, I’d pair with whoever knows it best in week one. For the second, I’d shadow two rotations before taking one.”

Why it works: it refuses to bluff, which is exactly what this question is designed to test.

A seven-day preparation plan (no coach required)

  1. Research the employer from primary sources. Write down one strategic bet, one visible risk, and three questions.
  2. Rewrite the job description as a list of worries. Map each to one of your true stories.
  3. Write three achievement stories: problem, action, evidence, result. Cut each to ninety seconds.
  4. Run the ten-second résumé test and the adjective purge. Fix what fails.
  5. Rehearse aloud: your introduction, the five hard questions above, and the question you most dread.
  6. Do a mock interview with a friend or a sceptical AI. Ask for one thing to cut and one thing to add.
  7. Rest. Re-read your notes once. Prepare your questions for them. Sleep matters more than one more flashcard.

Part 6

The devil’s advocate: when persuasion becomes manipulation

It would be convenient to end here with “communicate well and the best candidate wins”. It would also be untrue.

Persuasive communication can conceal incompetence. A meta-analysis of self-presentation in interviews found that tactics such as impression management related more strongly to interview ratings than to later job performance — what you see may not be what you get, as the authors titled it Research. Unstructured interviews are especially vulnerable.

Confidence is often mistaken for competence. Experiments have found that overconfident people tend to gain status in groups, because others read their certainty as ability Research. Some of the charismatic candidates who win are simply the ones who are wrong most loudly.

Networking can become favouritism. Referrals help candidates; they also reproduce whoever is already inside. Familiarity (lesson 3) is not merit.

Personal branding can become exaggeration. The line between “framing” and “inflating” is crossed the moment a claim would not survive the follow-up question.

The good news cuts both ways. The same research that shows interviews can be fooled also shows how to fool them less: structured interviews, consistent questions, work samples and scoring against criteria predict performance better than charm Research.

So if you are a hiring manager reading this: structure your interviews. If you are a candidate: prefer employers who do. Their process is fairer — to you, and to the quieter competent person sitting next to you.

Successful job seeking must not depend on deception, manufactured crises, hidden conflicts, manipulated emotions, or exploiting other people’s weaknesses. Recognising manipulation is as important as understanding influence, because the techniques are the same; only the respect for the other person’s judgment differs.

You should never have to become a professional manipulator to build a successful career. If a job requires it, that is information about the job.

Part 7

Knowledge, wisdom and the honest microphone

Knowledge is knowing how attention, framing and trust work. Wisdom is knowing when using them would cost someone their judgment. Communication is the bridge between what you can do and what anyone else will ever know about it.

Bernays taught the world how perception could be shaped. Herman and Chomsky examined how institutional structures influence what the public hears. Timsit’s ten strategies challenge us to recognise manipulative communication when we meet it.

Your career need not be an exercise in manufacturing illusions.

Learn how influence works, recognise when it is being used against you, and communicate your genuine abilities so clearly that nobody needs to manufacture your worth. Candidate A, from the beginning of this essay, never needed to become Candidate B’s louder twin. A needed one sentence about a problem solved, and the nerve to say it first.

This week: choose one thing — your résumé’s first third, your “tell me about yourself”, or one promise in your current job that only exists out loud — and change it. Just one. Then notice what changes back.

Before your next interview: a checklist

Print it, or keep it open in a tab. Each line takes minutes, not hours.

  • I can name the problem this role exists to solve.
  • My three strongest, relevant achievements are in the top third of my résumé.
  • Each achievement has a number, a date or another checkable detail.
  • My introduction takes under a minute and ends with a question.
  • I have three questions drawn from primary research.
  • I know the pay range — posted, or researched from credible sources.
  • I have asked for important promises in writing.
  • I can explain my most technical work to a non-specialist.
  • No adjective about me appears without a fact behind it.
  • I have a true answer to “What don’t you know yet?”
  • I know what the assessment measures and whether a human reviews it.
  • Every sentence AI helped me write is one I could defend under follow-up.
  • I have rehearsed out loud at least once.
  • I know which stage I usually fail at, and I practised that stage.
