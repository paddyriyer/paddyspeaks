The Job Posting
Is Not the Job.

The posting is an advertisement. The job is a problem somebody owns, a budget somebody approved, and a decision somebody has to defend.

Paddy Iyer10 September 202613 min read12 scenes

How we know — read this once, then forget it

Three marks appear throughout. Evidence is a figure from a named, checkable source. Practice is my recommendation — useful, unmeasured, occasionally wrong. Contested means the data disagrees with itself, or a famous claim turns out to have no source at all.

Full sourcing sits at the bottom. Nothing here is invented, and where a number is a vendor's own I say so.

Scene 01 · The queue

Everyone can reach the Apply button now.

Which is exactly why reaching it stopped counting for anything.

AI made applying cheap. Cheap signals become noise.

Nobody got lazier. The marginal cost of one more application fell to roughly zero, so sensible people send more of them — LinkedIn reports around 11,000 applications a minute, up about 45% in a year. Evidence [2]

Meanwhile the pile grew fivefold and the number of hands halved. A recruiter in that position does not become a more careful reader. They become a triager — looking for reasons to stop reading, and leaning hard on the two channels that historically wasted the least of their time.

Neither of those channels is the job board.

See the data

Where hires actually come from

In the same Greenhouse dataset, job boards produced nearly 75% of applications but under 50% of hires. Referrals: ~7% of applications, ~40% of hires. Recruiter-sourced: ~3% of applications, ~10% of hires — the best conversion of any channel. [1]

The claim you should stop repeating

Contested “80% of jobs are never advertised.” This has no credible source; it traces back through career books to non-representative surveys, and the channel data above contradicts it — boards still produce close to half of all hires. [3]

The honest version: most jobs are advertised, and most advertised jobs go to the small minority of applicants who arrived with context. There is no hidden job market. There is a hidden queue.

And the market itself?

Not 2009. US job openings sat at 7.3m in July 2026, but the hires rate softened to 3.2% — employers are slow, not absent. Long-term unemployment is real: 1.8m people, a quarter of all unemployed, had been searching 27 weeks or more. If your search is long, you are not an outlier. [4][5]

A 22-year-old and a 52-year-old both need networking. They do not need the same networking strategy.

Why the motion is opposite

The graduate's network is latent — hundreds of people share an institution with them and are mildly obliged to reply. Their job is converting strangers into acquaintances.

The professional's network is dormant — dozens of people have seen them work and haven't heard from them in four years. Their job is reactivating warm-but-cold ties, which needs a completely different opening line.

Evidence The most productive contact is neither a close friend nor a stranger. A five-year randomised experiment across 20m+ LinkedIn users found an inverted-U: moderately weak ties drove the most job mobility — roughly, people with about ten mutual connections you rarely speak to. Close friends know the same jobs you do; strangers have no reason to act. [6]

Contract runs on different physics

It's faster because it usually spends project budget rather than approved headcount, the agency has already screened you, and the loop is one or two conversations. So speed of response is a screening criterion, not politeness. Two rules that save candidacies: never let two agencies submit you for the same requisition (the vendor system records the first, and the client resolves the clash by dropping you), and ask “which client, and are you on their approved vendor list?” before agreeing to anything.

Evidence The market is real and growing modestly — US staffing revenue is forecast at about $183bn in 2026, up 2.4%. [7]

Before you spend 45 minutes tailoring a résumé, spend four minutes proving the job exists.

Scene 03 · The job autopsy

Scene 03 · The four-minute autopsy

Half the evidence isn't in the advertisement.

Four things are on the posting. Five are not — and those five decide whether it's worth your afternoon.

Green

The job looks alive.

On the careers site, real req ID, recent, a nameable human attached. Spend the full 45 minutes.

Yellow

Something smells odd.

Old, reposted, generic, or nobody identifiable. Spend ten minutes and one question — not an afternoon.

Red

Stale, or not a job.

Free-email recruiter, identity or bank details requested, payment asked for. Stop. Send nothing.

Use the full checklist & scoring

Score it, then let the score decide your effort

Practice Positive: on the careers site +2 · plausible req ID +2 · originally posted under 30 days +2 · a named human attached +2 · platform shows active recruiter engagement +1 · other recent postings in the same org +1 · an employee confirmed the team is growing +3.

Negative: live 90+ days unchanged −2 · reposted repeatedly for months −1 · near-identical duplicates or several agencies −1 · announced freeze or active layoffs −2 · no identifiable human anywhere −2.

6 or more → green. 2 to 5 → yellow. Under 2 → red. The point isn't precision. It's making the effort decision before sunk cost sets in.

Six things a posting can be — and only two deserve alarm

  • Genuinely active. Funded, owned, someone needs it filled.
  • Evergreen / pipeline. Deliberately permanent, building a pool. Legitimate. Apply cheaply, don't tailor.
  • Stale. Real once; filled, frozen or forgotten, and nobody closed it. One question settles it.
  • Duplicate / agency repost. One client requirement, republished by several firms. Find the original.
  • Ghost. No intention to fill. Kept up to signal growth or collect market data.
  • Fraudulent. Not a job at all.

Contested How common are ghosts? Studies disagree because they measure different things: roughly 1 in 7 of 175,000+ listings in one analysis (about 21% at senior level), at least 1 in 5 never filled in another. “Never filled” includes cancelled budgets and internal hires — business, not deception. [8][9]

Fraud: the one rule that makes you nearly unscammable

Money and identity move only after a written offer, through channels you initiated. No real employer needs your bank details, national ID or passport scan to interview you.

Evidence This isn't a fringe risk. Reports of job scams to the US FTC tripled between 2020 and 2024, with losses rising from about $90m to $501m. [10] And the 2026 versions are polished: AI-written offer letters, cloned careers pages, deepfaked interviews. “It looked professional” is no longer evidence of anything.

Hard stops, any one of which ends it: you're asked to pay for anything · a cheque arrives and you're asked to forward part of it · an offer with no real interview · the whole process lives in a chat app · a free-email or lookalike domain · unpaid “tasks” that generate value.

Scene 04 · The map

Don't just find the job. Map the humans.

A requisition is the shadow of an org chart. Everything useful is in the chart.

The posting rarely names the manager. You can usually deduce them, because titles encode a function and a domain, and organisations name their managers with the same vocabulary. “Senior Data Engineer — Payments” implies somebody called Engineering Manager, Payments, or Director of Data Engineering.

The second one finds the manager's own post about the role — the strongest proof of life there is, and far warmer to reply to than a cold message. The third reveals every site that republished the ad.

Where the line is

Everything above uses public professional information for its intended purpose. On the other side of the line: hunting for personal phone numbers or home addresses, messaging someone across five platforms, contacting family accounts, scraping at volume, or continuing after no reply.

The asymmetry is brutal. Professional research has modest upside and no downside; one intrusive move converts a possible advocate into someone who warns colleagues about you.

Scene 05 · Which human

“Always go straight to the hiring manager” is wrong about half the time.

Don't pick by seniority. Pick by what you're short of.

The default order, when no signal points either way
  1. A warm internal contact, at any strength. Always first.
  2. An alumnus or second-degree connection — for context and one more name.
  3. A potential teammate — to learn the real problem and identify the manager.
  4. The recruiter — to confirm it's live and get routed.
  5. The hiring manager — once you finally have something specific to say.

The manager is last not because they matter least — they matter most — but because the message you send after four conversations is a completely different message. That's the entire argument for the order.

Two contacts people misjudge: the sourcer is rarely worth messaging (their job is finding people, so the winning move is being findable), and the former employee is undervalued — unconstrained by the recruiting process, often remarkably candid about why the seat is empty.

Scene 06 · The ladder

Nobody skips six steps and gets a referral.

Each rung has an ask that fits it. Asking for rung five from rung zero is the single most common failure.

These are both called “referrals.”
They are not the same thing.

A referral

I submitted Paddy because he asked me to.

Moves a résumé into a better queue. The employee has staked nothing, and the recruiter can tell.

Advocacy

I spoke with Paddy. He solved almost exactly the problem Marcus's team is dealing with. Marcus should meet him.

Moves a human. The employee has attached their judgement to yours — which is the thing that cannot be requested, only earned.

A referral gets your résumé moved. Advocacy moves a person.

Why asking strangers fails — and what to do instead

A referral is an employee lending you their credibility. A stranger has none to lend on your behalf — no basis for the claim. Asking anyway is asking them to be dishonest in writing, inside their employer's systems. Most people intuit this and simply don't reply, which is why the tactic produces silence rather than refusal.

Practice So the objective of a referral conversation is not to obtain a submission. It is to give the person one specific, repeatable thing about you — a problem you solved that they recognise. That sentence is what they'll repeat internally. If they can't repeat anything, you have a rung-five referral no matter how willing they are.

Evidence Referrals aren't magic, they're a selection effect you can deliberately stand inside. Research across nine large firms found referred candidates were more likely to be hired and to accept, substantially less likely to quit, and more profitable per worker — mostly because referrals let firms match people to jobs better, not because referred people were more skilled. [11]

Scene 07 · The message

The recipient has read forty of these this month.

Yours has about six seconds to be different from the other thirty-nine.

What almost everyone sendsDelete in 4.2 seconds

Hi, hope you are doing well. I came across your profile and would love to connect and explore opportunities at your esteemed organisation.

Contains: no role · no evidence · no question · no reason it was sent to you

Context+Relevance+Evidence+A small ask
The same length. Completely different object.To a hiring manager
Context Hi Marcus — your team appears to be hiring for REQ-12345.
Evidence I spent the last three years on payment-data pipelines, and recently cut reconciliation failures by 28%.
Action I applied this morning.
Small ask You may not own the role — if you don't, could you point me toward whoever does?

The goal is almost never “can you get me a job.” It's “am I knocking on the right door?”

That question is answerable in ninety seconds, costs nothing, commits no reputation — and is useful to you whichever way it's answered. It also gets longer replies than the big ask, because people enjoy being knowledgeable far more than they enjoy being solicited.

Practice Keep every one under about 120 words. Length signals that you didn't decide what mattered.

Show the other five — recruiter, employee, alumnus, former colleague, contract recruiter

To a corporate recruiter

Hi Dana — I applied to REQ-12345 this morning. Four years on payments data infrastructure, most recently fraud-scoring pipelines at about 4 billion events a day. Two quick questions if you own this one: is it still active, and is it scoped closer to platform work or analytics enablement? Happy to be redirected if something else on your team fits better.

To an employee on the team

Hi Priya — I'm looking at the data engineer opening on payments and trying to understand the work before I invest in it properly. Ten minutes? I'm mainly curious whether the hard part is throughput or correctness — I've spent four years on payments pipelines and those lead to quite different jobs. No referral request. The substantive question is what eventually earns one.

To an alumnus

Hi Sanjay — same programme at Illinois, some years apart. I'm a data engineer looking at payments-infrastructure teams and you've been inside one through a full build-out. Fifteen minutes to tell me which teams here are genuinely growing versus consolidating? I'm trying to spend my effort in the right places.

To a former colleague

Ravi — long time. I'm back on the market as of last month; the platform group got restructured. You're at Stripe now and I'm looking at payments data roles. Not asking you to refer me — just whether your side is actually hiring, and whether there's anyone worth talking to. Also genuinely want to hear how it's going. Taking the referral off the table in message one is what makes the reply easy.

To a stranger, with no warrant at all

Hi Tom — no connection between us, so I'll be brief. I read your write-up on migrating off the legacy ledger. I've done that migration twice and the reconciliation phase nearly killed us both times. If you're still in it and a comparison would help, glad to share what we got wrong. If not, no reply needed. No ask whatsoever. This is an investment in becoming a familiar name.

To a staffing recruiter

Hi Kevin — data engineer, four years in payments, Spark/Flink/dbt, available from 1 November. Targeting $95–105/hr W-2, open on rate for the right engagement length. Happy to send my résumé. Two things before any submission: which client, and are you on their approved vendor list? I keep a log to avoid duplicate submissions. Completely different register — four facts and two questions that signal experience.

And accept the base rate

Most messages will not be answered. A 20% response rate on careful outreach is good. That isn't a verdict on you; it's everyone's inbox. Send five thoughtful messages rather than fifty identical ones, and follow up once.

Scene 08 · The résumé

Tailor the argument. Don't rewrite history.

A résumé isn't an autobiography. It's an argument for fit, read in about eight seconds by someone looking for a reason to stop.

Generic

“Responsible for data pipelines and analytics.”

Describes what the job was called. The reader already knew that from your title.

Problem → action → outcome

“Nightly reporting failed twice a week on late-arriving data; introduced schema contracts and staged loads, cutting failure alerts 70% and the run from 6 hours to 40 minutes.”

The problem clause is what makes the outcome legible — and it's the part interviewers ask about.

Contested You've heard that “75% of résumés are rejected by ATS before a human sees them.” No credible source exists. Tracking systems are mostly databases with search over them — they rarely auto-reject.

But the practical conclusion is nearly identical for a more interesting reason. Your résumé isn't discarded by a machine; it is never retrieved, because a recruiter with 244 applications searches the pile instead of reading it. So keywords matter — not to satisfy a robot, but to appear in a human's search. Use the words that actually appear in six real postings for your target role, in the sentences where you genuinely did the thing.

Practice And the governing test for anything AI helped you write: can you defend every line, out loud, for ten minutes, to someone who does this for a living?

If Gen AI gives you experience you never had, it hasn't improved your résumé. It has written fan fiction.

Cover letter?

Worth writing

  • Career change — your résumé argues against you
  • An unusual background needing one sentence of context
  • Genuine, evidenced company-specific motivation
  • Mission-driven organisations, internships, small companies
  • They explicitly asked for one

Wasted motion

  • “I am excited to apply for this opportunity…”
  • Anything you'd send with the company name swapped
  • High-volume postings where the field is optional
  • Contract roles — the agency wants a rate, not prose

The 100-word micro-cover-letter answers three questions and nothing else. Why this problem — something specific about their work you actually read. Why me — the closest thing you've done. What evidence — one number or artifact. If you can't fill all three, you've learned something useful about whether to apply.

Don't do this — hidden text for AI screeners

Evidence Some candidates now embed invisible instructions in résumés — white or 2-point text telling an AI screener to rank them highly. It's measurable: research led by Duke found at least 1% of résumés submitted to a major hiring platform contained hidden instructions. [12]

Three reasons not to, any one decisive. It's fraud. Platforms are building detection, and it's visible to anyone who presses Ctrl-A. And when it's caught you aren't merely rejected — you're documented, permanently, in an applicant tracking system as someone who tried to manipulate the process.

Scene 09 · Sequencing

Apply first, or network first?

It looks like a strategic dilemma. It's two variables: how fast the thing is moving, and how strong your warmest connection is.

FastSlow

Fast · weak connection

Apply today. Outreach the same afternoon.

Early applications get read in a less exhausted state, and there's no relationship to leverage yet. Applying costs ten minutes and keeps the option alive.

Fast · strong connection

Talk to the human first — within 24 hours.

Ask them directly: “is it better if you refer me before I apply?” They'll know. Some employers process referrals differently from cold applications.

Slow · weak connection

Validate, build context, then apply.

An old posting deserves one question before an hour of tailoring. Spend the time finding a human instead — the posting isn't going anywhere.

Slow · strong connection

Use the relationship. Let them tell you when.

The dream company with no opening lives here. There's nothing to apply to, so the whole game is being the known quantity when something opens. Months, not days.

Weak connectionStrong connection

Networking and applying were never competing strategies. Doing both costs fifteen extra minutes.

Scene 10 · The black hole

Silence at day nine is not a rejection.

Median time to fill was 39 days in 2026. Evidence [14] Most candidates stop following up about a week before the process reaches them.

Persistent

  • “Thought you might find this relevant —”
  • A finished project or certification
  • A reaction to something the company just announced
  • A mutual connection you've since discovered
  • A clarified availability date

Every one gives the recipient something to act on.

Annoying

  • “Following up.”
  • “Following up again.”
  • “Just bubbling this up.”
  • “Circling back.”
  • “Bumping.”
  • “Hello???”

Every one means “please feel guilty.” None of them work.

Closing the loop gracefully

When it ends — a rejection, or silence that has clearly become one — send one short message. Almost nobody does, which is exactly why it works.

“Thanks for letting me know, and no hard feelings. I'd still like to be considered for payments-data roles on your team in future — if something opens I'd welcome hearing about it. Either way, good luck with the build-out.”

The recruiter who rejected you today is filling three other roles this quarter and will change employers within two years. A gracious close puts you in a small, memorable category. A bitter reply also makes you memorable, in the way that gets a note added to your record.

The full field list, and the five numbers to count weekly

Fields: company · role · job ID · date discovered · source · posting age · proof-of-life verdict · hiring manager (and whether inferred or confirmed) · recruiter (and whether verified) · internal contacts · connection strength as a ladder rung · application date · résumé version · referral status · last contact · next action · follow-up date · response · outcome.

Two of those carry more weight than the rest. Résumé version, so you know what they're looking at when they call. Follow-up date — sort by it every morning, and that one column replaces remembering.

The weekly ten minutes

Count: targets added · validated · humans actually contacted · replies · conversations held. If “humans contacted” is zero and “applications sent” is twenty-two, you have diagnosed your own week without needing anybody's benchmark.

Stages: Target → Validate → Network → Referred → Applied → Human contact → Interview. This guide ends at human contact. What happens after interview is a different craft.

Scene 13 · Pick your situation

Eight situations. One wrong move each.

The wrong move is always the one that feels most natural.

New graduate, almost no professional network

Wrong move
Decide networking is for people who already have connections, and settle in for a long campaign of applications. Or fire off 200 connection requests with no note, producing a great many strangers and zero relationships.
Smart move
You have more network than you think, and it's institutional: every graduate of your programme, every professor, every manager from any job including retail. Work it systematically — five messages a week, each asking one specific question, each ending with “is there anyone else you'd suggest I talk to?” That last question compounds.
Do this today
Open your school's alumni directory, filter to your target industry, send three messages that each ask one real question.

Switching from UX design into product management

Wrong move
Apply to PM roles with a UX résumé and hope somebody sees the potential. A recruiter screening against a PM specification sees a designer, and the burden of the transition lands on them rather than on you.
Smart move
Do PM work where you already are, visibly: own a roadmap slice, run the prioritisation, write the spec, present the metrics. Then it's experience, not aspiration. Internal transfers are much the easiest path — talk to a PM lead at your own company first. This is also the clearest case for a cover letter, since your résumé argues against you.
Do this today
Ask a PM at your current company what they'd need to see to consider you credible for the role.

Mid-career, targeting one large technology company

Wrong move
Apply to eleven similar-sounding openings across the company in one afternoon. It's visible internally, it signals you haven't worked out what you want, and it usually ends with one recruiter deciding on your behalf which of the eleven you are for.
Smart move
Pick two. Large employers post many near-identical requisitions belonging to genuinely different teams — and the team is the job. Read the engineering blog, find which team owns the problem you're actually good at, and target that team. Then find people who moved there from your current employer.
Do this today
Read two of their engineering posts and write down the one problem you could speak to credibly. That sentence is your outreach message.

Senior professional, 50-plus, network gone stale

Wrong move
Apply through portals — the channel where age is screened most efficiently and twenty-five years reads as a cost. Or hide the experience, producing a résumé that's both weaker and dishonest.
Smart move
Name the reality: Evidence 64% of workers 50-plus report seeing or experiencing age discrimination, and 14% say it cost them a specific job. [13] Which is precisely why the portal is the wrong channel. Bias works most efficiently on anonymous applications and least on warm referrals. You know hundreds of people who have seen you work; most haven't heard from you in years. Reactivate one a week. Lead with scope, not duration.
Do this today
List ten people who have directly seen your work. Message one — about them, not about you.

Laid off last week, needs income

Wrong move
Apply to 200 jobs in ten days out of panic. It feels like the only responsive action available and produces the worst conversion of any strategy, at exactly the moment you can least afford it.
Smart move
Run two tracks with different clocks. Income: contract work moves in days — contact every former manager and every recruiter who has placed you, and say plainly that you're available now. A former manager with a three-month problem doesn't need to interview you. The right role: run the targeted process at 20 opportunities, not 200. Don't let track one's urgency wreck track two.
Do this today
Message your three most recent managers: available now, here's what I'm looking for, do you know of anything short or long.

Contractor whose engagement ends in six weeks

Wrong move
Wait until week five, then cold-call agencies. You'll compete on availability against people who've been in those pipelines for months, and accept a worse rate because you have no runway.
Smart move
Start now — six weeks is early in contract terms. Message every recruiter who has placed you with a precise availability date; precision is the product here. Tell your current client's manager too, since extensions are the cheapest next engagement. And keep the submission log rigorously: two agencies submitting you for the same requisition can eliminate you outright.
Do this today
Two lines to your five best recruiters: available from [exact date], here's what I just finished, here's my rate range.

A six-week-old posting that feels off

Wrong move
Either spend 45 minutes on a bespoke application, or dismiss it as a ghost job. Both are guesses, and one of them is expensive.
Smart move
Score it, then resolve it with one question. Six weeks isn't damning on its own — senior and specialist roles routinely take longer. What matters is the combination: on the careers site, reposted or not, hiring elsewhere, a human attached, any sign of a freeze. If it lands yellow, spend ten minutes rather than forty-five.
Do this today
Send exactly one message: “Is REQ-12345 still active, and is the team still growing?” Recruiters answer this readily — it saves them work too.

The perfect role, and no recruiter named anywhere

Wrong move
Apply through the portal and hope. Or message the chief executive, on the theory that senior people make things happen — which they do, and what they'll make happen is your message travelling downward with a note attached.
Smart move
Infer rather than escalate. Decompose the title into function and domain, generate the plausible manager titles, and search for those. Then search the company plus “#hiring” plus the department — managers frequently post their own openings, and replying to that post is warmer than any cold message. Failing that, ask a teammate who they report to. It's an entirely normal question.
Do this today
Run the two searches from Scene 04. Ten minutes, usually decisive.

The whole thing

  1. Job boards show openings.
  2. Networks reveal context.
  3. Evidence creates credibility.
  4. Reputation creates trust.
  5. Follow-up creates memory.

The goal isn't to know 10,000 people.The goal is for the right 10 people to know what you can do.
