Stop Maximizing Attention. Start Maximizing Opportunity.

Professional networks became social networks with a dress code. Here is what one looks like when you rebuild it from first principles around outcomes — and a prototype you can click through.

PaddySpeaks 14 min read Design spec + live prototype
THE HIRING INTENT STATE. SWITCHING THE CHIP IN THE TOP NAVIGATION REASSEMBLES THE DASHBOARD, THE RANKING AND BOTH RAILS.
THE ENTIRE ARGUMENT: THE INPUTS ARE ALREADY THERE. ONLY THE OBJECTIVE FUNCTION CHANGES.

Open a professional network on a Tuesday morning. Here is what greets you, in roughly this order: a post that consists of one sentence and a line break every four words. A photograph of a marathon medal, professionally captioned as a lesson about resilience in enterprise sales. Four people you have never met, recommended because they also once worked at a company where you worked. A notification that twelve people viewed your profile, and an invitation to pay to find out who.

Somewhere behind all of that sits the thing you actually opened the app for: a role you would be good at, a person who is hiring for it, or an answer to a technical question you have been circling for a week.

This is not a failure of execution. It is a faithful implementation of the wrong objective. Somebody wrote down time spent and sessions per week, and a very competent engineering organisation optimised for them for fifteen years. The feed is not broken. The feed is winning.

The mechanics that maximise time spent are precisely the mechanics that get in the way of finding a job.

So I built the alternative, rather than writing another complaint about it. CareerOS is a working interactive prototype of a professional network with one substitution at the centre of it: replace the engagement objective with an outcome objective, then follow that change honestly into every screen, every card, every notification and every default. This piece is the design spec. The prototype is linked at the bottom, and again here for the impatient: open the CareerOS prototype.

One clarification before the argument. This is an independent concept, inspired by problems any user of any professional network will recognise. It uses no branding, logo or visual asset belonging to an existing platform, and it is not affiliated with one. Every person, company, role, recruiter and metric inside it is invented.

01 The one sentence the product is built on

Everything downstream is a consequence, not a separate decision.

A product with a clear thesis is easier to design than a product with a feature list, because the thesis settles arguments. When someone proposes a streak counter, you do not need a debate. You need one sentence.

Stop maximizing attention. Start maximizing opportunity.

In the prototype that line appears exactly once, on a product-philosophy screen. This is deliberate. A principle repeated on every page is a slogan; a principle applied on every page is a product. If the sentence is doing its job, you should be able to feel it in the interface without ever reading it.

From it follows one operating rule, which the prototype applies without exception:

The relevance test

Every post, person, role, event and notification must be able to answer: why is this useful to me professionally? If the system cannot answer, the item does not appear. Not lower down. Not later. It does not appear.

That rule has a consequence most products would consider a defect: the feed ends. In the prototype, the Knowledge Feed shows eight items and then stops, with a note saying eight of two hundred and forty candidates qualified. There is no infinite scroll, because there is no more useful material, and manufacturing some would be a lie told in the shape of a card.

What it optimises for

  • Successful hires
  • Relevant recruiter–candidate matches
  • Meaningful professional relationships
  • Career progression
  • Mentorship
  • Knowledge exchange
  • Skill growth
  • Trust and professional reputation

What it refuses to optimise for

  • Endless scrolling
  • Likes
  • Viral reach
  • Empty engagement
  • Time spent
  • Random profile views
  • Generic popularity

02 Intent is the primary input, not the profile

Job hunting and hiring are different jobs. One feed serves neither.

Existing networks model you as a static profile — a title, a company, a history — and then serve everyone the same surface. But the same person, in the same job, wants completely different things in different months. In March they are hiring. In September they are quietly looking. In between they are trying to understand a technology they will have to defend a decision about.

So the prototype puts a control in the top navigation reading Today I am, with seven states: job hunting, hiring, learning, networking, mentoring, building, exploring. It defaults to job hunting, and changing it is not cosmetic. Switch to Hiring and the summary cards change from strong role matches and skill gaps to high-potential candidates and hiring bottlenecks; the centre column swaps job cards for candidate cards; the right rail replaces recruiters-active-in-your-category with your interview pipeline. There is a second toggle inside that state, because a hiring manager and a recruiter are also doing different jobs from each other.

Under the hood

Intent is a first-class ranking parameter, persisted in local storage alongside the user's saved items, dismissals and feed preferences. In the prototype it does three things: it selects which dashboard composition renders, it re-weights the people ranker (a person relevant to your active intent is boosted above a higher raw match score), and it shifts the feed's scoring function — under job hunting, hiring-relevant posts gain fourteen points; under learning, technical depth does instead.

The design note that matters: intent is stated, not inferred. Inferring it from behaviour would be more elegant and considerably worse, because a system that guesses you are job hunting is a system that can leak that guess.

The hardest thing about the hiring dashboard was resisting the urge to make it flattering. The most useful panel in it is titled Where your hiring is actually stuck, and all three findings are about the employer: interviews taking nine days to schedule because two interviewers decline most requests, a salary band sitting below three of the five strongest candidates, and a job description demanding Kubernetes for work that is eighty per cent Spark. Most hiring tools blame the market. The bottleneck is usually on the near side of the table.

03 Evidence, not endorsement

A skill nobody can point at is a claim.

Endorsements are the weakest signal in professional software. They measure reciprocal politeness, and everyone involved knows it. So the prototype does not count them at all. Instead every skill carries three things: an evidence strength, the specific work behind it, and — this is the part that makes people uncomfortable — the gaps a hiring manager would notice.

SkillStrengthWhat backs itWhat is missing
Lakehouse architecture Strong · 90 Decision framework adopted by three teams; conference session; cited by two practitioners —
Kubernetes Medium · 54 Mentioned in two project descriptions No public leadership example; last demonstrated 18 months ago; affects 12 job matches
ML / feature infrastructure Emerging · 41 A feature-store prototype that never reached production No production serving path on record; affects 9 job matches

Skill evidence as the prototype renders it. The third column is what most profiles show. The fourth column is what actually determines the outcome.

Notice the framing of the gaps. Not a percentage — a count of specific roles. "Your Kubernetes signal is at 54%" motivates nobody. "One gap is holding twelve roles below your threshold, and you have already done the work, you just never published it" is a Tuesday evening task.

The same logic drives the profile's featured section, Evidence of Work: five claims, each with the detail that lets someone verify it and a label saying who confirmed it. Reduced pipeline cost by 34% — verified against six months of billing data by the platform finance team. A claim nobody can check is a sentence, not a credential.

04 A reputation score you can audit, and switch off

The alternative to a mysterious number is not no number.

Any reputation system is one design decision away from being dystopian, and most of the distance is covered by whether the subject can see the derivation. So the prototype shows an overall score — 782 out of 1000 — and makes it fully traceable. Eight dimensions, each expanding to reveal exactly what it was computed from.

Technical credibility · 88 · +4 in 90 days
  • 3 architecture articles with reproducible detail
  • 2 peer endorsements from engineers who reviewed the work
  • 1 conference presentation
  • 4 verified project outcomes with named metrics
  • Recent activity weighted more heavily than older work

Four safeguards do the real work here. The score is visible only to its subject unless they choose otherwise. Low-sample dimensions are labelled provisional rather than scored confidently — hiring influence: 58, low sample size, treat as provisional — because a confident number from eleven data points is a fiction. Follower count is excluded, stated explicitly in the panel's own footer. And there is a Hide button, which works, and which does not nag.

The dimension that earns the design is leadership: 61, the user's lowest. It sits directly under technical credibility at 88, and the agent explains why that gap matters: every Principal- and Director-titled role the user has looked at weights leadership evidence above technical depth. A reputation panel that only tells you what you are good at is a compliment, not an instrument.

05 The agent explains; it does not nudge

Five findings a quarter, each one an evidence chain.

"Career AI" in most products means a prompt to post more. The prototype's Career Agent is bound to a strict schema, and every finding must fill all five slots: what happened, why it matters, the evidence, one recommended action, and the expected benefit alongside the effort it costs.

A worked finding

What happened: Three hiring managers viewed your profile after searching for AI platform experience. All three left within forty seconds of reaching your skills section.

Why it matters: Your Kubernetes work is not visible. It is the first requirement listed in two of the three roles those managers are hiring for.

Evidence: Search terms recorded in aggregate, no individual identified · Kubernetes evidence strength medium, last demonstrated 18 months ago · your résumé names the operator work, your evidence section does not · twelve roles sit between 72% and 84% because of this single gap.

Action: Add Kubernetes evidence. Benefit: twelve roles cross your threshold, two of which you have already saved. Effort: about forty minutes — the material exists, it is not published.

What the schema forbids: "improve your profile", "network more", "stay active". None of these can fill the five slots, which is the point of having them.

The same explanation contract runs through the whole product, not just the agent. Every recommendation — person, role, post, event — carries a Why this? panel that names the inputs and how much each one counted, the confidence, the signals deliberately excluded, and four controls: more like this, less like this, never use this signal, report bad recommendation.

The exclusion list is rendered as struck-through text, which is a small thing that took a while to get right. It reads: ethnicity, gender, age, personal appearance, unrelated social activity, follower count, photo quality. Stating what a ranker refuses to use is a stronger commitment than describing what it does use, because it is falsifiable.

06 The uncomfortable numbers stay on screen

Recruiter transparency, competitiveness, and the internal candidate you were not told about.

This is where the thesis costs something, and where most products quietly flinch.

Recruiter trust. Every recruiter carries eight aggregated metrics: response time, candidate response rate, ghosting rate, interview transparency, offer acceptance, role accuracy, and satisfaction from both candidates and hiring managers. One of the three recruiters in the prototype has a 54% response rate and an elevated ghosting rate, and it is shown — with a confidence range, a sample size, and a note that agency recruiters often carry roles whose process they do not control. Metrics are suppressed entirely below ten responses. The purpose is calibration, so a candidate knows how much hope to invest in a conversation, and the copy works hard to stay on that side of the line rather than becoming a public rating of a person.

Application competitiveness. Job cards state your realistic odds. One reads: "Low odds, worth reading. An internal candidate is in the process. Your leadership evidence is the weakest of your dimensions and this role weights it heavily." No job board tells you this, because telling you costs them an application.

Is the role even real. Every role carries a verification date and a plain statement — "Genuine: the requisition is open, funded, and the hiring manager has replied to candidates this week." Saved roles are re-verified weekly and archived when they close, so nobody spends an evening preparing for a role that closed eleven days ago.

And the primary action on a job card is not Apply. It is View role story — why the role exists, what the first six months actually look like, what happened to the last two people in the seat, and the risk worth asking about. Where the employer has not answered those questions, the panel says so and offers to ask them, rather than inventing an answer. One-tap apply optimises application volume, which is a metric that serves the platform and nobody else in the transaction.

07 Making the visual language do the work

Calm, editorial, and mono where the machine is speaking.

The visual direction had one job: feel like a serious instrument rather than a social product. Warm bone background instead of the institutional blue-grey, white cards, deep pine as the primary, muted gold reserved exclusively for the places where the system is explaining its own reasoning. An editorial serif for headings, a clean sans for interface text, and a monospace face used strictly for labels, match scores and system explanations — so the machine's voice is typographically distinct from the human one.

#FAF7F0 background #FFFFFF surface #18140F text #1E4A3D primary #855E1E accent #8F4E2E warning #E8E1D2 border

Two accessibility decisions turned out to be design decisions in disguise. First: no meaning may be carried by colour alone, which is why every match score renders three redundant cues — the number, a proportional bar, and a word ("strong match", "partial match"). Second: the whole palette had to clear WCAG AA at 4.5:1, and two tokens initially missed by a hair against the warm background. Darkening them slightly was not a compromise on the aesthetic; the muted gold reads better against bone at #855E1E than it did at #AD7C2B.

Build notes

No build step, no framework, no runtime CDN. Thirty-five ES modules — nine views, twenty component modules, five mock-data modules, a state store and a shell — assembled with a small escaping template helper. Interaction is expressed declaratively: components emit data-action attributes, and one delegated listener dispatches them, which keeps every state change auditable from a single file.

Intent, saved items, dismissals, feed preferences and read notifications persist in localStorage. There is no backend, no analytics and no network request after the fonts load. Twenty-eight scripted interaction checks pass against it — intent switching, filtering, disclosure panels, persistence across reload, keyboard access, mobile navigation — with no console errors.

08 How you would know it worked

A different objective requires a different scorecard.

A product with this thesis cannot be governed by the metrics it was built to reject. That sounds obvious and is the single most likely place for the whole idea to quietly die, eighteen months in, when someone builds a growth dashboard out of habit.

Success metrics

  • Placements per active job seeker, and time to placement
  • Share of first messages receiving a substantive reply
  • Candidate-reported quality of the hiring processes the product routed them into
  • Mentor relationships both sides confirm six months later
  • Market-wide recruiter ghosting rate, trending down

Explicitly not success metrics

  • Daily active users
  • Session length
  • Posts per user
  • Feed impressions
  • Connection count

The honest tension in this design is a business one, and it is worth naming rather than hiding. A product that works this way is used less. The user gets what they came for and leaves. That is fatal under advertising and fine under subscription, employer fees tied to actual hires, or anything else that gets paid when the outcome happens rather than when the session lengthens. You cannot adopt the thesis and keep the business model, and pretending otherwise is how the thesis gets eroded one quarter at a time.

The inputs already exist. Every platform already knows who is hiring, who published what, whose demonstrated stack matches which requirement, and which recruiters never reply. None of that is a modelling breakthrough. It is a choice about what to rank.
