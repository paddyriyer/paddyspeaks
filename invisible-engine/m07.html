Module 7 · When the person located is not the owner

Privacy, threat modelling and survivor safety

Strong encryption keeps a location secret from strangers. It cannot ask the person carrying a tag whether they agreed. This module separates the questions, shows where alerts help and where they stop, and ends every path for a person at risk at help, not at a setting.

Scenes 50–55Prerequisites: Module 5 (how a sighting reaches its owner) and Module 6 (keys, accounts and who can see what). If you are reading this because you think you are being tracked, start with Scene 53 and the essay’s safety section: From Tap to Trace, Part XI.

How to read every diagram

  • Orange arrow — energy or power
  • Blue arrow — radio signal or physical wave
  • Green arrow — plaintext or local information
  • Purple arrow — encrypted or cryptographic
  • Grey dashed arrow — registration or authorisation, never data
  • Red dashed outline — attacker zone or hazard
  • Thick dark box — trust, hardware or organisational boundary
  • Dashed blue rings — illustrative field drawings, not visible waves
Scene 50

Where the system can fail people

A finding network has more than one way to go wrong, and only some of them are about secrecy. This scene names them in four groups and then separates four questions people often blur into one.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. A tag helps people find lost things.
  2. It whispers, and a stranger’s phone passes a sealed note on.
  3. Only the owner can open the note.
  4. But someone could hide a tag in another person’s bag.
  5. A secret note can still be unfair if nobody asked.

Keeping a message secret is not the same as asking people if they agree.

B · For a curious adult

Engineers sort what can go wrong by what it attacks. Confidentiality asks who can read a report. Integrity asks whether it was changed. Authenticity asks whether it really came from a tag and a genuine finder. Encryption mostly answers the first. The fourth question is different in kind: consent — did the person being located agree?

Where the analogy breaks: “threats” sounds like outside hackers. Several of the most serious ones here come from inside the system’s normal use: an owner who reads exactly what the design allows, about a person who never agreed to carry the tag.

C · For an engineering student

  • Find My reports are end-to-end encrypted: Apple says only the owner can see the location, and neither Apple nor the relaying devices learn it. documented
  • The broadcast key changes about every 15 minutes, which limits passive following of one fixed signal. documented
  • Spoofed or replayed reports and correlation by radio fingerprint or movement are threat classes studied in independent research on crowd-sourced finding networks. research This lesson names them and does not describe how to carry them out.
  • Metadata (lookup values, upload times, account queries) remains visible to the service even when content is sealed (Module 5, Scene 36). conceptual

D · For a security architect

Draw the trust boundaries first, then place threats on them: the radio is public; the relay is trusted for availability and metadata but not for content; the owner’s account is trusted for everything. That makes account compromise and authorised misuse the threats with the widest blast radius — both pass every cryptographic check. Consent must be handled by product controls (alerts, sound, sharing reviews), not by the cipher.

E · For a CISO

  • Keep four separate rows in the risk register: confidentiality, integrity, authenticity and consent. A vendor answer to the first three is not an answer to the fourth.
  • For company-owned trackers, the realistic top risks are account takeover and misuse by someone with legitimate access, not radio interception.

Consent is not confidentiality

A lost bagThe account holder put the tag in their own bag and lost it at a station. The location shown is the bag’s. Nobody else is being located. Strong encryption protects the owner from the relay and from anyone on the network.

Strong encryption keeps the relay and the network out, and in doing so keeps the misuse private too. The account holder sees the same locations at every setting. Encryption cannot tell a lost bag from a person being followed.

  • No encryption. A teaching extreme: reports travel and are stored readable.
  • Encrypted in transit only. Protected on the wire (TLS), readable by the service that stores it.
  • End-to-end encrypted. Only the owner’s devices hold the key that opens a report (the design Apple, Google and Samsung document).
  • End-to-end, post-quantum ready. The same, with key exchange designed to resist a future quantum computer (Modules 8–10).

What protects the person carrying it

  • An alert on the carrier’s own phone when an unknown tracker, separated from its owner, seems to travel with them (iPhone and Android, through a shared specification).
  • A sound from a separated tag when it is moved.
  • A tap with any NFC phone, which shows the tag’s serial number and part of the owner’s details for a tag found moving with you.
  • Platform safety tools such as Apple’s Safety Check and its Personal Safety User Guide, and Google’s acceptable use policy for Find Hub.
  • People: someone you trust, local support services, and the police where appropriate.

None of these depend on the encryption setting. They protect consent, which encryption does not.

If you think a tracker is being used to follow you, consider your safety first: disabling it can alert the person who placed it. Apple’s and Google’s safety guides describe the options.

Scene 51

Two good goals that pull against each other

Rotating identifiers protect an owner from strangers following their tag. Unwanted-tracker alerts protect the person carrying a tag by noticing that the same tag keeps showing up. Those goals pull against each other, and designers tune the balance.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. A tag changes its secret name often.
  2. That stops strangers following it.
  3. But if a tag sneaks into your bag, your phone needs to notice it.
  4. Noticing means spotting the same tag again and again.
  5. So the tag has to be a little easier to spot when it is away from home.

Hiding a tag from strangers and spotting a sneaky tag pull in opposite directions, so designers balance them carefully.

B · For a curious adult

If a tag kept one fixed name, anyone with a phone could follow its owner around. So tags change their name. But a person who unknowingly carries someone else’s tag needs their phone to say: this same tag has been with you for a while. The answer most designs use is to treat a tag differently when it is separated from its owner: that is when being noticeable matters most.

Where the analogy breaks: a balance suggests one setting fixes everything. In practice there are several dials, and each also affects false alarms, battery life and how quickly a real problem is spotted.

C · For an engineering student

  • Rotating identifiers limit passive linking (Apple: the broadcast key changes about every 15 minutes). documented
  • Alerts depend on the tracker being separated from its owner and seen with you over time. documented
  • A separated AirTag also plays a sound when it is moved. documented Exact timing is not stated here. check Apple’s current page
  • The four dials are an illustrative way to describe the design space, not a published parameter list. illustrative

D · For a security architect

State the protection goals explicitly and test both: (1) an observer who sees the tag at different places cannot link the sightings while the tag is near its owner; (2) a person carrying a separated tag is told within a bounded, tested time. The second goal is where the user’s safety lives, so measure it on real routes and phones rather than assuming it.

E · For a CISO

  • When choosing trackers for staff or assets, ask the vendor how its product behaves when separated, how it is detected on other platforms, and how that has been tested.
  • Never treat “privacy” as only the owner’s privacy: the person carrying a tag is a data subject too.

Scene 52

Alerts across iPhone and Android

For years, detection depended on which phone you carried. In May 2024 Apple and Google shipped alerts built on one industry specification. The standard behind it is still being written, and alerts have limits.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Some phones are iPhones and some are Android phones.
  2. Now both kinds can warn you about a tag that is not yours.
  3. The warning says a tag seems to be travelling with you.
  4. Sometimes a phone may not notice.
  5. If you are worried, tell a grown-up you trust.

Both kinds of phone can now warn about a strange tag, but a warning can be late or missing, so worries should go to a trusted grown-up.

B · For a curious adult

Apple and Google wrote one industry specification, Detecting Unwanted Location Trackers. Since May 2024, iPhones (iOS 17.5 and later) and Android phones (Android 6.0 and later) can alert about trackers from either network and from makers who adopt it. Android can also scan for trackers nearby.

Where the analogy breaks: an alert is not a smoke alarm with a tested response time. It depends on the tag being separated from its owner, on enough time together, on settings and software, and on the tag following the specification.

C · For an engineering student

  • Cross-platform alerts: iOS 17.5+ and Android 6.0+, announced May 2024. documented
  • IETF DULT working group: drafts for accessory behaviour and detection. standards work check current document status
  • Google: a manual scan shows a tracker near you now, which may not have travelled with you; only trackers separated from their owners appear. documented
  • No platform publishes a guaranteed time-to-alert. conceptual

D · For a security architect

Model detection as a probabilistic control with false negatives you can name: not-separated state, short co-travel, radio or OS conditions, non-compliant hardware. Treat the specification as raising the floor across platforms, not as closing the risk. Compensating controls belong in the human process (Scene 53), not in more scanning.

E · For a CISO

  • Do not let device baselines turn off Bluetooth or the alerts on managed phones without weighing staff safety.
  • Ask tracker suppliers whether their products implement the cross-platform specification and how they were tested.

Scene 53

Three people, three safe paths

What to do depends on who you are and what you are facing. These three short paths are about safety and getting help. None of them asks anyone to confront, search or bypass anything.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. If your phone says a strange tag is with you, stay calm.
  2. Go somewhere you feel safe.
  3. Tell a grown-up you trust.
  4. Do not try to sort it out alone.
  5. Grown-ups can get help from people whose job is to keep you safe.

A strange-tag warning is a reason to get a trusted grown-up, who can find the right help.

B · For a curious adult

Every path here ends with other people, because the safest next step depends on things a phone cannot know. A tag in a shared family car is probably innocent; a tag after a relationship has ended may not be. If placement might be deliberate, the timing of what you do matters.

Where the analogy breaks: a decision tree looks complete. Real situations are not; these paths are a starting point for a conversation with someone who can help, not a substitute for it.

C · For an engineering student

  • Use the alert’s own tools: play a sound, and on supported phones find it more precisely; on Android, the alert and Scan now can help. documented
  • Apple’s guide says to consider your safety before disabling a tracker, because the owner then stops getting its location. documented
  • Google’s Find Hub acceptable use policy forbids tracking people without their knowledge and consent. documented

D · For a security architect

For enterprise programmes, design the human process, not just the tooling: a named contact in security or HR, safe options for workplace, travel and parking, legal advice before law enforcement where policy requires it, and a rule that nobody searches colleagues’ belongings on someone’s behalf.

E · For a CISO

  • Publish one internal page: “If you get an unknown-tracker alert”. Put safety and support first and the security team’s contact second.
  • Train managers not to investigate or confront; their job is to connect the person to help.
Engineering notes, accuracy check and sources

Engineering notes

The three people are illustrative and generic. Support options are named by kind because services differ by country; the essay lists a US hotline as an example.

  • Is level A gentle and protective? (Yes: tell a grown-up you trust.)

Scene 54

What can be kept, and what it can and cannot show

If it is safe to do so, a few things are worth keeping. They help an advocate, an employer or the police act. They rarely prove who placed a tag.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. If something worries you, a grown-up can help keep notes.
  2. They can take a picture of the phone’s warning.
  3. They can write down when and where it happened.
  4. Then they can ask people whose job is to help.

Writing down what happened helps the people whose job is to help you.

B · For a curious adult

Evidence here is mostly records you already have: the alert, the times and places, the tag’s serial number. The vendor holds the link from a tag to an account and can provide it to police through a valid request. Collect only what you can collect safely; do not put yourself at risk to get more.

Where the analogy breaks: this is not a crime-scene kit. The records point to an account, and the place in a sighting is where a stranger’s phone was. Who placed a tag, and why, is a question for people with legal authority.

C · For an engineering student

  • Tapping a found AirTag with an NFC-capable phone opens a web page with its serial number; for an AirTag found moving with you, Apple shows part of the owner’s phone number. documented check Apple’s current page
  • Apple says it can provide paired-account details in response to a valid request from law enforcement. documented
  • Safety Check on iPhone reviews and stops sharing in one place. documented check current version
  • Apple’s Find My privacy notice says a located device’s location is kept for up to 24 hours to provide the service. documented Retention elsewhere differs; ask early, through proper channels.

D · For a security architect

Preservation is time-sensitive and person-centred: the person’s own screenshots and notes are durable; service-side data has retention limits and needs legal process. Advise a preservation request through counsel or police rather than self-help. Never route evidence-gathering through the person’s own account in a way that could alert the other party.

E · For a CISO

  • Agree in advance with legal how the company supports an employee’s report: what you preserve, who requests vendor records, and what you never do (search, surveil, confront).
  • Record decisions and timestamps; they matter more than device forensics in most cases.

Scene 55

Proportionate protection: who can do what

Most protections have an owner: a person, a household, an employer, a vendor or a standards body. Putting each control in the right column avoids two failures — nobody doing it, or someone doing too much.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Keeping people safe is a team job.
  2. You can ask before sharing where you are.
  3. Families can check who sees what.
  4. Companies that make tags build in warnings.
  5. Everyone does their part.

Each person and company has its own job in keeping people safe, and nobody should spy on the people they protect.

B · For a curious adult

A good protection is proportionate: it fits the risk and respects the person it protects. Reviewing who can see your location once a year is proportionate. Secretly tracking a family member “for their safety” is not — it creates the very harm this module is about.

Where the analogy breaks: a table makes responsibility look tidy. In a household or a workplace the columns overlap, and the person at risk should always be part of the decision.

C · For an engineering student

  • Safety Check (iPhone) reviews and resets sharing and access. documented check current version
  • Anti-abuse controls: unwanted-tracking alerts, a sound when a separated tag moves, serial number and NFC information, one account per tag. documented
  • Cross-platform alerts via a shared specification; IETF DULT drafts. documented check status
  • The who-columns are an illustrative allocation. illustrative

D · For a security architect

Map each control to an owner and an evidence source: who implements it, and how you would know it is working. Controls without an owner decay; controls owned by the wrong party (an employer “protecting” staff by tracking them) become the threat.

E · For a CISO

  • Write a short tracker policy: company devices on company accounts; tags locate assets, never people; alerts left on; a named route for reports.
  • Review it with HR and legal once a year, and after any incident.
Engineering notes, accuracy check and sources

Engineering notes

Filled dot: implements. Ring: supports. Dash: not usually that party’s job. The allocation is ours, for teaching.
