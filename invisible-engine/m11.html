Module 11 · Governing an endpoint you may not own

CISO operating model

No company owns the finder networks, yet trackers walk into every office in visitors’ bags, ride in fleet cars and follow executives on trips. This module draws where a company’s authority stops, names threats honestly, writes a risk register with its reasons showing, and builds a safety policy that protects people without watching them.

Scenes 79–84Prerequisites: Module 6 (accounts and who can see what) and Module 7 (threats and survivor safety). The essay version, with full tables, is From Tap to Trace, Part X.

How to read every diagram

  • Orange arrow — energy or power
  • Blue arrow — radio signal or physical wave
  • Green arrow — plaintext or local information
  • Purple arrow — encrypted or cryptographic
  • Grey dashed arrow — registration or authorisation, never data
  • Red dashed outline — attacker zone or hazard
  • Thick dark box — trust, hardware or organisational boundary
  • Dashed blue rings — illustrative field drawings, not visible waves
Scene 79

Where the company’s authority stops

Before any control, a map: which things the company owns and manages, which belong to people, which belong to vendors, and which belong to nobody at all.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. A school owns its building, its laptops and its accounts.
  2. Pupils, parents and visitors bring their own phones and bags.
  3. The school cannot open someone else’s bag or phone.
  4. It can set rules for its own things and help people who ask.

A school keeps people safe by looking after its own things well and helping people, not by searching everyone.

B · For a curious adult

Most of what a company can do about trackers is about its own things: the tags it buys, the accounts it registers them to, the phones it manages and the rules it sets for visitors. The finder networks belong to Apple, Google, Samsung and Life360; a visitor’s tag belongs to the visitor; the air belongs to nobody.

Where the analogy breaks: the lines blur. A personal phone may carry a work profile; a company tag may have been bought by a team and registered to someone’s personal account (Scene 81). The map is drawn to find those blurred places, not to pretend they do not exist.

C · For an engineering student

  • Mobile device management can enforce settings on managed devices; it cannot see or control personal Find My, Find Hub or SmartThings sharing. conceptual
  • Company tags should be registered to company-controlled accounts; a tag is linked to one account at a time in the documented ecosystems (Module 5, Scene 31). documented
  • The enterprise reaches vendor networks only through procurement requirements and lawful requests, both governance edges, never data flows. conceptual

D · For a security architect

Draw the trust zones first: enterprise (owned and managed), personal, and vendor ecosystem. Any control proposal that crosses into the personal zone without the person’s consent, or claims to see inside the vendor zone, is wrong before it is evaluated.

E · For a CISO

  • Owners: physical security (premises and visitors), IT asset management (company tags), identity (managed accounts), HR with legal (people).
  • Decide in writing what the company will not collect when it protects people.
  • Put the four authority bands in the policy so nobody promises what the company cannot do.
Engineering notes, accuracy check and sources

Engineering notes

People, devices and clouds are generic and fictional. The vendor zone is drawn outside the enterprise boundary with no data edge into it, as in From Tap to Trace, Figure 16.

Accuracy check (red team)

  • Does the map give the company authority over personal phones or private trackers? (No: advise and support only.)
  • Does it show any data flowing from vendor networks into the company? (No.)

Sources

  • From Tap to Trace, Part X (“The CISO’s view of the invisible endpoint”: attack surface, control matrix, risk register and roadmap)
  • NIST, Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 (2024) — nist.gov/cyberframework.

Scene 80

Two lenses: security threats and privacy threats

A tracker ecosystem’s worst harms are often privacy harms that break no security rule. So use two lenses together, and say how strong the evidence is instead of colouring a heatmap.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Grown-ups ask: could someone pretend, break or sneak in?
  2. They also ask: could someone find out where a person goes?
  3. They write down each worry next to the thing it is about.
  4. They say how sure they are, instead of guessing a number.

Asking both “is it safe?” and “is it private?” finds problems that one question alone would miss.

B · For a curious adult

STRIDE asks how a system could be attacked; LINDDUN asks how it could expose people. A tag hidden in someone’s bag breaks no cipher and spoofs nothing, yet it identifies a person who is unaware: a privacy threat that a security lens alone would not list.

Where the analogy breaks: the categories overlap and argue with each other; LINDDUN even lists non-repudiation, which security usually wants, as a privacy threat. The value is in the conversation the lenses force, not in filling every cell.

C · For an engineering student

  • STRIDE: spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, elevation of privilege. framework
  • LINDDUN: linking, identifying, non-repudiation, detecting, data disclosure, unawareness (and unintervenability), non-compliance. framework
  • Evidence labels used here: documented (vendor or long-standing public record), research (independent, published), long-standing (weakness known for many years). method
  • No numeric risk scores: likelihood without local data is a guess, and a colour per cell would present it as a measurement.

D · For a security architect

Threat-model each surface with both lenses and keep the evidence label beside every entry. When two surfaces share a root cause (for example, static identifiers in badges and in card numbers), fix the cause once.

E · For a CISO

  • Ask for the evidence label on every risk presented to you; reject heatmaps that cannot show their data.
  • Owner: security architecture, with privacy counsel for the LINDDUN half.
Engineering notes, accuracy check and sources

Engineering notes

The table is a teaching selection of five surfaces, not a complete model. Fuller STRIDE and LINDDUN tables are in From Tap to Trace, Appendix B.

Accuracy check (red team)

  • Does the scene give numeric scores or a heatmap? (No.)
  • Is a privacy-only harm (a hidden tag identifying a person) included? (Yes, under LINDDUN.)
  • Are research findings labelled as research, not as documented vendor behaviour? (Yes.)

Sources

  • Microsoft, “Microsoft Threat Modeling Tool threats” (STRIDE) — learn.microsoft.com.
  • LINDDUN privacy threat modelling framework, DistriNet, KU Leuven — linddun.org.
  • From Tap to Trace, Part X (“The CISO’s view of the invisible endpoint”: attack surface, control matrix, risk register and roadmap)

Scene 81

A risk register with its reasons showing

A risk register is useful when every field answers a question someone will ask later: who owns this, why we think it is likely, and what is left after the controls.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. A grown-up writes down each worry on its own card.
  2. The card says who should help and what to do.
  3. It says why they think it could happen.
  4. It says what could still go wrong afterwards.

Writing down who helps and why means nobody forgets a worry, and nobody pretends it is gone.

B · For a curious adult

Three worked rows, each with eleven fields. The important ones are the least glamorous: the incident owner (so someone acts), the likelihood basis (a reason, not a number), the residual risk (what is left, in words) and the privacy and legal review (so a protection does not become surveillance).

Where the analogy breaks: a real register has many more rows and changes over time; these three are examples to copy the shape from. Re-rate every row for your own organisation.

C · For an engineering student

  • Fields: threat, actor, preconditions, victim and system impact, likelihood basis, controls, detection, incident owner, residual risk, evidence confidence, privacy/legal review.
  • Row 1 control rests on vendor behaviour: one account per tag; removal before transfer (Module 5). documented
  • Row 2 detection rests on platform alerts, whose limits are documented: separation from the owner, time, and a compatible phone. documented
  • Row 3: static-ID 125 kHz credentials can be read and copied; challenge–response credentials resist copying and replay, not relay. long-standing conceptual

D · For a security architect

Preconditions are where controls bite: a tag on a personal account, a bag left unattended, a static-ID badge still in service. Write them precisely and the controls follow. Keep the evidence-confidence field honest; “documented elsewhere, no local data” is a valid and useful entry.

E · For a CISO

  • Every row has a named incident owner before it is accepted.
  • Residual risk is accepted by someone with authority to accept it, in writing.
  • Rows that touch people go through privacy and legal review before controls are deployed.
Engineering notes, accuracy check and sources

Engineering notes

Rows are worked examples. From Tap to Trace, Table 10, gives a twelve-row register with illustrative H/M/L starting points for a typical office.

Accuracy check (red team)

  • Is likelihood given as a number? (No: as a basis in words.)
  • Does any control involve watching employees? (No; door-event review concerns doors, and logs are kept briefly.)
  • Does every row name an owner? (Yes.)

Sources

  • NIST, Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 (2024) — nist.gov/cyberframework.

Scene 82

Accounts first: company tags on company accounts

Zero trust starts with identity. For finder networks, that means the first question about any tag is which account owns it, and the second is who else that account lets in.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. The school’s things are signed in to the school’s account.
  2. Your own things stay on your own account.
  3. Only the people who need to can see where the school’s things are.
  4. When someone leaves, they hand back their keys.

Keeping the school’s things and your own things on separate accounts stops anyone seeing more than they should.

B · For a curious adult

Most enterprise tracker problems are account problems: a tag bought by a team and registered to someone’s personal account, a share that outlived the job, a leaver who can still see where the laptops are. Separate the accounts, share narrowly, sign in strongly, review regularly, and close everything when someone leaves.

Where the analogy breaks: what is possible depends on what each vendor offers; not every ecosystem supports managed accounts, shared groups or hardware security keys in the same way.

C · For an engineering student

  • Zero trust: no implicit trust from network location; every access decision per request, based on identity and device state. NIST SP 800-207
  • A tag is linked to one account at a time; sharing with up to five people is documented for AirTag (Module 5). documented
  • How a shared user’s devices receive access is not publicly documented (Module 6). not disclosed
  • Deprovisioning order: remove shares, transfer or remove tags (in Bluetooth range where the vendor requires it), then revoke devices and credentials. conceptual

D · For a security architect

Treat the managed account that owns company tags as a privileged identity: hardware-backed sign-in, a small set of administrators, logged changes. The asymmetry matters: anyone who controls that account can read every company tag’s location.

E · For a CISO

  • Joiner–mover–leaver: add a tracker and sharing step (HR triggers, IT executes).
  • Quarterly sharing review for company tags (IT, with team leads).
  • Never ask staff to share their personal location with the company.
Engineering notes, accuracy check and sources

Engineering notes

The “never mix” mark is a policy rule, not a technical barrier: nothing stops someone registering a company tag to a personal account except process and review.

Accuracy check (red team)

  • Does the scene ask employees to share personal locations with the company? (No: explicitly never.)
  • Does every step name who does it? (Yes.)

Sources

  • NIST SP 800-207, Zero Trust Architecture (2020) — csrc.nist.gov.
  • From Tap to Trace, Part X (“The CISO’s view of the invisible endpoint”: attack surface, control matrix, risk register and roadmap)

Scene 83

A tracker safety policy that protects people without watching them

A company can do a lot to protect people from unwanted tracking. It must not do it by tracking them.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. The school tells everyone what its trackers are for: finding its things.
  2. If someone is worried, they can tell a grown-up quietly.
  3. The grown-ups help them stay safe and get support.
  4. The school never follows pupils around to keep them safe.

Helping people who ask, quietly and kindly, keeps them safe without anyone being watched.

B · For a curious adult

Four parts and one prohibition. Tell people what company tags are for; prepare the people most at risk; make it easy and private to ask for help; support the person and treat everyone fairly. And never turn protection into surveillance: following staff, scanning people or reading personal phones harms the people the policy exists to protect.

Where the analogy breaks: laws on monitoring, works councils and evidence differ by country. This is an outline to take to legal and HR, not legal advice.

C · For an engineering student

  • Phone alerts depend on separation from the owner, time and a compatible phone; they can miss or be late. documented
  • Consider safety before disabling a tracker: the owner stops receiving its location and may notice. Apple Personal Safety User Guide
  • Google’s Find Hub acceptable use policy forbids tracking people without their knowledge and consent. documented
  • Restricted-area scanning, if used, should be minimised and reviewed; it is a design decision for legal, security and staff representatives together. conceptual

D · For a security architect

Design the reporting route so that the person stays in control: they decide what is recorded, when security acts and whether police are involved. The playbook never has staff search colleagues’ belongings or confront anyone.

E · For a CISO

  • Owners: physical security (notices, restricted areas), travel security (executives), HR with legal (support and due process), communications (notices).
  • Review the policy with staff representatives before rollout.
  • Check that endpoint baselines do not switch off the detection that protects staff.
Engineering notes, accuracy check and sources

Engineering notes

The four panels follow the response principles in From Tap to Trace, Part X (safety first, preserve evidence, legal before law enforcement, never confront). The red panel lists prohibitions, not options.

Accuracy check (red team)

  • Does the policy require monitoring employees’ locations? (No: it is listed as something it must not do.)
  • Does it say that disabling a tracker can alert whoever placed it? (Yes, frame 3.)
  • Does it give legal conclusions? (No: it sends decisions to legal review.)

Scene 84

The first 180 days: decide, fix, build in

A roadmap with an owner on every step, and metrics that measure what the organisation can do, never how its people behave.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. First, the grown-ups decide who is in charge of what.
  2. Next, they count the school’s things and tidy up their accounts.
  3. Then they make checking a habit.
  4. They check how well the school helps, not how pupils behave.

A plan with a person in charge of each step gets done, and checking the plan, not the people, keeps everyone’s trust.

B · For a curious adult

Thirty days to decide, sixty more to inventory and fix, ninety more to make it routine. The metrics count things the organisation controls: how many company tags are on managed accounts, how quickly someone who reports a tracker gets a safety plan.

Where the analogy breaks: real programmes slip and overlap; the three boxes are an order of work, not a guarantee of dates.

C · For an engineering student

  • Follows the NIST CSF 2.0 functions: Govern (owners), Identify (inventory), Protect (managed accounts), Detect (alerts left on), Respond (playbook), Recover (re-register, retire). framework
  • Procurement criteria can follow the NIST IR 8259A device baseline: identification, configuration, data protection, logical access, software update, cybersecurity state awareness. NIST IR 8259A
  • Metrics are ratios and times over company assets and processes; none takes a person as its unit. method

D · For a security architect

The cryptographic inventory in the last phase connects this module to Module 10: readers and badges installed now may outlive the algorithms they use, so record them while you are counting tags anyway.

E · For a CISO

  • Report the four capability metrics to the risk committee each quarter.
  • Refuse vanity metrics: a count of incidents found rewards looking harder at people, not protecting them.
  • Owners on the roadmap are typical; assign your own before day 30 ends.
Engineering notes, accuracy check and sources

Engineering notes

Steps and metrics follow From Tap to Trace, Part X (the 30/90/180-day roadmap and the measures in Table 10), with the incident tabletop and staff education brought forward.

Accuracy check (red team)

  • Does any metric rank or score people? (No: listed under “never measure”.)
  • Does every step name an owner? (Yes.)
  • Does the roadmap contradict the essay’s order of work? (No; it follows it.)
