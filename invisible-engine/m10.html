Module 10 · Changing the locks without breaking the doors

Future cryptography of cards, trackers and accounts

The replacement locks exist: NIST published them in August 2024. Fitting them is the hard part — a card chip, a coin-cell tracker and a cloud account each change on a different clock, and some will only change when they are replaced.

Scene 73

Inventory first: which cryptography does each part use?

Before anyone can migrate, someone has to write down what cryptography runs where — and admit where the answer is not published.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. A house has many locks: front door, garage, a safe, a bike lock.
  2. Before buying new locks, you walk round and list every one.
  3. Some locks have a label that says what kind they are.
  4. Some have no label at all — you write “unknown”.
  5. Only then can you plan which to change first.

You cannot change every lock until you have listed them all, including the ones whose type you do not know.

B · For a curious adult

A single tap, a single tag and a single account each use several kinds of cryptography for different jobs. Some are symmetric (both sides share a secret key, as in a card’s transaction cryptogram); some are public-key (key agreement and signatures). Quantum computers would affect these two families very differently (Module 9, Scene 72).

Where the analogy breaks: a house’s locks are all visible. Cryptography inside a card, a tag or a cloud service often is not: vendors document some of it, some only for certain versions, and some not at all. “Unknown” is a finding, not a blank.

C · For an engineering student

  • EMV transaction cryptograms (ARQC) are computed with symmetric keys derived per card; the algorithm is TDES or AES depending on the issuer and card generation. EMV specification
  • EMV offline data authentication (SDA, DDA, CDA) uses RSA certificate chains; EMVCo also publishes elliptic-curve specifications — which applies to a given card is not visible from outside. verify per card
  • Bluetooth LE Secure Connections pairing: ECDH on P-256. Core Specification Find My location reports: ECDH on NIST P-224 with AES-GCM. Apple documented
  • Tracker firmware signature algorithms are not publicly documented. not disclosed
  • TLS 1.3 uses ephemeral elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman; hybrid X25519MLKEM768 key exchange is being deployed by major browsers and content-delivery networks, and Apple’s iMessage PQ3 uses ML-KEM in a hybrid. verify per service

D · For a security architect

An inventory is a trust-boundary map with algorithms attached. Record, for each component and version: the job (authentication, key agreement, confidentiality, integrity), the algorithm and key size, the evidence (vendor document, standard, your own measurement) and who can change it. The public-key rows that protect long-lived confidentiality are the first migration candidates; symmetric rows mostly need key-length checks.

E · For a CISO

  • Ask every vendor for a cryptographic bill of materials by product and firmware version, with dates. “We use strong encryption” is not an inventory entry.
  • Track UNKNOWN rows as open risks with an owner, not as gaps to ignore.

Scene 74

The new standard locks: three NIST algorithms

In August 2024 NIST published three post-quantum standards. They are ordinary software and hardware, designed to resist known quantum attacks.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Clever people designed new kinds of locks.
  2. One lock is for agreeing a secret.
  3. Two locks are for signing your name so no one can fake it.
  4. They work on the phones and computers we already have.
  5. They are built so that even a future quantum computer should not open them.

The new locks run on ordinary computers and are built to resist the tricks a quantum computer is expected to have.

B · For a curious adult

Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) is not quantum technology. It is new mathematics — mainly lattices and hash functions — chosen because no known quantum algorithm solves those problems efficiently. ML-KEM replaces the key-agreement job; ML-DSA and SLH-DSA replace the signature job. Symmetric ciphers such as AES stay, with long keys.

Where the analogy breaks: “new locks” suggests the old ones are already being picked. They are not (Module 9, Scene 72). And no lock is proven to hold forever: these are designed to resist known attacks, classical and quantum, and are still being studied.

C · For an engineering student

  • ML-KEM (FIPS 203), parameter sets ML-KEM-512/768/1024; security from the Module Learning With Errors problem. FIPS 203
  • ML-DSA (FIPS 204), ML-DSA-44/65/87; Module-LWE and Module-SIS. FIPS 204
  • SLH-DSA (FIPS 205), stateless hash-based (SPHINCS+ lineage); security rests on the hash function. FIPS 205
  • FN-DSA (draft FIPS 206) and HQC (selected March 2025 as an additional KEM) — status changes; check NIST. verify
  • NIST IR 8547 sets out a transition timeline for retiring quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms. draft; check status

D · For a security architect

Treat the standards as building blocks, not as a migration plan. The protocol around them (TLS, account key sync, a tracker’s report format) must be redesigned and reviewed; parameter choice (e.g. ML-KEM-768 vs 1024) is a security-level decision; implementations need side-channel care, especially on smart cards and microcontrollers.

E · For a CISO

  • Require vendors to name the standard and parameter set they will use, and their implementation’s validation status, rather than marketing claims of immunity.

Which locks does a quantum computer threaten?

RSA specification

Would fail on a large fault-tolerant quantum computer. None is publicly known to exist.

What it is
Public-key encryption and signatures, resting on the difficulty of factoring.
Where this lesson meets it
Card certificates for offline data authentication; older TLS certificates (Scenes 14, 73).
Established quantum impact
Shor’s algorithm factors efficiently on a large fault-tolerant quantum computer.
Caveats
Published resource estimates for RSA-2048 keep falling (about 20 million noisy qubits in 2019; under a million in 2025) but remain estimates, and dated ones.
Post-quantum considerations
ML-KEM for key establishment, ML-DSA or SLH-DSA for signatures; first find where RSA actually lives.

The scene that explains it

ECDH / ECDSA specification

Would fail on a large fault-tolerant quantum computer. None is publicly known to exist.

What it is
Elliptic-curve key agreement and signatures, resting on discrete logarithms.
Where this lesson meets it
Bluetooth LE Secure Connections pairing (P-256), Find My reports (P-224), TLS key exchange (X25519), passkeys (Scenes 44, 73).
Established quantum impact
Shor’s algorithm also solves elliptic-curve discrete logarithms, again only on a large fault-tolerant machine.
Caveats
Elliptic-curve keys are tiny; their replacements are not (Scene 75), which matters for tags and advertisements.
Post-quantum considerations
Hybrid key establishment (for example X25519 with ML-KEM-768) where the protocol has room; redesign where it does not.

The scene that explains it

AES conceptual

Weakened only in an idealised count. Not broken.

What it is
Symmetric block cipher: one shared secret key.
Where this lesson meets it
Card cryptograms on newer cards, Find My report encryption (AES-GCM), disk and backup encryption.
Established quantum impact
Grover’s algorithm gives at most a square-root speed-up in an idealised count: AES-128 to about 2⁶⁴ steps, AES-256 to about 2¹²⁸.
Caveats
Grover parallelises poorly: splitting the search across k machines saves only √k. Not broken.
Post-quantum considerations
Prefer 256-bit keys for data that must stay secret for decades; no new algorithm needed.

The scene that explains it

SHA-2 hashes conceptual

Weakened only in an idealised count. Not broken.

What it is
Hash functions: fingerprints of data, used in signatures, lookups and key derivation.
Where this lesson meets it
Find My lookup values (SHA-256 of the public key), key derivation, firmware digests.
Established quantum impact
Finding a preimage drops to about 2¹²⁸ steps for SHA-256 in an idealised count; collision search gains little in practice.
Caveats
Not broken. Hash-based signatures (SLH-DSA) are built on exactly this robustness.
Post-quantum considerations
SHA-256 and SHA-384 remain the building blocks of the new standards.

The scene that explains it

ML-KEM (FIPS 203) specification

Designed to resist known quantum attacks. Runs on ordinary chips.

What it is
Key-encapsulation mechanism from lattice problems; standardised August 2024.
Where this lesson meets it
Hybrid TLS key exchange already deployed by some browsers and networks (verify current status); future device pairing.
Established quantum impact
No known quantum algorithm breaks it efficiently.
Caveats
Young implementations: side channels and bugs are the near-term risk. ML-KEM-768 sends a 1,184-byte key and a 1,088-byte ciphertext.
Post-quantum considerations
Use in hybrid with a classical exchange during migration; measure sizes against each link’s limits.

The scene that explains it

ML-DSA (FIPS 204) specification

Designed to resist known quantum attacks. Runs on ordinary chips.

What it is
Lattice-based digital signature; standardised August 2024.
Where this lesson meets it
Future certificates, firmware signing and accounts.
Established quantum impact
No known quantum algorithm breaks it efficiently.
Caveats
ML-DSA-65 has a 1,952-byte public key and 3,309-byte signatures: tens of times larger than ECDSA.
Post-quantum considerations
Plan storage, bandwidth and boot-time verification for the larger sizes.

The scene that explains it

SLH-DSA (FIPS 205) specification

Designed to resist known quantum attacks. Runs on ordinary chips.

What it is
Stateless hash-based signature; standardised August 2024.
Where this lesson meets it
Long-lived roots of trust, such as firmware signing keys.
Established quantum impact
Security rests on hash functions alone, the most conservative choice.
Caveats
Large, slow signatures: the small variant SLH-DSA-SHA2-128s signs with 7,856 bytes.
Post-quantum considerations
Suited to rare, high-value signatures, not chatty protocols.

Bluetooth LE authentication specification

Would fail on a large fault-tolerant quantum computer. None is publicly known to exist.

Firmware signatures not publicly disclosed

Several parts with different exposure: look at each one.

What it is
The device checks a signature before installing new software.
Where this lesson meets it
Every tag, phone and reader that accepts updates. The AirTag’s algorithm is not publicly documented.
Established quantum impact
If the scheme is RSA or elliptic-curve, a large fault-tolerant quantum computer could forge updates for devices that still accept them.
Caveats

What it is
TLS to the service, plus sign-in with passwords, passkeys or security keys.
Where this lesson meets it

Caveats

Hybrid TLS where offered; plan passkey and certificate migration with vendors.

Scene 75

Why trackers cannot simply switch: bytes, chips and batteries

Post-quantum keys and signatures are tens to hundreds of times larger than the elliptic-curve ones they replace. For a coin-cell tracker that whispers 31 bytes at a time, that is a design problem.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. The new locks are much bigger than the old ones.
  2. A tracker can only whisper a few words at a time.
  3. A big new key would need dozens of whispers.
  4. Every whisper uses a little battery.
  5. So engineers must design new ways to talk, not just swap the lock.

Because the new keys are so much bigger, small devices need new designs, not just new locks.

B · For a curious adult

An elliptic-curve public key fits in a single Bluetooth advertisement with room to spare; a post-quantum key does not come close. That changes how a tracker could broadcast, how many packets it sends, how long its battery lasts, and whether its chip has the memory and speed to compute the new algorithms. The owner’s phones and the cloud service must change in step.

Where the analogy breaks: “bigger lock” suggests weaker or clumsier. Size is not weakness: these are different mathematics with different costs. And a design may avoid sending the large object at all (for example, keeping post-quantum exchange between phone and cloud), which is for the vendor to design and document.

C · For an engineering student

⌈1184 / 31⌉ = 39 advertisements ; ⌈1184 / 517⌉ = 3 ATT packets

  • X25519 public key 32 B RFC 7748; compressed P-256 point 33 B SEC 1; ECDSA P-256 signature 64 B as raw r‖s (DER encoding is up to 72 B) FIPS 186-5.
  • ML-KEM-768: encapsulation key 1,184 B, ciphertext 1,088 B FIPS 203; ML-DSA-65: public key 1,952 B, signature 3,309 B FIPS 204; SLH-DSA-SHA2-128s signature 7,856 B FIPS 205.
  • Legacy advertising data ≤ 31 B; ATT MTU default 23 B, maximum 517 B. Bluetooth Core Spec The counts above are computed from those limits. computed
  • How any vendor would carry post-quantum keys in a tracker protocol is not published. not disclosed

D · For a security architect

Map the migration boundary: which link can change by software (phone ↔ cloud TLS, account key sync), which needs new firmware (tag ↔ phone), and which is fixed in silicon (boot ROM signature check). Each boundary needs a compatibility plan for mixed fleets and a downgrade-resistant transition (Scene 76).

E · For a CISO

  • Ask device vendors three questions: can the boot chain verify a new signature type; is there a firmware update path for the installed base; and what is the replacement horizon for devices that cannot be updated?

Scene 76

Hybrid key exchange: two locks on one door

During the transition, many systems run a classical and a post-quantum key exchange side by side, and combine the results so the session stays secret if either one holds.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. You put two different locks on one door.
  2. A thief would need to open both.
  3. One lock is old and well tested; one is new.
  4. If either lock holds, the door stays shut.
  5. And you check nobody quietly removed one of the locks.

Using an old lock and a new lock together keeps the door shut as long as either one holds.

B · For a curious adult

The old algorithm has decades of study behind it; the new one resists quantum attacks but is younger. A hybrid uses both, so a surprise weakness in either does not expose the session. The two results are combined carefully and the whole handshake is checked, so nobody can quietly strip the new part out.

Where the analogy breaks: two door locks are independent; two key exchanges must be combined correctly, and a naive combination can lose the benefit. That is why only reviewed, standardised constructions should be used.

C · For an engineering student

session key = KDF( ssX25519 ‖ ssML-KEM ‖ transcript )

  • TLS 1.3 hybrid key exchange concatenates the two shared secrets into the key schedule; X25519MLKEM768 is the deployed combination. IETF drafts; check status
  • Downgrade resistance comes from authenticating the full transcript (Finished messages, signatures over the handshake). TLS 1.3, RFC 8446
  • Apple’s iMessage PQ3 combines ML-KEM with elliptic-curve keys in its own protocol. vendor documented
  • This scene shows the pattern, not a protocol specification. conceptual

D · For a security architect

Put the hybrid at the key-establishment boundary where long-lived confidentiality is at stake (Scene 78). Require transcript binding, a clear negotiation policy (when to refuse classical-only peers) and monitoring of which mode each connection actually used. Never compose primitives in-house.

E · For a CISO

  • Track the share of your traffic (and your vendors’) that uses hybrid key exchange, and set a date-free policy for when classical-only connections become exceptions.

Scene 77

Crypto agility: a lifecycle, not a switch

Organisations do not “switch on” post-quantum cryptography. They inventory, prioritise, test, roll out in stages, keep the ability to roll back, and check again — and some devices will only change when they are replaced.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. First you list every lock.
  2. Then you decide which to change first.
  3. You try the new locks in a workshop.
  4. You change a few, then more.
  5. Some tiny old locks cannot change — you plan to replace them.

Changing locks is a careful cycle you repeat, and some small devices can only be replaced, not upgraded.

B · For a curious adult

Crypto agility means a system can change its algorithms without being rebuilt. Most phones and servers have it through software updates; many cards and tags have little or none. The cycle shown is how organisations manage that difference over years.

Where the analogy breaks: the steps look sequential, but in practice different product lines are at different steps at once, and standards change underneath them.

C · For an engineering student

  • Inputs: the inventory (Scene 73), the three clocks (Scene 78), size and performance limits (Scene 75). process
  • Lab measurement covers handshake size and latency, CPU and energy per operation, memory, and interoperability with older peers. conceptual
  • Rollback must not reopen a downgrade path (Scene 76). conceptual

D · For a security architect

Agility is an architectural property: algorithm identifiers in formats, negotiable suites, updatable trust anchors and key rotation that does not need re-enrolment. Devices without these are migrated by replacement, so their end-of-life dates belong in the security plan.

E · For a CISO

  • Assign an owner to each step and each product line; report progress as “share of inventory at each step”, not as a single “quantum-ready” flag.

Scene 78

Three clocks: shelf life, migration time, and the unknown

When should you start? Not when someone predicts a date, but when the time your data must stay protected, plus the time change will take, looks long.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Some secrets only matter for a minute.
  2. Some secrets must stay secret for years.
  3. Changing all the locks takes a long time.
  4. Nobody knows when a lock-breaking machine might exist.
  5. So we start first where secrets last longest.

Because nobody knows when, we protect first the secrets that must last longest and take longest to fix.

B · For a curious adult

Three durations decide urgency. If secret data must stay secret for 10 years (x) and moving to new cryptography takes 5 years (y), then it matters whether a capable quantum computer arrives within 15 years (z) — and nobody knows z. The comparison tells you what to do first, not when the danger arrives.

Where the analogy breaks: real clocks run at known speeds; z is not a clock anyone can read. Treat it as an uncertainty to plan around, not a deadline to quote.

C · For an engineering student

risk if   x + y > z

  • Mosca’s inequality, as a planning heuristic. concept
  • Harvest now, decrypt later applies to confidentiality (x for encrypted data). For signatures, the concern is authenticity: a forged signature only matters once a quantum computer exists, but devices whose trust anchors cannot change carry that exposure for their whole lifetime. conceptual
  • Shelf lives in the table are typical examples. illustrative

D · For a security architect

Classify data and trust anchors by shelf life and exposure, estimate y per system from the inventory and vendor answers, and do not invent z. Systems with long x (backups, health or identity data) and long y (embedded devices, firmware trust) go first.

E · For a CISO

  • Report risk as “long-shelf-life data still protected by quantum-vulnerable key exchange” and “devices with fixed trust anchors and long lifetimes”, with owners — never as a countdown to a predicted date.

Engineering notes
