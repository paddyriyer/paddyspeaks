Module 8 · Before anyone says “quantum computer”

Quantum from switches to interference

Every claim about quantum computers rests on one idea: amplitudes that add and cancel. This module builds it from a light switch, through water waves and arrows on a circle, to a three-gate circuit that always gives the same answer, and says plainly where each picture stops being true.

Scenes 56–64Prerequisites: None beyond school algebra. Complex numbers are introduced in Scene 59. Module 1 (energy versus information) helps but is not required.

How to read every diagram

  • Orange arrow — energy or power
  • Blue arrow — radio signal or physical wave

Scene 56

A bit is a switch that is either on or off

Start with what a classical computer is made of: switches. Each one is a bit, definitely 0 or definitely 1, readable as often as you like, and copyable.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. A light switch is on or off.
  2. Inside a computer, tiny switches do the same.
  3. On means 1, off means 0.
  4. Three switches can make eight patterns.
  5. But they show only one pattern at a time.

A computer stores everything as switches that are either on or off, and you can look at them without changing them.

B · For a curious adult

A transistor in a chip works as an electrically controlled switch. Circuits treat a voltage near the supply as 1 and a voltage near zero as 0. Group three of them and you have a register with eight possible patterns; group 64 and there are about eighteen billion billion. But the register holds one pattern at any moment, and you can read it, copy it and read it again without disturbing it.

Where the analogy breaks: a real transistor is not a light switch: it has thresholds, noise margins and a time to switch. “On” and “off” are bands of voltage that circuit designers keep well apart so that the logic stays definite.

C · For an engineering student

  • Digital logic maps voltage ranges to 0 and 1, with a forbidden band between them (noise margin). logic-family specifications

  • n bits → 2ⁿ distinct states; a register holds exactly one of them at a time.

    calculated
  • Reading a classical bit is non-destructive and copying it is exact. The quantum no-cloning theorem says an unknown quantum state cannot be copied. theorem (Wootters and Zurek, 1982)

D · For a security architect

Everything a security architect protects today, keys included, is stored as definite, copyable bits. That is what makes key backup, escrow and exfiltration possible. A quantum state behaves differently: it cannot be copied unread. That property is the basis of quantum key distribution, a separate topic from quantum computing and from post-quantum cryptography.

E · For a CISO

  • Post-quantum cryptography (Module 10) still runs on ordinary bits, on ordinary chips. “Quantum-safe” does not mean “quantum hardware”.

Scene 57

Waves that add and waves that cancel

Before any quantum idea, borrow a picture from water: two sets of ripples meeting. Where crests meet, the water rises twice as high; where a crest meets a trough, it stays flat. The scene is labelled as an analogy because it is one.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Throw two stones into a pond.
  2. Each makes rings of little waves.
  3. Where two bumps meet, the water jumps higher.
  4. Where a bump meets a dip, the water stays flat.
  5. Remember this; we will use it as a picture, not as the real thing.

Waves can add up to something bigger or cancel to nothing, depending on how they line up.

B · For a curious adult

Superposition, in ordinary physics, just means that when two waves overlap the displacements add, point by point. Line up two crests and you get a crest twice as tall. Line up a crest with a trough and they cancel: that spot stays still. This adding and cancelling is called interference.

Where the analogy breaks: water is a substance and its height is something you can see. In quantum mechanics what adds are amplitudes, mathematical numbers that set the odds of an outcome. Nothing is rippling. Keep the arithmetic; drop the water.

C · For an engineering student

  • y(t) = A·sin(ωt) + A·sin(ωt + φ)

    In phase (φ = 0): amplitude 2A. Opposite phase (φ = 180°): 0. calculated
  • Linear superposition holds for small water waves, sound and light; real water waves become non-linear when steep. conceptual
  • The pond diagram is a schematic top view; ring spacing is illustrative. illustrative

D · For a security architect

The analogy is a good teaching tool and a frequent source of bad claims. Watch for vendor or press language that treats qubits as “waves inside the chip” or interference as something that happens to data. In a quantum computer, interference is a property of how amplitudes combine in a computation; the hardware does not contain ripples.

E · For a CISO

  • When a briefing leans on a wave picture, ask what adds: heights of something physical, or amplitudes in a calculation. The answer tells you whether the claim is about physics or about marketing.

Scene 58

Frequency, wavelength and phase

Three words do most of the work in any wave story. Frequency is how fast it repeats, wavelength how far apart its crests are, and phase where in its cycle it is. Shift one wave’s phase against another and their sum grows, shrinks or vanishes.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. A wave goes up and down again and again.
  2. How fast it repeats is its frequency.
  3. Where it is in its up-and-down is its phase.
  4. Two waves in step make a big wave.
  5. Two waves out of step cancel out.

Whether two waves add or cancel depends on how far one is shifted against the other.

B · For a curious adult

Slide one wave along relative to the other and watch the sum. In step (0°) they make a wave twice as tall. A quarter-cycle apart (90°) they make a wave about 1.41 times as tall. Half a cycle apart (180°) they cancel completely. Energy follows the square of the height, so doubling the height quadruples the energy.

Where the analogy breaks: where two waves cancel in a pond, the energy does not vanish; it appears at the loud spots instead. Quantum amplitudes are not energy either: their squares are probabilities, which is the next big step (Scene 60).

C · For an engineering student

  • A sin(ωt) + A sin(ωt + φ) = 2A cos(φ/2) · sin(ωt + φ/2)

    so the sum amplitude is 2A·|cos(φ/2)|. calculated

  • λ = v / f     intensity ∝ amplitude²

    standard physics
  • Sum amplitudes for φ = 0°, 90°, 180°, 270°: 2.00, 1.41, 0.00, 1.41 (× A). calculated

D · For a security architect

Phase is the parameter that will matter most in Modules 8 and 9: quantum algorithms work by arranging phases so that unwanted outcomes cancel. Any claim of a quantum speed-up should be able to say which interference does the work; if it cannot, treat it with suspicion.

E · For a CISO

  • No action here; this is vocabulary. It is enough to know that “phase” decides whether things add or cancel.

Scene 59

Complex amplitudes: arrows with a length and an angle

The quantum version of a wave’s height is an amplitude, and amplitudes are complex numbers: arrows with a length and an angle. The angle is the phase. Two kinds of phase need to be told apart, because only one of them changes anything.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Think of a clock hand that can point anywhere.
  2. Its length says how likely something is.
  3. Its direction is called its phase.
  4. Turning every hand together changes nothing you can notice.
  5. Turning one hand against another changes what happens next.

In quantum maths, each possibility is an arrow, and how the arrows point decides whether they add up or cancel later.

B · For a curious adult

A quantum system with two possible outcomes is described by two amplitudes, one for each. Each amplitude is a complex number, which you can picture as an arrow: its length sets how likely that outcome is, and its angle is its phase. When two arrows are added they can reinforce or cancel, just like waves, depending on how they point.

Turn both arrows by the same angle and nothing observable changes: that is global phase. Turn one against the other and the next step can come out differently: that is relative phase, the quantity Scenes 62 and 63 put to work.

Where the analogy breaks: the arrows are a way to picture numbers in a calculation. You cannot point a camera at a qubit and see its arrow.

C · For an engineering student

  • e^(iφ) = cos φ + i·sin φ     |e^(iφ)| = 1

    Euler’s formula
  • Global phase: e^(iθ)|ψ⟩ gives the same probabilities for every measurement, so states that differ only by global phase are physically identical. standard quantum mechanics
  • Relative phase: (|0⟩ + e^(iφ)|1⟩)/√2 for different φ are different states; φ shows up after a further gate (Scene 63, demo G). calculated

D · For a security architect

“Phase” in quantum computing is a property of the mathematical state, controlled by gates and destroyed by noise. It is not a hidden channel and not something an eavesdropper reads off a device. Keeping relative phase intact long enough to use it is the central engineering problem (Module 9, Scene 70).

E · For a CISO

  • Vocabulary only. The point to keep: quantum advantage depends on fragile relative phases, which is why error correction, not raw qubit count, sets the pace (Module 9).

Scene 60

A qubit, and the rule that turns amplitudes into odds

A qubit is described by two amplitudes, one for 0 and one for 1. Measure it and you always get a definite 0 or 1; the squares of the amplitudes are the odds. That rule, the Born rule, is the bridge from the arrows to anything you can observe.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. A qubit is like a special coin that has not landed yet.
  2. Two numbers say how likely heads (0) or tails (1) are.
  3. When you look, it always shows a definite 0 or 1.
  4. Do it many times and you see the odds.
  5. But unlike a coin, a qubit can be steered before you look.

Squaring the qubit’s numbers gives the odds of seeing 0 or 1 when you finally look.

B · For a curious adult

Write a qubit as α|0⟩ + β|1⟩. The numbers α and β are amplitudes. Measure it and you get 0 with probability |α|² and 1 with probability |β|², and the two always add to 1. Each measurement gives one whole answer; the odds only show up if you prepare and measure the same state many times.

Two states can have identical odds and still be different, because amplitudes can be negative (or, generally, complex). A coin flip and a qubit in superposition both look like 50:50 at first; one more gate tells them apart, because amplitudes can cancel and a coin’s odds cannot.

Where the analogy breaks: “a coin that has not landed” suggests the qubit secretly is heads or tails already. Experiments rule out that simple picture; the amplitudes are not a hidden answer.

C · For an engineering student

  • |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩,    |α|² + |β|² = 1,    P(0) = |α|², P(1) = |β|²

    Born rule
  • Frame 1: α = √3/2 ≈ 0.866, β = 1/2 → P = 0.75, 0.25. Frame 2: ±1/√2 → 0.50, 0.50. calculated
  • The 100 outcomes are a fixed illustrative sample with exactly 75 zeros; real runs fluctuate around 75 (standard deviation √(100·0.75·0.25) ≈ 4.3). illustrative
  • A classical 50:50 mixture and |+⟩ have the same Z-basis statistics but different X-basis statistics. conceptual

D · For a security architect

Measurement gives one sample per run. Any algorithm that relies on a quantum computer must therefore make the answer likely enough to show up in a few runs, which is what interference is for. A device that merely produces random-looking bits is not computing anything quantum-mechanically useful; randomness alone is cheap.

E · For a CISO

  • When a quantum result is quoted, ask how many runs (shots) it took and with what success probability. A single sample proves little.

Scene 61

The Hadamard gate: even odds, not half a particle

The Hadamard gate, H, is the workhorse of quantum circuits. It turns a definite 0 into equal odds of 0 and 1, and a definite 1 into the same odds with a minus sign. Applied twice, it undoes itself, which is the first sign of interference.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. A special step called H mixes a qubit.
  2. Start with a sure 0, and after H it is 50:50.
  3. Start with a sure 1, and it is also 50:50, with a hidden minus sign.
  4. It never makes half a thing; you always see a whole 0 or 1.
  5. Do H twice and you are back where you started.

The H step spreads a qubit evenly between 0 and 1, and doing it twice brings it back because the in-between parts cancel.

B · For a curious adult

H takes |0⟩ to an equal mixture of amplitudes for 0 and 1, and |1⟩ to the same mixture with the sign of the 1-part flipped. Measured straight away, both give 50:50 odds. But they are different states, and that difference becomes visible if you apply H again: |0⟩ → H → H gives |0⟩ every time, because the two routes to 1 have opposite signs and cancel.

Where the analogy breaks: it is tempting to say the particle is “half 0 and half 1”. Nothing is split: every detection finds a whole particle, and the halves are odds. The state is a list of amplitudes, not a mixture of two half-objects.

C · For an engineering student

  • H = (1/√2) [[1, 1], [1, −1]]    H|0⟩ = |+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2    H|1⟩ = |−⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2

    standard definition

  • H·H = I

    so H|+⟩ = |0⟩: amplitude to 1 is (1/√2)(1/√2) + (1/√2)(−1/√2) = 0. calculated
  • Ideal gates are unitary (reversible, length-preserving); real gates add errors (Module 9, Scene 70). conceptual

D · For a security architect

H is how circuits create the even superpositions that algorithms such as Grover’s start from (Module 9, Scene 66). Its reversibility matters: quantum gates do not discard information until a measurement or noise does.

E · For a CISO

  • Vocabulary. If a briefing says a quantum computer “stores every value at once”, translate it to “prepares amplitudes over many values, of which one is read”.
Engineering notes, accuracy check and sources

Engineering notes

Amplitudes shown to three places; 0.707 is 1/√2. Gates are ideal.

  • Does the scene say “half a particle” as fact? (No: frame 4 rejects it.)
  • Do the bars add to probability 1? (Yes: 0.5 + 0.5.)
  • Is H·H = I shown with the cancelling term? (Yes: frame 5.)

Scene 62

The Z gate changes a phase you cannot see directly

The Z gate leaves the odds exactly as they were. All it does is flip the sign of the 1-part. Measured straight away, nothing seems to have happened; one more gate later, everything has.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Z is a step that changes a hidden sign.
  2. Before and after, the odds are still 50:50.
  3. So if you look right away, nothing seems different.
  4. But the hidden sign changes what the next step does.

Some changes cannot be seen straight away but decide what happens next.

B · For a curious adult

Z multiplies the amplitude of 1 by −1. On |+⟩ it produces |−⟩. If you measure either one directly, you get 0 or 1 with equal odds; no single reading can tell which state you had. The difference is a relative phase of 180° between the two parts.

That invisible difference becomes decisive as soon as another gate mixes the parts again: H turns |+⟩ into 0 every time and |−⟩ into 1 every time. Phase is information you cannot read directly but can convert into something you can.

Where the analogy breaks: the arrows suggest the |1⟩ part “points left”. They are a picture of a sign in a calculation, not a direction in space.

C · For an engineering student

  • Z = [[1, 0], [0, −1]]    Z|+⟩ = |−⟩    P(0) = P(1) = 1/2 before and after

    calculated
  • Z is a phase gate with φ = π; the general phase gate is diag(1, e^(iφ)). standard definition
  • |+⟩ and |−⟩ are orthogonal: a measurement in the X basis (H, then measure) distinguishes them perfectly. standard quantum mechanics

D · For a security architect

Phase errors are a large share of real qubit errors (dephasing, Module 9 Scene 70). They are dangerous precisely because they do not show up in a direct measurement: a qubit can look fine and still have lost the relative phase an algorithm needs.

E · For a CISO

  • When a vendor quotes only “readout accuracy”, ask about phase coherence (often reported as T2). Both matter.

Scene 63

Interference: H, then Z, then H always gives 1

Put the pieces together. Three gates turn a definite 0 into a definite 1, not because anything is “tried”, but because the routes to 0 cancel and the routes to 1 add. Change the phase in the middle and the odds follow a smooth curve.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Start with a qubit that says 0.
  2. Step H spreads it out evenly.
  3. Step Z flips a hidden sign.
  4. Step H brings the parts back together.
  5. The parts that lead to 0 cancel, so you always get 1.

When hidden signs line up just right, the wrong answers cancel and only the right answer is left.

B · For a curious adult

Follow the amplitudes, not the qubit. After the first H there are two parts of equal size. Z flips the sign of one. The last H sends each part towards both outputs: towards 0, the contributions are +0.5 and −0.5, which cancel; towards 1, they are +0.5 and +0.5, which add to 1. The result is certain.

Replace Z with a general phase φ and the odds become cos²(φ/2) for 0 and sin²(φ/2) for 1. At φ = 0 the circuit always returns 0, at 180° always 1, and in between the odds slide smoothly. This is the whole trick behind quantum algorithms: arrange the phases so that wrong answers cancel.

Where the analogy breaks: “paths” is bookkeeping in a calculation, not a qubit travelling two routes in space.

C · For an engineering student

  • H Z H = X    so H Z H |0⟩ = |1⟩

    calculated

  • H · (|0⟩ + e^(iφ)|1⟩)/√2 = ((1 + e^(iφ))/2)|0⟩ + ((1 − e^(iφ))/2)|1⟩

    P(0) = |1 + e^(iφ)|²/4 = cos²(φ/2),   P(1) = sin²(φ/2)

    calculated
  • Endpoints: φ = 0°, 90°, 180°, 270°, 360° → P(0) = 1, 0.5, 0, 0.5, 1 (as plotted). calculated

D · For a security architect

This three-gate circuit is the smallest honest example of interference doing work. Every quantum speed-up claim should reduce, on inspection, to a larger version of this pattern. Noise that randomises φ flattens the curve towards 50:50 and destroys the advantage, which is why phase coherence is the resource to watch.

E · For a CISO

  • The question to ask of any claimed quantum advantage: which wrong answers cancel, and how is the phase kept intact long enough for them to cancel?
Engineering notes, accuracy check and sources

Engineering notes

The curve and the dots are computed from cos²(φ/2) and sin²(φ/2). Path contributions are products of amplitude and the H matrix entry, 1/√2 · 1/√2 = 0.5.

  • Do the plotted endpoints match the formula? (Yes: 1, 0.5, 0, 0.5, 1.)
  • Do the path contributions add to the final amplitudes? (Yes: 0 and 1.)

Scene 64

The double slit: stripes made of single dots

Interference is not only arithmetic in a circuit; nature does it. Send electrons, photons or even large molecules through two slits, one at a time, and stripes build up dot by dot. Ask which slit each one went through and the stripes disappear.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Shoot tiny particles at a wall with two gaps.
  2. Each one makes a single dot on a screen.
  3. After many dots, stripes appear.
  4. If you peek at which gap each one used, the stripes go away.
  5. So looking changes what you get.

Tiny particles land in stripes only when nobody can tell which way they went.

B · For a curious adult

This is the experiment Feynman called the heart of quantum mechanics. Particles arrive one at a time and each lands as a whole dot. Yet after thousands of dots, the pattern shows stripes that only make sense if the amplitudes for the two slits interfere. It has been done with photons, electrons (Tonomura’s 1989 film of single electrons building up the pattern) and molecules of sixty carbon atoms (Arndt and colleagues, 1999).

If anything records which slit a particle used, a detector or merely stray light and heat, the two possibilities no longer interfere and the stripes go. That loss is decoherence, and it is why quantum computers are cooled and shielded.

Where the analogy breaks: particles are not little ripples, and the stripes are not a substance on the screen. They are a pattern in the probabilities of where whole particles land.

C · For an engineering student

  • Two-slit pattern with slit separation d, slit width a, wavelength λ, screen angle θ:

    I(θ) ∝ cos²(π d sin θ / λ) · sinc²(π a sin θ / λ)

    standard optics The drawn curve uses this shape with illustrative values. illustrative
  • Single-electron buildup observed (Tonomura et al. 1989); C60 interference observed (Arndt et al. 1999). historical experiment
  • Which-path information, whether read or merely available to the environment, suppresses interference (complementarity; decoherence). established physics

D · For a security architect

For engineers the lesson is environmental: any unintended coupling that could reveal a qubit’s state destroys the interference an algorithm needs. Isolation, cooling and error correction (Module 9) exist to stop the environment from “asking which slit”.

E · For a CISO

  • Decoherence is why practical, large quantum computers are hard and slow to arrive. Plan cryptographic migration on standards and inventories (Module 10), not on headlines about qubit counts.
