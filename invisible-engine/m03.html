Module 3 · Tags that name things

RFID and adjacent physical identity

A warehouse counts a pallet without opening it, and an office door decides whether to open. Both rely on a tag that says only “I am number …”. What that number can and cannot prove is the whole story.

Scenes 18–22Prerequisites: Module 2, especially Scenes 12 (power from the field) and 13 (load modulation).

How to read every diagram

  • Orange arrow — energy or power
  • Blue arrow — radio signal or physical wave
  • Green arrow — plaintext or local information
  • Purple arrow — encrypted or cryptographic
  • Grey dashed arrow — registration or authorisation, never data
  • Red dashed outline — attacker zone or hazard
  • Thick dark box — trust, hardware or organisational boundary
  • Dashed blue rings — illustrative field drawings, not visible waves
Scene 18

Passive RFID: the warehouse dock door

A pallet rolls through a dock door and dozens of boxes are counted in a second, without anyone opening them or seeing a label.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. A truck brings boxes to a big shop’s back door.
  2. Each box has a sticker with a tiny wire and chip inside.
  3. A machine at the door sends out an invisible radio wave.
  4. The wave wakes each sticker, and it whispers back its number.
  5. The computer writes down: “these boxes came through door 3 just now”.

The door machine powers the stickers and hears their numbers, so the computer knows which boxes passed which door.

B · For a curious adult

A passive RFID tag is a label with an antenna and a chip, and no battery. A reader at the dock door sends a radio signal that does two jobs: it powers the chips and it carries commands. Each tag answers with an identifier, its EPC (Electronic Product Code), and the reader can count hundreds of tags a second.

The tag never says where it is. The inventory system knows which reader and antenna heard the tag and when, and from that it infers “pallet 17 left through door 3 at 10:42”.

Where the analogy breaks: “whispering back” suggests the tag transmits. A passive UHF tag has no transmitter: it changes how strongly its antenna reflects the reader’s own wave (backscatter), much as a mirror flashing sunlight sends a signal without a lamp.

C · For an engineering student

  • Bands: LF 125–134.2 kHz; HF 13.56 MHz (ISO/IEC 14443 proximity, ISO/IEC 15693 vicinity); UHF 860–960 MHz, “RAIN RFID”, with regional bands such as 865–868 MHz (Europe) and 902–928 MHz (US). specification
  • UHF air interface: GS1 EPC UHF Gen2, also published as ISO/IEC 18000-63. specification
  • Near-field boundary, roughly where the reactive field stops dominating:

r ≈ λ / 2π  ·  HF: λ = c/f = 3×10⁸ / 13.56×10⁶ ≈ 22 m → r ≈ 3.5 m  ·  UHF (915 MHz): λ ≈ 0.33 m → r ≈ 5 cm

  • An HF tag a few centimetres from the reader is deep in the near field: coupling is magnetic (inductive) and the tag answers by load modulation, as in Module 2. calculated
  • A UHF tag a metre or more away is several wavelengths out, in the far field: it harvests energy from a propagating wave and answers by modulating its antenna’s reflection (backscatter). conceptual
  • Gen2 inventory, simplified: Select picks a population, Query opens a round of 2Q slots, each tag picks a random slot and replies with a 16-bit random number (RN16), the reader ACKs it, and the tag sends its PC + EPC + CRC. Collisions are resolved by adjusting Q (slotted ALOHA). specification
  • Passive UHF read ranges of several metres are typical; up to about 10 m is often quoted, but tag orientation, metal, liquids and regional power limits change it greatly. illustrative

D · For a security architect

The tag is not a trust anchor. A basic Gen2 tag returns its EPC to any compliant reader in range, with no authentication of the reader; its 32-bit access and kill passwords are not strong security. Location is a property of the reader infrastructure (which antenna, which time), so the integrity of “where” depends on the readers, their clocks and their network, not on the tag.

Gen2v2 defines optional cryptographic suites for tag authentication, but most supply-chain tags do not use them. verify per tag model

E · For a CISO

  • Treat a tag read as an observation by your reader, not as proof that a specific item was present; reconcile with weights, counts or scans where losses matter.
  • If tagged goods leave your site, anyone with a compatible reader can read their EPC. Decide whether item-level tags should be killed or encoded without personal links at the point of sale.
  • Owner of the risk: the operator of the readers and the inventory system, not the tag supplier.

Scene 19

Three kinds of RFID tag

Some tags borrow all their energy from the reader; some carry a small battery for their chip; some carry a battery and a transmitter of their own.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Some tags have no battery at all. The reader’s wave wakes them.
  2. Some tags have a tiny battery to keep their brain awake.
  3. Some tags have a battery and their own little radio, and they call out by themselves.
  4. The more energy a tag carries, the further away it can be heard.

A tag with its own battery and radio can be heard from much further away, but it costs more and its battery runs out.

B · For a curious adult

Passive tags are powered entirely by the reader, which keeps them tiny and cheap: they go on parcels, clothes and library books. Battery-assisted passive (BAP) tags add a small cell so the chip and sensors (say, a temperature logger in a cold-chain shipment) can run even when the reader’s signal is weak; they still answer by reflecting the reader’s wave. Active tags have a battery and their own radio, and typically transmit on a schedule: they track shipping containers, vehicles and expensive tools across a yard.

Where the analogy breaks: “more battery, more range” is a rule of thumb. Range also depends on the reader’s power and antennas, the frequency, the surroundings and the regulations, and an active tag’s battery life depends on how often it transmits.

C · For an engineering student

  • Passive range is usually limited by the forward link: the tag must harvest enough power to switch on. A BAP tag removes that limit, so its range is set by the reader’s receive sensitivity to the weak backscattered signal. conceptual
  • Free-space path loss grows with distance squared (one way), so backscatter, which travels out and back, falls roughly with the fourth power of distance: calculated

Ptag ∝ 1/d²  ·  Pbackscatter at reader ∝ 1/d⁴ (free space, idealised)

  • Active RFID is not one standard; systems run at 433 MHz, 2.4 GHz and other bands, and some active “tags” are Bluetooth LE beacons (Module 4). conceptual
  • The range bars in the chart are illustrative typical figures, not specifications. illustrative

D · For a security architect

More range means more people can read a tag, from further away, without being noticed. An active tag that transmits on a timer can be heard by any compatible receiver, not only the owner’s. Treat the tag’s identifier as observable by others, and ask whether the identifier is fixed or changes (Module 4, Scene 28).

E · For a CISO

  • Choose the least-capable tag that does the job: passive for item counting, BAP for sensors, active for yard-scale tracking.
  • For active tags, battery replacement and disposal are an operating cost and an environmental obligation; budget for both.
  • Any tag that transmits continuously can, in principle, be tracked by others. Record that in the privacy assessment for tags on people or vehicles.

Scene 20

An office badge: the door asks a server

The badge does not open the door. A server does, after looking a number up in a table someone maintains.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Sam holds a badge up to a little box next to the door.
  2. The box reads the number on the badge.
  3. It asks the office computer: “Is number 48213 allowed in?”
  4. The computer checks its list and says yes. Click: the door opens.
  5. The computer remembers who came in, and when.

The door opens because the computer’s list says that badge number is allowed, not because the badge itself is magic.

B · For a curious adult

Office access control has four parts: the credential (the badge), the reader, the controller that drives the door, and the server that holds the permissions and the audit log. The badge supplies a number; the decision is a database lookup: is this number active, allowed through this door, at this hour?

Where the analogy breaks: calling the badge a “key” suggests it proves something by itself. Many badges only present a number. Whether that number can be copied, and whether the person holding the badge is its owner, are separate questions (Scene 21).

C · For an engineering student

  • Common credential families: 125 kHz proximity cards (a fixed number), and 13.56 MHz smart cards (ISO/IEC 14443) that can run cryptographic protocols. specification
  • Reader-to-controller links range from the legacy Wiegand interface, which carries the number unencrypted, to OSDP (Open Supervised Device Protocol) with its Secure Channel. documented
  • The controller can cache permissions so doors keep working if the server is unreachable; that cache must be refreshed when a badge is revoked. conceptual

D · For a security architect

Trust boundaries: the badge is outside your control once issued; the reader is on the unsecured side of the door; the wire and controller sit inside. A system that reads only a fixed number trusts anything that presents it. Possession is not identity: a lent, lost or stolen badge opens the same doors until it is revoked, so revocation speed and second factors (PIN, biometric check, guard) matter.

E · For a CISO

  • Know which credential technology each site actually uses; legacy fixed-number cards often survive in older buildings.
  • Own the joiner–mover–leaver process for badges as tightly as for accounts: revocation on the day someone leaves.
  • Review the audit log for impossible patterns (two doors far apart within seconds) as a cheap detection control.
  • Owners: physical security for readers and doors; IT or security for the server, its database and its logs.

Scene 21

Weak badge, strong badge

Two badges can look identical. One repeats a number; the other proves, each time, that it holds a secret it never reveals.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. One badge just says its number, the same way every time.
  2. If someone copies that number, the door can’t tell the difference.
  3. A better badge gets a new puzzle every time.
  4. It solves the puzzle with a secret only it knows, and never says the secret out loud.

A badge that answers a new puzzle each time is much harder to fake than one that repeats the same number.

B · For a curious adult

A static-ID badge is like a password said out loud every time you use it: anyone who records it can repeat it. A challenge–response badge is like being asked a different question each time, which you can answer only if you know a secret; hearing one answer does not help with the next question.

Neither design stops a relay, in which two devices pass the live conversation between a distant badge and the reader in real time. That is why high-security doors add timing checks or a second factor.

Where the analogy breaks: “secure badge” is a property of a whole system: card, keys, reader, configuration. A secure card read only for its serial number by a misconfigured reader is no stronger than a static-ID card.

C · For an engineering student

  • Challenge–response, generically: reader sends nonce N; card returns R = MACK(N, …) or a mutual-authentication exchange; the verifier holds or derives K. Keys are usually diversified per card from a master key and the card’s ID. conceptual
  • MIFARE Classic’s Crypto1 cipher was publicly broken in 2008; cards using it remain in service. historical
  • Newer families (for example MIFARE DESFire EV2/EV3, HID Seos and iCLASS SE, the LEAF credential) advertise AES-based mutual authentication. vendor documentation verify configuration
  • Distance bounding measures round-trip time to bound how far away the responder can be; light travels about 30 cm per nanosecond, so the timing must be tight. conceptual

D · For a security architect

The questions to ask: does the reader verify a cryptographic response, or read only the card serial number (UID)? Where are the keys held, and are they diversified per card? Is the reader-to-controller link authenticated (OSDP Secure Channel) or a plain Wiegand wire? A strong card behind a weak reader or link inherits the weakest link.

Relay is a precondition-heavy attack (proximity to the victim’s badge and to the door at the same time) but it is not prevented by encryption alone.

E · For a CISO

  • Inventory credential types by site and plan the retirement of fixed-number and Crypto1 cards.
  • Require readers configured for cryptographic authentication, not UID-only reads; check this at commissioning.
  • For high-value doors, add a second factor rather than relying on the card alone.
  • Residual risk: lost or lent badges, and relay. Owner: physical security, with key management agreed with IT security.

Scene 22

Six ways to identify a thing

RFID, NFC, a QR code, a printed serial number, a Bluetooth beacon and a GPS tracker all “identify” an object. Only one of them knows where it is.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. A sticker that answers a radio reader.
  2. A card you tap on a phone.
  3. A square pattern a camera can read, and a number printed on a label.
  4. A little gadget that calls out to nearby phones.
  5. And a tracker that asks satellites where it is, then phones home.

Most of these only say “I’m here” to whoever is nearby; only the satellite tracker works out its own place on a map.

B · For a curious adult

It helps to separate three ideas. Identification: the thing carries a name or number. Detection: something nearby notices it. Location: someone knows where on a map it is. RFID, NFC, QR codes, printed serials and Bluetooth beacons do identification; detection happens wherever a reader, camera or phone is; location is known by the observer, because the observer knows where it is itself.

A GPS tracker is different: it computes its own position from satellite signals and reports it over a mobile network to its owner’s account.

Where the analogy breaks: a Bluetooth tracker in a crowd-sourced finding network (Modules 4–5) is detected like a beacon but can still end up on its owner’s map, because the phones that hear it know where they are.

C · For an engineering student

  • Power source and link: RFID and NFC, reader-powered radio; QR codes and printed serials, optical, line of sight; BLE beacons, battery-powered 2.4 GHz advertising (iBeacon, Eddystone formats); GPS trackers, GNSS receiver plus cellular modem (often LTE-M or NB-IoT). specification
  • Typical ranges: NFC a few centimetres; passive UHF RFID several metres; BLE beacons tens of metres; printed codes as far as a camera or eye can resolve them. illustrative
  • Only GNSS gives the device its own coordinates; every other entry relies on the observer’s known position. conceptual

D · For a security architect

For each identifier ask: who can read it without the owner’s consent, from how far, and is the identifier fixed? Fixed identifiers readable at a distance (UHF RFID, a beacon with a static ID) allow anyone with a receiver to correlate sightings. A GPS tracker’s data goes to one service; its exposure is that service and the accounts that can read it.

E · For a CISO

  • When a team proposes “tracking”, ask which of the six it means; the privacy, cost and legal profile differ completely.
  • Tracking people (staff, visitors) raises consent and labour-law questions that tracking assets does not; route those proposals through privacy review.
  • Prefer identifiers that cannot be read at a distance or that change over time where people may carry them.
