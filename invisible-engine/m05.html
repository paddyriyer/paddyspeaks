Module 5 · How a lost thing is found

AirTag life cycle, finding network and handshakes

A suitcase is left on a platform. Its tag cannot see a map and cannot phone home. This module follows the whisper from the tag to a stranger’s phone, through a sealed report, to the owner’s screen — and shows what each handshake does and does not establish.

Scenes 31–41Prerequisites: Module 4 (the BLE whisper and rotating identifiers), and Module 2 Scene 15 for the idea of a cryptographic proof.

How to read every diagram

  • Orange arrow — energy or power
  • Blue arrow — radio signal or physical wave
  • Green arrow — plaintext or local information
  • Purple arrow — encrypted or cryptographic
  • Grey dashed arrow — registration or authorisation, never data
  • Red dashed outline — attacker zone or hazard
  • Thick dark box — trust, hardware or organisational boundary
  • Dashed blue rings — illustrative field drawings, not visible waves
Scene 31

First-time setup: from a new tag to a named item

Setting up a tracker takes a few taps. Those taps are documented; most of what happens underneath is not.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Dad gets a new tag for his keys.
  2. He holds it next to his phone.
  3. The phone asks: “Connect?”
  4. He says yes and calls it “Keys”.
  5. Now the tag belongs to Dad’s account.

The tag joins one person’s account only when that person says yes on their own phone.

B · For a curious adult

Setup ties a tag to one Apple Account. You hold the tag near an unlocked iPhone or iPad, tap Connect, choose a name, and tap Continue; from then on the tag shows up in Find My on every device signed in to that account. The account must use two-factor authentication, and Find My and Location Services must be on.

Where the analogy breaks: it feels like “pairing” a pair of headphones, but the important link is not tag-to-phone; it is tag-to-account. Replace the phone and the tag is still yours (Scene 41).

C · For an engineering student

  • Documented steps: hold near device → Connect → name → Continue → registered to the Apple Account. documented
  • Requirements: a compatible iPhone or iPad on a current iOS/iPadOS, Find My and Location Services on, two-factor authentication. documented
  • One account at a time; to give a tag away, remove it from your account first. documented
  • The radio and server messages during setup are not published. not publicly disclosed

D · For a security architect

The security-relevant step is account binding under an authenticated session, not radio proximity. Proximity limits who can start setup; the account binding (with two-factor authentication) decides who can later read locations. Treat “held near the phone” as a usability gate, not an authentication factor.

E · For a CISO

  • If staff register trackers to company assets, decide whose Apple Account they are bound to. A personal account binding means the company cannot recover the asset’s history when that person leaves.

Scene 32

What setup actually establishes, layer by layer

Six things are set up when a tag joins an account. For each one we say what Apple documents, what we infer, and what is not public.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Setting up a tag is like several locks clicking shut.
  2. One lock is the tag’s own number.
  3. One is Dad saying “yes”.
  4. One gives the tag secret codes to whisper.
  5. Some locks we can see; some are hidden inside.

Several separate things get set up at once, and only some of them are explained in public.

B · For a curious adult

“Pairing” is really six jobs: identify the hardware, talk to it, get the owner’s permission, record the owner, give the tag what it needs to make rotating identifiers (Module 4), and make it possible to undo all of that later.

Where the analogy breaks: a list of layers suggests a neat, documented protocol stack. Here, several layers are only visible through their effects. Labels below tell you which.

C · For an engineering student

  1. Hardware identity. Serial number on the tag and on the NFC page. documented Factory-provisioned keys or certificates. not publicly disclosed
  2. Transport. Close-range radio during setup (Bluetooth LE is the natural candidate). inferred Message contents. not publicly disclosed
  3. Authorisation. User consent on a signed-in device with two-factor authentication. documented
  4. Registration. One account at a time; removal required before transfer. documented Server API and formats. not publicly disclosed
  5. Key provisioning. Apple describes a P-224 key pair (d, P) and a secret SK₀ created on the owner’s device, from which time-indexed keys are derived; the tag can compute the public keys it advertises, while private keys stay with the owner. documented verify against the current guide How SK₀ and P reach the tag. not publicly disclosed
  6. Revocation. Remove Item, then another account can add the tag. documented Whether and how keys are erased on the tag. not publicly disclosed

D · For a security architect

Threat-model each layer separately. The strongest public guarantees are in layers 3 and 5 (authenticated consent; private keys held only by the owner’s devices). The least visible are 1, 2 and 6: factory provisioning, the setup transport and erasure on removal. Independent research has examined parts of the system research; claims beyond it are inference.

E · For a CISO

  • When a vendor says a device is “securely paired”, ask which of these six layers that covers, and which are documented. “Undisclosed” is not “insecure”, but it is unverifiable by you.

Scene 33

Far-away luggage: a tag that does not know where it is

A suitcase is left on a station platform. The owner is far away. What does the tag actually know?

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Grandma leaves her suitcase on the train platform.
  2. Its tag cannot see a map.
  3. It has no phone inside, so it cannot call.
  4. It just keeps whispering a secret word.

The tag cannot find itself, so it whispers and waits for a phone that knows where it is.

B · For a curious adult

Item trackers like AirTag are deliberately simple: a coin cell, a Bluetooth Low Energy radio, and a few other parts (Module 4). They have no GPS and no mobile connection. That is why they are small and why the battery lasts about a year or more, and it is also why they depend entirely on other people’s phones to be found far away.

Where the analogy breaks: “the tag reports its location” is the common phrase, and it is wrong. The tag reports nothing; phones near it report their own location (Scene 35).

C · For an engineering student

  • AirTag hardware: Bluetooth LE, ultra-wideband, NFC, speaker, accelerometer, user-replaceable CR2032 cell. No GNSS receiver, no cellular modem. documented
  • Compare a GPS tracker: GNSS receiver + cellular modem + larger battery; it knows and sends its own coordinates (Module 4, Scene 30).
  • Advertising intervals and power are covered in Module 4 (Scenes 24 and 29); values there are labelled illustrative.

D · For a security architect

The design choice (no GNSS, no cellular) moves the location capability to a crowd of third-party devices. That keeps the tag cheap and long-lived, but makes the system’s privacy depend on how those third parties report: encrypted, anonymous, and with rotating identifiers (Scenes 36 and 28).

E · For a CISO

  • For high-value assets that must be tracked anywhere, a crowd-sourced tag is the wrong tool: coverage depends on passers-by. Use dedicated GNSS + cellular trackers under a managed contract.

Scene 34

A passer-by’s phone overhears the whisper

A stranger walks past the suitcase. Their phone hears the tag’s advertisement. Nobody pairs with anything.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. A lady walks past the suitcase.
  2. Her phone hears the tag’s whisper.
  3. The phone does not answer the tag.
  4. The lady does not even notice.

A passing phone can overhear a whisper without talking back, like hearing a bell ring.

B · For a curious adult

Bluetooth LE devices can broadcast short advertisements that any nearby receiver may hear — the same mechanism shops use for beacons. The tag advertises; a passing iPhone that participates in the Find My network scans and picks it up. There is no pairing and no two-way exchange, and the finder does nothing.

Where the analogy breaks: “the tag connects to nearby phones” is a common description and it is wrong. A connection would let the two sides exchange messages; here information moves one way only.

C · For an engineering student

  • BLE advertising is connectionless; scanners can receive advertisements without pairing (Bluetooth Core Specification, advertising and scanning). specification
  • The Find My advertisement carries (part of) the current public key Pᵢ. documented Exact payload layout is described by independent research research, not by Apple.
  • Reception is probabilistic: scan windows, distance, walls and bodies matter (Module 4, Scene 27 and demo C).

D · For a security architect

The finder phone is an untrusted relay that learns little: a random-looking key that changes over time. It cannot link the key to an owner. Risks remain elsewhere: the same broadcast can be heard by any scanner, which is why the identifiers rotate (Module 4, Scene 28).

E · For a CISO

  • Your managed iPhones may act as finders for strangers’ items by default. Know whether your mobile policy changes that, and what it costs in privacy and in battery: very little, per Apple’s design.
Engineering notes, accuracy check and sources

Engineering notes

“Participating” means an Apple device with Find My network participation on; reception is not guaranteed.

  • Does the scene say the finder is notified or asked? (No.)

Scene 35

Whose location is it? The finder phone’s own

The whisper has no position in it. The location in a sighting is where the finder’s phone thinks it is.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. The tag whispers, but it does not know where it is.
  2. The lady’s phone knows where it is from satellites and Wi-Fi.
  3. The phone says: “I heard it, here, now.”
  4. That is the clue.

The clue is the place of the phone that heard the tag, not a place the tag knew.

B · For a curious adult

Every modern phone can estimate its own position. When a participating iPhone hears a lost item, it attaches its own estimated location and the time. The resulting point is approximate twice over: the phone’s position has an error, and the tag could be anywhere within Bluetooth range of the phone.

Where the analogy breaks: a map pin looks precise. A sighting is closer to “near here, at about this time”.

C · For an engineering student

  • Phone location fuses GNSS, Wi-Fi and cellular data and motion sensors; accuracy varies from metres to hundreds of metres. conceptual
  • BLE range to a phone is typically tens of metres, less through bodies and walls. illustrative

Sighting uncertainty ≈ phone position error + radio range (order of magnitude, not a formula Apple publishes). conceptual

D · For a security architect

The location in a report is the finder’s location. The design protects the finder by encrypting it to the tag’s key and not attaching the finder’s identity (Scene 36). That also means a sighting is evidence of proximity, not of the tag’s exact position.

E · For a CISO

  • When someone presents a tracker map as evidence (asset loss, an incident), treat each point as “near, at about this time”, with error bars, not as a GPS fix.

Scene 36

The sighting is sealed before it leaves the finder’s phone

The finder phone encrypts the sighting to the tag’s public key, then uploads it. Only the owner’s devices can open it.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. The lady’s phone writes a note: “here, now”.
  2. It locks the note with the tag’s open padlock.
  3. It posts the locked note to a big mailbox.
  4. The mailbox cannot open it.

The finder locks the note with the tag’s public padlock, so only the owner’s key can open it.

B · For a curious adult

This is public-key encryption: anyone can lock a message with a public key; only the holder of the matching private key can unlock it. The tag broadcasts a public key; the finder’s phone uses it to seal the location; the private key never leaves the owner’s devices. Apple stores the sealed report filed under a fingerprint (a hash) of that public key.

Where the analogy breaks: “end-to-end encrypted” means the content is hidden. It does not mean nothing is known: a report exists, at a time, under a lookup value.

C · For an engineering student

  • Curve NIST P-224; the finder generates an ephemeral key pair, performs ECDH with Pᵢ, derives a symmetric key, and encrypts the location with AES-GCM. documented verify against the current guide
  • Apple states it cannot read the location and does not know the identity of the finder. documented
  • Upload is over a normal TLS connection, so network metadata exists at that layer. conceptual

report = (ephemeral public key, AES-GCM(KDF(ECDH(e, Pᵢ)), location ‖ time))  ·  index = SHA-256(Pᵢ)

D · For a security architect

Two separate protections: confidentiality of the location (encryption to Pᵢ) and unlinkability of reports across time (rotating Pᵢ, so hashes change). Residual exposure: upload timing, the volume of reports per hash, and transport metadata, which depend on Apple’s retention practices rather than on cryptography. Research has analysed the design and some implementation issues research.

E · For a CISO

  • “Apple can’t see it” is a statement about content. If your risk includes metadata (who uploads when, from where), ask what is retained and for how long; the cryptography does not answer that.

Engineering notes

Scene 37

How the owner gets the answer

The owner’s own device fetches the sealed reports and opens them. What it shows is a last-seen point, not a live feed.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Grandma opens the Find My app.
  2. Her phone works out which secret words the tag used.
  3. It fetches the locked notes from the mailbox.
  4. Only her phone can unlock them.
  5. It shows: “near Platform 3, 14 minutes ago”.

The owner’s phone collects the locked notes and opens them itself, so it sees where the suitcase was, not where it is right now.

B · For a curious adult

The owner’s device knows the secret from which every past public key was derived, so it can recompute them, fetch the reports filed under their hashes, and decrypt them. The map then shows the most recent sighting with a time.

Where the analogy breaks: a moving dot on a map looks like live tracking. It is a series of separate, delayed reports; if no participating phone passes by, nothing new arrives.

C · For an engineering student

  • Owner devices derive Pᵢ and dᵢ for each time period from (d, SK₀), compute SHA-256(Pᵢ), query the relay and decrypt reports locally. documented verify against the current guide
  • Access requires an authenticated Apple Account session on an owner device. documented
  • Report frequency and latency depend on passers-by and are not published as guarantees. not publicly disclosed

D · For a security architect

The decryption boundary is the owner’s device set. The account service authorises the query; the relay returns ciphertexts. Compromise of the owner’s Apple Account and a trusted device is therefore the main path to someone else reading the locations — an identity problem, not a cryptographic one (Module 6).

E · For a CISO

  • Protect the accounts that own trackers like any account with location data: strong two-factor authentication, reviewed trusted devices, and a recovery plan.

How a lost tag is found

1 of 4

  1. The tag is alone. A suitcase sits at a station. Its tag has no GPS and no mobile connection, so it does not know where it is. It only whispers a changing identifier over Bluetooth.
  2. A stranger walks past. A passer-by’s phone overhears the whisper. It does not pair with the tag. It uses its own location services to estimate where it (the phone) is, and seals that location so only the tag’s owner can open it.
  3. The sealed report is stored. The phone uploads the sealed report, filed under a lookup value made from the identifier. The relay stores it but cannot open it.
  4. The owner opens it. Later, the owner’s signed-in device works out which identifiers its tag would have used, fetches the matching sealed reports and opens them locally: an estimated location and a time, not a live feed.

Scene 38

The whole exchange in five lanes

The same story as a sequence diagram: five parties, eleven numbered steps, and two trust boundaries.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. The tag whispers.
  2. A stranger’s phone writes a locked note.
  3. The mailbox keeps the note.
  4. Grandma proves who she is.
  5. Her phone fetches and unlocks the note.

Five helpers pass one locked note along, and only the last one can open it.

B · For a curious adult

Read the diagram top to bottom. Blue is radio, purple is encrypted, green is local or plaintext, grey dashed is permission. Notice which lanes never see a location in the clear: the tag (it never had one), the relay and the account service. The From Tap to Trace essay draws a shorter version as Figure 06.

Where the analogy breaks: a sequence diagram looks synchronous. In reality, steps 1–5 and 6–11 happen at different times and many times over.

C · For an engineering student

  • 1. Tag → finder: BLE advertisement containing Pᵢ. documented
  • 2. Finder: own location (GNSS / Wi-Fi / cellular) and time. conceptual
  • 3. Finder: ECIES-style encryption to Pᵢ (P-224, ECDH, KDF, AES-GCM). documented
  • 4. Finder → relay: SHA-256(Pᵢ), ciphertext, over TLS. documented
  • 5. Relay: store by hash. documented Retention period. not publicly disclosed
  • 6–7. Owner device ↔ account service: authenticated session. conceptual
  • 8. Owner device: derive Pᵢ, compute hashes. documented
  • 9–10. Query by hash; receive ciphertexts. documented Exact API. not publicly disclosed
  • 11. Owner device: decrypt with dᵢ. documented

D · For a security architect

Map controls to lanes: the tag (rotation, hardware), the finder (encrypt before upload, no identity attached), Apple services (storage, authorisation, metadata retention), the owner’s devices (key custody, device trust). The weakest boundary in practice is usually the owner’s account, not the cipher.

E · For a CISO

  • Use this diagram to assign ownership in a review: which risks are yours (accounts, devices, people), which are the vendor’s (relay, retention), and which no one controls (who walks past).

Scene 39

Finding it nearby is a different mechanism

The map says roughly where. In the same room, Find My switches to sound and ultra-wideband — a separate exchange.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. The map says the keys are at home.
  2. At home, Dad asks the tag to beep.
  3. His phone and the tag ping each other very fast.
  4. An arrow says: “8 metres, that way”.

Far away a map gives a rough place; close by the phone measures how far and which way.

B · For a curious adult

There are three different answers: where on Earth (from sightings, Scene 37), how far (from ultra-wideband ranging) and which way (ranging plus the phone’s own sensors). Play Sound simply uses a Bluetooth connection when you are within range.

Where the analogy breaks: the arrow looks like the tag is pointing at itself. The phone works the direction out; nothing in the tag’s broadcast says “I am to your left”.

C · For an engineering student

  • Precision Finding uses ultra-wideband with camera input, ARKit, accelerometer and gyroscope. documented
  • Requires an iPhone with ultra-wideband (iPhone 11 and later, excluding iPhone SE models). documented check current models
  • Ultra-wideband ranging is a two-way exchange between the owner’s phone and the tag, not a location broadcast. conceptual

distance ≈ c · (t_round − t_reply) / 2, with c ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s (two-way ranging) conceptual

D · For a security architect

Nearby finding is an owner-initiated, short-range interaction and uses different radios from the crowd-sourced network. Threat models for “someone nearby can locate the tag precisely” differ from those for “someone far away can read sightings”; keep them separate in reviews.

E · For a CISO

  • Precision Finding is a feature of the owner’s phone hardware. When buying devices for staff who must locate assets, check UWB support rather than assuming every iPhone has it.

Scene 40

NFC Lost Mode: a tap that shows a note, not a location

Someone finds the tag itself. They tap it with an NFC phone and read only what the owner chose to share.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. A boy finds a tag on a bench.
  2. He touches it with his mum’s phone.
  3. A page opens: “Lost! Please call.”
  4. The page shows only what the owner wanted.

Touching the tag shows a short note the owner left, and only works when the phone is right next to it.

B · For a curious adult

Tags like AirTag also contain an NFC chip, the same short-range technology as contactless cards (Module 2). A tap opens a web page about the item. If the owner has put the item in Lost Mode, the page also shows the owner’s message and the contact details they chose to share.

Where the analogy breaks: this is not how the item is found far away. NFC works at centimetres, so it only helps the person already holding the tag.

C · For an engineering student

  • Any NFC-capable smartphone can read the tag; the page shows the serial number. documented
  • In Lost Mode the owner can add a message and a phone number or email; “Notify When Found” alerts the owner when the item is seen. documented
  • For a tag found moving with you that is not yours, Apple’s guidance says the page can show the serial number and the last four digits of the owner’s phone number. documented verify current behaviour
  • NFC operating distance: a few centimetres (ISO/IEC 14443-class proximity). specification

D · For a security architect

The NFC page is a deliberate, owner-controlled disclosure. Review it as such: what the owner publishes (phone number, email) is visible to whoever holds the tag. Safety guidance for people who find an unknown tag belongs to Module 7.

E · For a CISO

  • If company assets carry trackers in Lost Mode, publish a role mailbox or switchboard number, never a person’s mobile number.

Setup messages and the keys the owner’s devices will use later. The message-level detail is not publicly documented.
Who learns what
Apple records that the item belongs to the account. The owner’s devices hold the keys.
What it is not
Not something a stranger’s phone can do: a tag already linked to an account cannot simply be added to another.

Source: Apple Support, “Set up your AirTag”.

Crowdsourced sighting documented

Who talks to whom
The tag whispers; any participating passer-by’s Apple device overhears; the sealed report goes to the relay; the owner’s device fetches it later.
Radio or link
Bluetooth Low Energy advertising (listen-only), then the finder’s internet connection.
How close
Bluetooth range: metres to tens of metres, depending on walls, bodies and interference.
Does anyone pair?
No. The finder only overhears a broadcast; there is no handshake with the tag.
What moves
A changing identifier over the air; then a report encrypted to that identifier, filed under its lookup value.
Who learns what
The finder learns nothing about the owner. The relay stores ciphertext and metadata. The owner’s device decrypts an estimated location and a time.
What it is not
Not live tracking: reports arrive when, and if, a participating device passes by.

Source: Apple Platform Security guide, “Find My”.

Nearby Precision Finding documented

Who talks to whom
The owner’s iPhone (or a shared user’s) and the tag, face to face.
Radio or link
Bluetooth to find and wake the tag; ultra-wideband ranging for distance and direction, combined with the phone’s camera and motion sensors.
How close
Nearby: within Bluetooth range of the tag.
Does anyone pair?
Only the owner’s devices, or people the owner shares the item with.
What moves
Ranging exchanges between the two devices: timing, from which distance and direction are calculated on the phone.
Who learns what
The owner’s phone learns how far and in which direction the tag is. Nobody learns a map position from this.
What it is not
Not a global location: an arrow and a distance, not a pin on a map. Requires an iPhone with ultra-wideband (iPhone 11 and later, excluding iPhone SE models).

Source: Apple Support, “Locate an AirTag with Precision Finding”.

Lost Mode NFC documented

Who talks to whom
Anyone who finds the tag, with an NFC-capable smartphone (iPhone or Android), and a web page from Apple.
Radio or link
NFC tap, then the finder’s browser over the internet.
How close
A few centimetres: the phone touches the tag.
Does anyone pair?
No. The tap opens a web page.
What moves
The tag’s serial number and, if the owner turned on Lost Mode, the message and contact details the owner chose to share.
Who learns what
The finder sees only what the owner chose to show. The owner can be notified when the item is found.
What it is not
Not distant tracking: NFC works only when touching, and adds nothing to the crowdsourced network.

Source: Apple Support, “Put an AirTag in Lost Mode” (verify current wording).

Scene 41

An owner’s life with the tag: what survives each change

Phones break, items get shared, accounts get lost. Ownership follows the Apple Account through all of it.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Dad’s tag belongs to his account, not his phone.
  2. He lets Mum see it too.
  3. His phone breaks; his new phone still finds the tag.
  4. If he gives the tag away, he removes it first.
  5. If he loses his account, he loses the tag.

The tag belongs to an account, so keeping that account safe matters more than keeping a phone.

B · For a curious adult

Think of the tag’s owner as an account, not a device. That is why a replaced phone just works, why giving a tag away starts with removing it, and why losing access to the account is the one change that can strand the item.

Where the analogy breaks: a state diagram suggests every transition is clean. Out-of-range removals and account recovery have edge cases; and what happens to keys at each step is not public.

C · For an engineering student

  • Item sharing: up to five people; recipients can see the location and play a sound; the owner can stop sharing. documented
  • Key material syncs among the owner’s devices through end-to-end encrypted iCloud Keychain. documented verify against the current guide
  • Remove Item; a reset procedure exists for tags removed out of range. documented
  • Viewing items on iCloud.com. verify current support
  • Key rotation, re-keying on share or removal, and erasure. not publicly disclosed

D · For a security architect

Model permissions, not devices: owner (full), shared viewers (location + sound), finders (none), Apple (store and route). The most consequential transitions are account compromise and account loss, both identity events. Shared viewers also receive location data; revocation is a policy action you must remember to take.

E · For a CISO

  • Add trackers to joiner–mover–leaver processes: who owns each tag’s account binding, who has been shared on it, and who removes it when a person or asset leaves.
