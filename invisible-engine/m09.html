Module 9 · What a quantum computer can and cannot do

Quantum computation, hardware and algorithms

Two entangled qubits, four drawers searched in one round, a number factored by finding a rhythm, and the cold, noisy machines that would have to run it all at scale. Then the honest link to security: which locks would fail, which only weaken, and why none has failed yet.

Scene 65

Entanglement: two qubits, one shared story

Two qubits, two gates, and a state that cannot be described one qubit at a time. Measure one and you know the other — yet no message can travel this way.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Two magic coins are made together.
  2. You and a friend each take one far away.
  3. You flip yours: heads! Your friend’s also says heads.
  4. Every time, they match — but which side comes up is random.
  5. So you cannot use them to send a secret message.

Entangled qubits always agree when measured, but because each result is random, they cannot carry a message on their own.

B · For a curious adult

A CNOT gate flips its second qubit only when the first is 1. Feed it a first qubit that is an even mix of 0 and 1 (made by H) and the pair ends up in a combined state: both 0 or both 1, with equal amplitude. Neither qubit has its own value until measured; what is fixed is the relationship between them.

Where the analogy breaks: magic coins suggest each coin secretly decided before it was flipped. Experiments that test Bell inequalities rule out that kind of hidden, pre-decided local explanation; the correlation is stronger than any such coins could give, which only shows when you measure in different bases.

C · For an engineering student

CNOT · (H ⊗ I) |00⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩) / √2

  • P(00) = P(11) = |1/√2|² = 1/2; P(01) = P(10) = 0. Computed with a two-qubit statevector simulation for the scene. computed
  • The reduced state of either qubit alone is maximally mixed: its own outcomes are 50/50 whatever operation is applied to the other qubit (no-signalling). theorem
  • Entanglement is a resource for teleportation, superdense coding and many algorithms; it never transmits information without a classical channel. conceptual

D · For a security architect

For security architecture the takeaway is narrow: entanglement does not create a covert faster-than-light channel, and quantum key distribution (a separate technology, not covered here) still needs an authenticated classical channel. Treat claims of “instant, untappable” links with the same scrutiny as any other channel claim.

E · For a CISO

  • Vendor material that says entanglement “sends data instantly” is wrong; use it as a signal to scrutinise the rest of the claims.

Scene 66

Grover with four drawers: making the right answer loud

Grover’s algorithm does not look in every drawer at once. It nudges amplitudes so that the right drawer’s grows and the others shrink. With four drawers, one round is enough.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. There are four drawers and one has a prize.
  2. A helper can only say “warmer” by flipping a sign.
  3. A clever trick makes the prize drawer get louder.
  4. After one go, only the prize drawer is loud.
  5. With many more drawers you need more goes — but far fewer than looking in each one.

Grover makes the right answer louder step by step, so you need far fewer tries than opening every drawer.

B · For a curious adult

Two moves repeat. The oracle recognises the answer and flips the sign of its amplitude — invisible to a measurement, because probabilities are squares. The diffusion step reflects every amplitude about their average, which turns the hidden sign into a larger amplitude for the marked item. Repeat about (π/4)√N times and the marked item dominates.

Where the analogy breaks: four drawers make it look like a one-step trick. For larger N it takes many rounds, overshooting is possible (too many rounds makes it worse), and the oracle must be built as a quantum circuit, which can be expensive.

C · For an engineering student

a ↦ 2⟨a⟩ − a ;   P(success after k rounds) = sin²((2k + 1)θ), sin θ = 1/√N

  • N = 4: start 1/2 each; oracle gives (1/2, 1/2, −1/2, 1/2); mean 1/4; after diffusion (0, 0, 1, 0). computed
  • Optimal number of rounds ≈ (π/4)√N; the quadratic speedup is provably optimal for unstructured search. theorem
  • Table values (rounds and success probability for N = 64, 1,024 and 1,048,576) computed from the formula above. computed

D · For a security architect

Grover’s bound is why symmetric keys are not “broken” by quantum computing: brute force over a k-bit key drops, in an idealised count, from about 2k to about 2k/2 sequential quantum operations, each a full error-corrected run of the cipher (Scene 72).

E · For a CISO

  • For long-lived data, prefer 256-bit symmetric keys; there is no need to replace AES itself.

Scene 67
Shor, intuitively: find the rhythm, then the factors

  
Shor’s algorithm factors a number by finding the period of a simple sequence. A quantum computer finds the period; ordinary arithmetic turns it into factors. Here with N = 15.

  
  

    
A · For a ten-year-old
  1. Some number patterns repeat like a song’s beat.
  2. If you know the beat, you can split a big number into its parts.
  3. A quantum computer is good at hearing the beat.
  4. A normal computer then does easy sums to find the parts.
  5. For the numbers that protect the internet, nobody has a big enough quantum computer yet.
Shor’s trick turns factoring into finding a repeating beat, which a big enough quantum computer could hear.

    
B · For a curious adult
Factoring is hard for ordinary computers. Shor noticed it can be turned into a different problem: find how often the sequence ax mod N repeats. A quantum computer can prepare a superposition over many x, compute the sequence, and then apply a quantum Fourier transform, which makes measurement outcomes cluster at multiples of 1/r. A few samples and some classical arithmetic give r, and from r the factors.


Where the analogy breaks: “hearing the beat” makes it sound instant. Each run yields one sample; some samples are useless; and for a 2048-bit RSA key the circuit is enormous, which is why resource estimates matter (Scene 72).

    
C · For an engineering student
f(x) = ax mod N,   f(x + r) = f(x);   N = gcd(ar/2 − 1, N) · gcd(ar/2 + 1, N) when r is even and ar/2 ≢ −1


    

  • 7x mod 15 for x = 0…11 and the 16-point Fourier transform of the period-4 pattern are computed exactly by the scene generator. computed
    • 

  • Outcomes y = 4 and 12 give r = 4 by continued fractions; y = 8 gives r = 2 (fails the check); y = 0 gives nothing — so runs repeat. computed
    • 

  • The same period-finding solves discrete logarithms, including on elliptic curves. Shor 1994/1997
    • 

  • Factoring 15 has been demonstrated on small devices with simplified (“compiled”) circuits; those demonstrations do not scale to cryptographic sizes. historical
    • 


    
D · For a security architect
Shor’s exponential speedup is what puts RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography on a migration plan. It applies to the public-key operations — key exchange and signatures — not to symmetric ciphers or hashes.

    
E · For a CISO
  • Inventory where RSA and elliptic-curve keys protect data that must stay secret for many years; those are first in line for migration (Module 10).

Scene 68
Gates and circuits: reversible steps, then one irreversible look

  
A quantum program is a circuit: wires for qubits, boxes for gates, read left to right, ending in measurements. Every ideal gate can be undone. Measurement cannot.

  
  

    
A · For a ten-year-old
  1. A quantum program is like a recipe on lines.
  2. Each box changes the qubits a little.
  3. You can always undo a box by running it backwards.
  4. At the end you peek, and the peek gives plain 0s and 1s.
  5. Peeking cannot be undone.
Quantum gates are reversible steps, but looking at the result is a one-way door.

    
B · For a curious adult
Gates act on amplitudes. Because they preserve total probability and are reversible, they are described by unitary matrices. Measurement is a different kind of step: it samples an outcome with probability |amplitude|² and leaves the qubit in that outcome.


Where the analogy breaks: “boxes on lines” looks like an electrical schematic. The wires are not conductors and the boxes are not components; on hardware, a gate is a timed pulse of microwaves or laser light (Scene 69).

    
C · For an engineering student
U†U = I;   H · H = I;   HZH = X


    

  • X, Z, H, CNOT and controlled phase are a standard teaching set; H plus CNOT plus a T (π/8) gate is universal for quantum computation. textbook
    • 

  • H · H computed numerically by the scene generator. computed
    • 

  • Measurement in the computational basis is irreversible and produces classical bits. textbook
    • 


    
D · For a security architect
Reversibility matters for cryptanalysis cost: quantum circuits for a cipher or for modular exponentiation must be built reversibly, which multiplies their size. That is one reason resource estimates are large.

    
E · For a CISO
  • When a vendor quotes “qubits”, ask how many gates of what fidelity, at what depth: the circuit, not the qubit count, decides what can run.

Scene 69
What a quantum computer physically is

  
There are no ripples in a tank. A quantum computer is either a very cold metal circuit steered by microwave pulses, or atoms held and steered by light — plus a lot of classical electronics.

  
  

    
A · For a ten-year-old
  1. Some quantum computers live in a giant fridge.
  2. It is colder than outer space at the bottom.
  3. Tiny metal loops there become qubits.
  4. Other quantum computers hold atoms still with light.
  5. There is no water inside — the waves are maths.
Real quantum computers are very cold circuits or carefully held atoms, not tanks of rippling water.

    
B · For a curious adult
A qubit needs two well-defined states that can be put in superposition and kept isolated from the world. Superconducting qubits are tiny circuits whose energy levels become usable only near absolute zero; microwave pulses rotate their state. Trapped ions use two energy levels of an atom; lasers rotate them. Both need elaborate classical control and readout.


Where the analogy breaks: drawings of “quantum waves” invite people to imagine water. The amplitudes are complex numbers describing probabilities, not physical ripples you could see.

    
C · For an engineering student
    

  • Dilution refrigerators reach base temperatures of around 10–20 mK; stage temperatures in the drawing are typical, not one machine’s specification. illustrative
    • 

  • Transmon-style qubits: a Josephson junction (thin insulating barrier between superconductors) makes the circuit anharmonic, so the lowest two levels can be addressed; control pulses are typically a few GHz. textbook
    • 

  • Trapped-ion qubits use hyperfine or optical transitions; gates are driven by lasers or microwaves. textbook
    • 

  • Neutral-atom (optical tweezer) and photonic platforms are also under active development. conceptual
    • 


    
D · For a security architect
Hardware platforms differ in gate speed, connectivity and error type, which changes which algorithms are practical first. For threat timelines, track demonstrated logical-qubit error rates and scale, not marketing qubit counts.

    
E · For a CISO
  • Build quantum risk on published, peer-reviewed milestones; treat roadmap slides as intentions.

Scene 70
Noise: why qubits forget, and what error correction costs

  
Qubits leak information to their surroundings. Errors pile up with every gate. Error correction can fix this, at the price of many physical qubits per reliable one.

  
  

    
A · For a ten-year-old
  1. Qubits are very forgetful.
  2. Heat and noise make them slip.
  3. The more steps you do, the more mistakes creep in.
  4. So scientists team up lots of qubits to act as one careful qubit.
  5. That needs a lot of qubits.
Because qubits make mistakes, many of them must work together to act as one reliable qubit.

    
B · For a curious adult
Two clocks matter. T1 measures how long a 1 stays a 1 before energy leaks away. T2 measures how long the phase between 0 and 1 stays well defined; without it, no interference. Every gate also has a small error rate, and errors multiply with circuit depth. Quantum error correction spreads one logical qubit across many physical ones and repeatedly checks for errors without measuring the data directly.


Where the analogy breaks: “forgetful” suggests a qubit simply loses its value. Dephasing can leave the probabilities of 0 and 1 unchanged while destroying the phase — the part interference depends on.

    
C · For an engineering student
P1(t) = e−t/T1;   P(no error in d gates) ≈ (1 − ε)d;   surface code ≈ 2d² − 1 physical qubits per logical qubit


    

  • T1 = 100 µs and T2 = 70 µs are illustrative; real values vary widely by platform and device. illustrative
    • 

  • The success table assumes independent errors and is computed from ε. computed
    • 

  • 2d² − 1 is one common surface-code layout (data plus measurement qubits); overheads for useful algorithms are typically far larger once logical operations are included. conceptual
    • 

  • Google reported logical error rates falling as the surface-code distance grew, on its “Willow” processor (2024). research verify
    • 


    
D · For a security architect
The relevant milestone for cryptographic risk is sustained, low-error logical qubits at scale, plus fast logical operations. Below-threshold demonstrations are necessary steps, not evidence that a cryptographically relevant machine exists.

    
E · For a CISO
  • Watch logical-qubit counts and logical error rates in peer-reviewed work; adjust migration urgency when those move, not when physical qubit counts are announced.

Scene 71
Where quantum helps, and where it does not

  
Quantum computers are not faster at everything. They help where a problem has a structure that interference can exploit — and only once the machine is good enough.

  
  

    
A · For a ten-year-old
  1. A quantum computer is like a special tool.
  2. It is brilliant at a few puzzles.
  3. For most jobs, like games and email, a normal computer is better.
  4. The two often work together.
Quantum computers help with a few special kinds of problem, not with everything.

    
B · For a curious adult
The known big wins come from structure: periodicity in factoring, the physics of molecules in simulation. Unstructured search gets only a square-root speedup. For broad classes of hard problems (NP-complete), there is no known quantum algorithm that makes them easy, and most researchers do not expect one.


Where the analogy breaks: a “special tool” sounds ready to use. Most of the wins in the table need a fault-tolerant machine; today’s devices are used mainly for research and small experiments.

    
C · For an engineering student
    

  • Shor: polynomial-time factoring and discrete logarithms on a quantum computer, versus sub-exponential best known classical algorithms. theorem
    • 

  • Grover: Θ(√N) queries for unstructured search, which is optimal. theorem
    • 

  • No known efficient quantum algorithm for NP-complete problems; BQP is not believed to contain NP. complexity theory
    • 

  • The N and √N curves are computed for N = 10 to 1012. computed
    • 


    
D · For a security architect
For security, the structured wins are exactly the bad news: factoring and discrete logs underpin today’s public-key cryptography. Everything else in the table is largely irrelevant to cryptographic risk.

    
E · For a CISO
  • Separate quantum computing as an innovation topic (simulation, optimisation research) from quantum computing as a cryptographic risk; they have different owners and timelines.

Scene 72
Quantum and security: which locks, and when

  
The bridge to the rest of the lesson. Public-key locks would fail on a large enough fault-tolerant machine; symmetric locks only weaken; and the replacements are already standardised.

  
  

    
A · For a ten-year-old
  1. Some locks on the internet use maths puzzles.
  2. A huge future quantum computer could solve some of those puzzles.
  3. That computer does not exist yet.
  4. Other locks just need longer keys.
  5. New locks are already ready.
Some digital locks would need replacing if big quantum computers arrive, and the replacements already exist.

    
B · For a curious adult
Public-key cryptography (RSA, elliptic curves) is the vulnerable part: Shor’s algorithm would recover private keys. Symmetric ciphers and hashes face only Grover’s square-root speedup, answered by longer keys. The open question is timing, and published estimates of what an attack would need have come down over the years — the reason to migrate early, not evidence of a current break.


Where the analogy breaks: “locks failing” suggests every encrypted thing at once. Each system needs its own analysis: what is encrypted with what, for how long it must stay secret, and whether anyone could have captured it (see the location-report analysis in From Tap to Trace, Part IX).

    
C · For an engineering student
    

  • Shor breaks factoring and discrete logarithms, including elliptic-curve, on a cryptographically relevant quantum computer; none is publicly known to exist. Shor 1994/1997
    • 

  • Grover against a k-bit key: about 2k/2 sequential operations in an idealised count; k-way parallelism saves only √k. theorem Collision search on hashes gains little in practice. conceptual
    • 

  • RSA-2048 resource estimates: ~20 million noisy qubits for ~8 hours (Gidney & Ekerå, 2019); under a million noisy qubits in under a week (Gidney, 2025), each under stated hardware assumptions. research Estimates change.
    • 

  • FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA), August 2024; post-quantum cryptography runs on classical hardware. standard
    • 

  • NIST’s draft transition timeline proposes deprecating and then disallowing quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms over the next decade. verify current status
    • 


    
D · For a security architect
Prioritise by data lifetime × exposure: long-lived confidential data exchanged with RSA or elliptic-curve key exchange today is the harvest-now risk; signatures matter for anything that must stay authentic for years (firmware, documents). Short-lived session data matters least.

    
E · For a CISO
  • Start a cryptographic inventory now; it is the prerequisite for every migration decision in Module 10.
    • 

  • Use 256-bit symmetric keys for long-lived secrets; do not replace AES itself.
    • 

  • Ask vendors for post-quantum migration plans with dates, not for claims of being “quantum-proof”.
