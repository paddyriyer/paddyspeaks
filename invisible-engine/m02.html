Module 2 · The card without a battery

Passive NFC and the anatomy of a contactless card

A child taps a card at a shop and asks how it works without a battery. The answer runs from a coil of copper to a cryptogram checked by a bank hundreds of kilometres away.

Scenes 09–17Prerequisites: Module 1, especially Scenes 02 (energy vs information) and 04 (induction).

How to read every diagram

  • Orange arrow — energy or power
  • Blue arrow — radio signal or physical wave
  • Green arrow — plaintext or local information
  • Purple arrow — encrypted or cryptographic
  • Grey dashed arrow — registration or authorisation, never data
  • Red dashed outline — attacker zone or hazard
  • Thick dark box — trust, hardware or organisational boundary
  • Dashed blue rings — illustrative field drawings, not visible waves
Scene 09

Tap at checkout

The everyday experience: a card that has no battery approaches a terminal and, a moment later, the screen says Approved.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Mum taps her card on the shop’s machine.
  2. The card has no battery inside!
  3. The machine makes an invisible magnet that wakes the card up.
  4. They talk for a moment.
  5. The bank says yes, and the screen says Approved.

The machine powers the card with an invisible magnet, they talk, and the bank decides whether the payment can go ahead.

B · For a curious adult

Three things happen in the second or so of a tap. The terminal powers the card through its magnetic field (Scenes 11–12). Terminal and card exchange messages, including a one-time cryptographic value computed by the card (Scenes 13–15). And for most payments the terminal sends that value onward so the card’s issuer can decide (Scene 15).

Where the analogy breaks: the screen’s “Approved” is the end of a chain involving the merchant, its bank (the acquirer), a payment network and the issuer. Some low-value payments can be approved offline by the terminal under rules set in the card; that is a policy choice, not the default picture.

C · For an engineering student

  • Carrier 13.56 MHz; proximity cards specified by ISO/IEC 14443. specification
  • Payment terminals are tested to work reliably only within a few centimetres of the reader surface (EMV contactless test volume, commonly summarised as about 4 cm). verify against EMVCo Book D
  • The exchange: field on → activation and anticollision (ISO/IEC 14443-3) → transport (14443-4) → EMV application selection and transaction (Scene 14).

D · For a security architect

The trust decision is not at the terminal. The terminal is an untrusted relay for the card’s cryptogram; the issuer verifies it. The terminal does have its own security role (certified readers, unpredictable numbers, offline data authentication in some flows), but “Approved” is ultimately an issuer and network decision.

E · For a CISO

  • For merchants: terminal certification and tamper protection are your responsibility; card authentication is the issuer’s. Map which party owns which control before an incident, not during it.

Engineering notes

Accuracy check (red team)

  • Does the scene suggest the card has a battery? (No.)
  • Is approval attributed to the issuer rather than the card? (Yes, with the offline caveat.)

  • NFC Forum, “NFC technology”.

Scene 10

Inside the card

Open the card. Most of it is plastic; the working parts are a coil of fine wire or etched track near the edge and a chip module.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. Inside the card is a loop of very thin wire.
  2. The loop is joined to a tiny chip.
  3. The chip has parts that make power, parts that talk, and a secret locked box.
  4. The secret never leaves the box.

A card is a loop of wire that catches power and a tiny chip that uses it to talk and keep a secret.

B · For a curious adult

The coil is the card’s antenna and its power supply: the reader’s field induces an alternating voltage in it. The chip turns that into a steady supply, recovers a clock from the 13.56 MHz carrier, listens for commands, answers by switching its load, and runs a small secure processor that holds the card’s keys and the payment application.

Where the analogy breaks: the block names are generic. Real payment chips integrate these functions differently and add security sensors (light, voltage, clock) and countermeasures.

C · For an engineering student

  • Card format ID-1: 85.60 × 53.98 mm (ISO/IEC 7810). specification
  • The coil and the chip’s input capacitance form a resonant circuit tuned near 13.56 MHz (often slightly above, to allow for detuning). conceptual
  • Dual-interface cards also expose ISO/IEC 7816 contacts; the same secure element serves both interfaces.
  • Coil-on-module designs couple a small module coil inductively to a larger card coil, avoiding a soldered connection.

D · For a security architect

The secure memory and crypto engine form the card’s trust anchor: keys are generated or injected at personalisation and are not meant to be exportable. Everything else on the chip — power, clock, communications — is attack surface for fault and side-channel attacks, which certified payment chips are evaluated against.

E · For a CISO

  • For your own access or loyalty cards, ask whether the chip is a certified secure element and what evaluation it passed; a cheap memory tag with an ID is a different class of product.

  • Are layout variations acknowledged? (Yes, frame 4.)

Scene 11

The reader makes the field

All the energy comes from the reader. Inside the terminal, an oscillator, a modulator, an amplifier and a matching network drive the reader’s coil.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. The machine has a tiny engine that wiggles electricity very fast.
  2. The wiggling electricity goes round a loop of wire.
  3. That makes an invisible magnet just above the machine.
  4. A card in that spot can catch some of its energy.

The reader makes an invisible, fast-flipping magnet, and a card held close can catch its energy.

B · For a curious adult

A reader (a payment terminal, a door reader or a phone in reader mode) generates the 13.56 MHz carrier, amplifies it and drives its own coil. The changing current makes a changing magnetic field; that field is both the card’s power supply and the reader’s channel for commands. The card does not generate a carrier of its own.

C · For an engineering student

  • Field strength for ISO/IEC 14443 proximity coupling: H between about 1.5 A/m (minimum the card must work at) and 7.5 A/m (maximum the reader may produce), unmodulated. specification, secondary summary
  • Matching network: transforms the antenna’s impedance to the amplifier’s and sets the antenna circuit’s Q (bandwidth vs efficiency).
  • Phones acting as readers produce weaker fields than countertop terminals, one reason phone-to-card distances are shorter.

D · For a security architect

Any device that can generate a compliant field can power and interrogate a card. Readers are commodity hardware. The security of a contactless system therefore cannot depend on “only legitimate readers can talk to the card”.

E · For a CISO

  • Physical controls on terminals (tamper evidence, certification, inventory) protect the merchant’s side; they do not stop other readers existing.
Engineering notes, accuracy check and sources

Engineering notes

Block diagram only; real reader ICs integrate oscillator, modulator, driver and receiver.

Accuracy check (red team)

  • Does the card appear to transmit a carrier? (No.)
  • Is the field visual labelled illustrative? (Yes.)

Sources

  • ISO/IEC 14443-2 field-strength limits, as summarised by Rohde & Schwarz application note 1MA113 and others.

Scene 12

How the chip wakes up without a battery

Follow the energy, stage by stage, from the reader’s field to a running chip.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. The magnet wiggles electricity in the card’s loop.
  2. A one-way gate lets it flow only one way.
  3. A tiny store smooths it out.
  4. Now the chip has steady power and wakes up.

The card turns the reader’s wiggling magnet into steady electricity, and that wakes the chip.

B · For a curious adult

The coil’s voltage alternates; chips need steady DC. A rectifier (diodes or transistors acting as one-way valves) flips the negative half-cycles, a capacitor stores charge between peaks, and a regulator holds the voltage steady and throws away excess when the card is very close. The chip also recovers its clock from the carrier, so it runs in step with the reader.

Where the analogy breaks: “closer is always better” is false. Very close coupling can detune both circuits; metal nearby, a second card or a phone case can absorb or distort the field. That is why the same card works at one angle and not another.

C · For an engineering student

  • Induced EMF: v = −N·dΦ/dt; for sinusoidal flux the amplitude is N·ω·Φ₀.
  • Available power depends on mutual coupling, Q of both circuits and the chip’s load; the chip’s current rises when it computes, which the reader sees as extra loading.
  • No calibrated watts are shown in this lesson: the figures vary by reader, card, position and chip activity. qualitative
  • Demo A, below, lets you move a card and see coupling change qualitatively.

D · For a security architect

Because power arrives only while the card is in the field, a card cannot run anything in the background; each transaction starts from a cold reset. That makes per-transaction counters (Scene 15) the card’s main memory of what has happened, and makes power-glitch fault injection a classic attack class that secure chips defend against.

E · For a CISO

  • Physical attacks on cards require possession; treat lost or stolen card reporting as a security control in its own right.
Engineering notes, accuracy check and sources

Engineering notes

Waveforms are idealised. A real rectifier output has ripple and the regulator a dropout; these are not drawn to scale.

Accuracy check (red team)

  • Is “closer is always better” refuted? (Yes.)
  • Are any calibrated power numbers shown? (No.)

Sources

  • Finkenzeller, RFID Handbook (power supply of passive transponders).

Scene 13

How the reader talks — and how the card answers without a transmitter

The reader speaks by dipping its field. The card answers by changing how much energy it absorbs.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. The reader talks by making its magnet weaker for tiny moments.
  2. The card notices the blinks and reads them as a message.
  3. To answer, the card takes a little more or a little less energy.
  4. The reader feels the difference — like noticing someone tugging on a rope.

The reader blinks its magnet to talk, and the card answers by tugging on the energy in different rhythms.

B · For a curious adult

Downlink: to send a 0 or 1, the reader modulates the amplitude of its field: Type A cards see short pauses (100 % amplitude-shift keying with modified Miller coding); Type B cards see a small dip of about 10 % (NRZ coding).

Uplink: a passive card has no transmitter. It switches a resistor or capacitor in and out across its coil in a rhythm — load modulation. Because the card and reader coils are coupled, the reader’s coil voltage changes slightly in the same rhythm, and the reader decodes it.

Where the analogy breaks: the effect is tiny compared with the carrier, which is why the card switches at a subcarrier frequency: it moves the answer away from the strong carrier so the reader can filter it out.

C · For an engineering student

  • Base data rate 106 kbit/s ≈ 13.56 MHz / 128; higher rates (212, 424, 848 kbit/s) exist. specification
  • Subcarrier f_c / 16 = 847.5 kHz; the reader detects sidebands at 13.56 MHz ± 847.5 kHz.
  • Card-to-reader coding: Manchester on-off keying of the subcarrier (Type A, 106 kbit/s) and BPSK (Type B), as summarised by independent sources. verify against ISO/IEC 14443-2
  • Some literature calls HF load modulation “backscatter”; strictly, backscatter describes reflected far-field UHF signals. This lesson keeps the terms apart.

D · For a security architect

Load modulation is detectable by anyone close enough with a suitable antenna, not only by the reader, so the uplink must be treated as public. Anything confidential must be protected at a higher layer. Most payment data elements are not encrypted on the air; the cryptogram protects against copying, not against reading.

E · For a CISO

  • Assume card-reader traffic can be recorded nearby. Controls should prevent it from being reused, rather than assume it cannot be heard.
Engineering notes, accuracy check and sources

Engineering notes

Waveform and spectrum are schematic; the sidebands are many orders of magnitude weaker than the carrier and their heights are not to scale.

Accuracy check (red team)

  • Does the card appear to transmit a carrier? (No: stated in frame 3.)
  • Is “backscatter” distinguished from HF load modulation? (Yes, in C.)

Sources

  • ISO/IEC 14443-2 parameters as summarised by independent sources (e.g. Rohde & Schwarz 1MA113); Finkenzeller, RFID Handbook.

Scene 14

Layers of agreement: from radio to payment

Contactless payment is a stack of standards. Each layer assumes the one beneath it works and adds one kind of agreement.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. First the reader and card agree how to talk.
  2. Then the reader picks one card, even if two are near.
  3. Then they agree how to send longer messages.
  4. Finally they do the payment.

Like learning to speak, then saying hello, then holding a conversation, a card and reader agree on each step before paying.

B · For a curious adult

Standards are layered so that each can change independently. The radio layer (14443-2) does not know about payments; the activation layer (14443-3) makes sure exactly one card is talking; the transport layer (14443-4) carries larger messages reliably; and the EMV application layer, defined by EMVCo and the payment networks, runs the transaction.

Where the analogy breaks: the sequence shown is a simplified Type A example. Real transactions differ by card type, network kernel and terminal configuration.

C · For an engineering student

  • Type A: REQA/WUPA → ATQA → anticollision loop over UID cascade levels → SELECT → SAK → RATS → ATS. specification
  • EMV: SELECT “2PAY.SYS.DDF01” (PPSE) returns the list of payment applications; SELECT AID chooses one; GET PROCESSING OPTIONS passes data requested in the card’s PDOL (amount, currency, unpredictable number…); READ RECORD may follow; the cryptogram appears in the GPO response or a GENERATE AC response depending on the kernel (EMV Contactless Book C-x).
  • Messages above 14443-4 are APDUs (ISO/IEC 7816-4 format).

D · For a security architect

The UID used for anticollision is a radio-layer identifier. Many payment cards use a random UID per session to avoid being a stable tracking identifier, but not every contactless card does; access badges and transit cards often expose a fixed UID. Never use the UID as an authenticator: it is broadcast in the clear and easily emulated.

E · For a CISO

  • Audit any internal system that authorises on a card’s UID alone (common in older door systems). That is identification without authentication.
Engineering notes, accuracy check and sources

Engineering notes

Message names follow ISO/IEC 14443-3/-4 and EMV terminology. Arrow colours: energy (field on), radio-layer signalling, application data, cryptographic payload.

Accuracy check (red team)

  • Is the sequence labelled illustrative and kernel-dependent? (Yes.)
  • Is UID-as-authenticator warned against? (Yes.)

Sources

  • ISO/IEC 14443-3 and -4; EMVCo Contactless Specifications (Book A, B and kernel books C-x) — see emvco.com.

Scene 15

The cryptogram: a fresh proof for every payment

The card proves it holds a secret key without ever revealing it, by computing a one-time value over this transaction’s data. The issuer checks it.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. The shop machine tells the card about the payment and gives it a surprise number.
  2. The card mixes these with its secret to make a special code.
  3. The code travels to the card’s bank.
  4. The bank, which knows the secret too, checks the code.
  5. Next time the code will be different, so copying it is useless.

The card makes a new secret code for every payment, and only the bank can check it, so a copied code is useless.

B · For a curious adult

A cryptogram here is a short value computed with a secret key over the transaction’s details. Because it includes the amount, an unpredictable number from the terminal and a counter that increases every time, it is different for every payment. The issuer, which can derive the same key, recomputes it and compares.

Where the analogy breaks: this proves the genuine card took part in this transaction; it does not make the payment private (Scene 17), and it does not prove the person holding the card is the cardholder unless a PIN or device authentication is also used.

C · For an engineering student

  • Cryptogram types: ARQC (authorisation request cryptogram, online), TC (transaction certificate), AAC (decline). specification
  • Session key derived from a card master key (itself derived from an issuer master key and the card’s identity) and the ATC; algorithm historically 3DES, with AES supported in newer specifications.
  • Input data typically include amount, currency, country, date, transaction type, unpredictable number and ATC, as listed in the card’s data-object lists. kernel-dependent
  • Offline data authentication (e.g. fDDA, CDA) uses RSA certificates so a terminal can check the card is genuine without going online.

D · For a security architect

Trust boundaries: the card holds a symmetric key it shares (by derivation) with the issuer; the terminal, acquirer and network carry the cryptogram but cannot create one. The terminal’s unpredictable number is what prevents pre-computation; research has shown that terminals with weak random number generators enable “pre-play” attacks. Offline authentication rests on an RSA certificate chain — a classical public-key dependency relevant to Module 10.

E · For a CISO

  • Merchants: keep terminals certified and patched; weak terminal randomness has been a real-world weakness (historical research).
  • Issuers: monitor counter anomalies and cryptogram failures; they are the signal of cloning or relay attempts.

Scene 16

A plastic card and a phone wallet are different credentials

Tapping a phone and tapping a plastic card look the same at the till. Underneath, they present different credentials and protect them differently.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. A plastic card tells the shop its real card number, plus a fresh secret code.
  2. A phone wallet uses a stand-in number instead of the real one.
  3. And the phone only pays after you show your face, finger or passcode.
  4. The bank still knows what you bought either way.

A phone wallet hides your real card number behind a stand-in and checks it is you, but the bank still sees the payment.

B · For a curious adult

Both credentials produce a fresh cryptogram for each payment. The differences are what number travels and what gates the key. A plastic card gives the terminal its real card number (PAN). A wallet gives a token bound to that phone, which is useless if copied to another device, and releases its cryptogram only after biometric or passcode authentication on the phone.

Where the analogy breaks: tokenisation applies to supported wallets and some card-on-file services; it is not a property of every plastic card. Wallet designs differ by vendor; Apple’s is described here because it is documented.

C · For an engineering student

  • Apple documents that payment transactions from the secure element carry a Device Account Number and a payment cryptogram computed from a transaction counter and a key, and that the secure element releases them only after the Secure Enclave confirms user authentication. manufacturer documented
  • Token service providers (typically the payment networks) map tokens to PANs under the EMV Payment Tokenisation framework.
  • Small plastic-card payments may not require cardholder verification below a regional limit; wallets verify the user on the device for every payment.

D · For a security architect

Trust boundaries differ: for plastic, the card chip is the only secure component; for a wallet, the secure element holds keys and the Secure Enclave (or equivalent) authorises use, with the token service as an external dependency. A wallet token limits the damage of a merchant breach (the token is device-bound) but adds account and device lifecycle risks: lost phones, re-provisioning, account recovery.

E · For a CISO

  • Corporate cards in employee wallets: include token suspension in the lost-device and offboarding process.
  • Do not describe either credential as anonymous in policy or customer communications.
Engineering notes, accuracy check and sources

Engineering notes

Scene 17

What contactless does not promise — and the layers that defend it

Dynamic cryptograms solve one problem: copying. They do not make payments private, do not stop relays by themselves, and are unrelated to a different risk, malicious NFC tags.

A · For a ten-year-old

  1. The shop and the bank can see what you bought.
  2. A trickster could try to pass a tap along a long way — banks check timing and limits.
  3. Someone very close could read part of the card, but not its secret.
  4. Sticky NFC labels can open fake websites — check before you type anything.
  5. Lots of small protections work together.

Contactless payments are protected in several layers, but they are not secret from the shop and bank, and you should still be careful what you tap.

B · For a curious adult

Privacy: a payment is a record shared by several organisations. Cryptograms stop a copied card from working; they do not hide who paid, where or for what.

Relay: short range is a property of the radio, not a security check. An attacker with two linked devices can, in principle, stretch the exchange. Payment systems respond with transaction limits, issuer risk scoring and, in some specifications, timing checks that measure how long the card takes to reply.

Malicious tags: an NFC sticker is a different object from a payment card. It cannot take money from a card; its risk is social engineering — a link that looks official.

C · For an engineering student

  • Mastercard specifies a Relay Resistance Protocol in which the reader times a nonce exchange with the card; independent researchers report it has been in the specifications since 2016 with limited deployment at the time of their work, and have analysed and implemented relay-resistant variants. independently demonstrated
  • Pre-play attacks exploited predictable unpredictable numbers in some terminals (2014). historical
  • NDEF (NFC Data Exchange Format) records can carry URIs that a phone offers to open; modern phones show the address first.

D · For a security architect

Separate the threat classes: data visibility (governance, not cryptography), relay (distance assurance), skimming (static data exposure; dynamic data protected), malicious tags (phishing), stolen cards (cardholder verification and limits) and compromised terminals (merchant security). Each has a different owner.

E · For a CISO

  • Owners: issuer (risk scoring, limits, monitoring), network (specifications, token service), merchant (terminal security), cardholder (reporting), employer (awareness about NFC phishing in the office).
  • Residual risk: limits cap damage; they do not remove it. Track contactless fraud as its own metric.
