Rāma Dayajūḍavē

The sāhitya belongs to Ramadasu; the musical setting may vary across bhajana and concert traditions. This composition is sung in Bhairavi, in Dhanyāsi and in Kīravāṇi depending on the lineage — all three are current, and no one of them can be shown to be Ramadasu's own tune. Ādi tāḷa is the one point on which the sources agree. Part Eight sets the versions side by side; Part Fifteen explains why this uncertainty is structural rather than a gap in the record.
Part Two

Who Was Bhadrachala Ramadasu?

Not a court poet who wrote about devotion. A tax officer who was ruined by it.

Kancherla Gopanna was tahsildar of the Palvancha paragana under Abul Hasan Tana Shah, the last Qutb Shahi ruler of Golconda. He collected the revenue of a district, and he spent a great deal of it rebuilding the Sītā Rāmachandra Swāmy temple at Bhadrachalam on the Godavari. He was arrested for it. Tradition puts his imprisonment in the Golconda fort at twelve years.

That biography matters here more than most, because it is audible in the songs. Ramadasu is not describing devotion from a comfortable distance. A great many of these kīrtanas are the record of a man who has already given everything, has nothing left to offer, and is not being answered.

Why his music sounds different

The distinctive thing about Ramadasu is who he thinks he is talking to. Rama, in these songs, is rarely a theological abstraction. He is addressed by turns as:

  • master and king — the one with the standing to command;
  • protector — the one under an obligation of office;
  • father — chakkani taṇḍri, “beautiful father,” a domestic endearment;
  • debtor — in Ikshvāku Kula Tilaka he itemises what the temple cost him, to the coin, and presents the bill;
  • judge — before whom he pleads his own case;
  • and sometimes simply someone who owes him an answer.

That last register is arguably what gives his work its unusual emotional force. In Paluke Bangāramāyenā he asks whether Rama's speech has turned to gold — meaning both precious and locked in a vault. It is an accusation. The tradition has a name for this: nindā-stuti, praise in the shape of reproach. It is not irreverence. Only an intimate has the standing to complain about silence; a stranger would have no expectation to be disappointed. The complaint is the theology.

His surviving work is predominantly Telugu kīrtanas centred on Rama, with some Sanskrit compositions also attributed to him and occasional lines in other languages. Alongside the songs stands the Dāśarathi Śatakam — a śatakam, a century of Telugu verse, addressed to Dāśarathi, the son of Daśaratha, traditionally counted at around 108 poems and still a set text in Telugu literary education. He is counted among the Telugu vāggēyakāras — those who make both the word and the music — with Annamacharya, Kṣētrayya and Tyāgarāja.

A stranger asks: “Will you help me?”
Ramadasu asks: “Why haven't You helped me yet?” That audacity is not arrogance. It is intimacy.

The whole song is in the second word textual

Ramadasu had every available petition open to him. He was a prisoner; he could have asked for release. He was ruined; he could have asked for restitution. He had built a temple with public money; he could have asked to be justified. He asks for none of these. He asks Rama to look at him — daya jūḍavē, cast a compassionate glance.

Notice the grammar of the request. Jūḍavē is not the polite imperative a subject would use to a king; it is the familiar one, softened by -vē into something between a plea and a coaxing. It is how you speak to family. The same -vē returns on brōvavē. Twice in one line he uses a form that presumes he has the right to be informal with God.

Bhadrāchala dhāmā — the epithet that is also a deed textual

Rama could be addressed here by any of a hundred epithets. Ramadasu picks the one that names the building he went to prison for. Dhāmā means abode, seat, dwelling — the place where a deity has consented to be found. So the line means: you, whose address is the temple I built. It is an epithet and a quiet piece of evidence at the same time.

There is a second thing in it. Achala literally means “unmoving” — a mountain is that which does not move. Ramadasu, in a cell, is addressing the Immovable One at the Auspicious Immovable Place. Everything in his life has moved; the hill has not.

A note on the text. Some printed and transliterated editions segment this line as bhadrāchala rāmā, “O Rama of Bhadrachala.” The better-attested reading — and the one the bhajana tradition sings — is bhadrāchala dhāmā. Sources differ; the sense of the address is not materially changed, and either way the line ends on sītā, which matters for the reason given below.

Why the pallavi ends on Sītā's name textual

The line opens by addressing Rama and closes by addressing Sītā. That is not a stray flourish — it is the beginning of a device that runs through the whole kīrtana, and Part Five returns to it. For now, notice only this: when the singer reaches sītā and loops back to rāma daya jūḍavē, the seam of the repetition reads sītā–rāma. The refrain manufactures a name that no single line of it contains.

The sequence is a chain of causes textual

Read the three verbs in order: daya jūchi → rakṣhiṁchi → mēlu. Having looked with compassion, having protected, then good. They are not three separate requests. They are one request stated as a chain of dependency: the protection follows from the glance, and the welfare follows from the protection. Nothing can begin until he is seen. Part Six is about why that ordering is the heart of the song.

Bhīma on the battlefield — and why that epithet, here traditional

Raṇaraṅga bhīmā is martial vocabulary, and it seems out of place in a plea for tenderness. It is not. The devotee is reminding Rama of a capability. The logic runs: you who are terrible on the field of battle — this is nothing to you. What would cost a lesser power everything costs you a glance. The formidability is invoked not to praise but to make the smallness of the request obvious.

There is also a scriptural reflex at work. In the Rāmāyaṇa, Rama's protection is repeatedly demonstrated by force — Tāṭakā on the road to Mithilā, the demons of the Daṇḍaka forest, the guarding of Viśvāmitra's sacrifice, and finally Rāvaṇa. To say you are terrible in battle is to point at a long record and ask for one more entry in it.

The word that is deliberately broken off textual

The anupallavi ends on jagadabhi — which is not a Telugu or Sanskrit word at all. It is a fragment. It becomes a word only when the singer returns to the pallavi and sounds rāma: jagadabhirāma, delight of the worlds.

This is the structural signature of the composition. The line is engineered so that it cannot be left — the sense is incomplete until the refrain comes round again. In a congregational setting, where the pallavi returns after every section, this means the text is physically built to circulate. You cannot sing this kīrtana once. Part Five takes up what that device means.

Rājīva daḷa lōchanā: why the lotus, and why the eyes traditional

Literally: lotus-petal-eyed. It is among the most common epithets in Vaiṣṇava poetry, and that very familiarity can make it slide past unread. It is worth slowing down, because the image is doing precise work.

The lotus petal is the traditional figure for a particular combination of qualities: it is soft, it is elongated and cool in shape, it is unstained — the flower grows in muddy water and takes none of the mud — and it opens of its own accord when the sun reaches it. Applied to the eyes, each of those carries over. The gaze is gentle rather than piercing. It is untouched by the sordidness of what it looks at, which is the theological point when the thing being looked at is a convicted embezzler. And it opens by itself, in response to light, not to force.

Which is why this epithet, of all epithets, belongs in this song. The pallavi has asked for a look. The caraṇam now describes the eyes that would do the looking — and describes them as soft, unstainable, and self-opening. Ramadasu is not padding the verse with a conventional compliment. He is specifying the instrument of his rescue.

Bhakta parādhīna: the most audacious idea in the song traditional

This deserves to be dwelt on, because a mechanical translation destroys it. Adhīna means subject to, under the control of, dependent upon. It is the word you would use for a servant in relation to a master, or a vassal to a king. Bhakta parādhīna therefore says: the Lord has become the dependent of His devotee.

Consider what that claims. The Supreme is by definition svatantra — independent, needing nothing. Nothing can constrain Him, which is what it means to be Supreme. And the bhakti traditions assert that He nonetheless chooses to be constrained, by love — that having accepted a devotee, He is no longer free to be indifferent. The independence is not lost; it is spent. He remains able to do anything except abandon the one who has surrendered.

This is not Ramadasu's private invention. It runs through the Vaiṣṇava tradition: Rama binding himself to Guha and Śabari and Vibhīṣaṇa, Kṛṣṇa allowing himself to be bound by Yaśodā's rope, the Āḻvārs' insistence that the Lord is the property of those who love him. Tyāgarāja works the same theme constantly. But placed here, in a prison song, it stops being a beautiful doctrine and becomes a legal argument: you have made yourself answerable to me. I am calling in that obligation.

And note where it sits in the line — immediately before bhava mōchana, the loosener of bonds. One half-line says you are bound to your devotees; the next says you unbind. The Lord who is tied is the Lord who unties. The juxtaposition is the argument.

Five rā-sounds and a hidden name textual

Read the caraṇam aloud and count. Of its four lines, three open on rā; the third line sounds it four times over — rāja rāja kula rāja rājārchita. Add rājīva and rājita and the syllable is everywhere.

This is not ornament. In a tradition that holds the Name itself to be the deity in audible form, an alliteration on rā means the name of Rama is being sounded continuously underneath the sense of the words — so that a listener who followed none of the Sanskrit compounds would still be hearing the syllable, over and over, for the length of the verse. The meaning is for the mind. The sound is doing something else.

Why Tāṭakā, of all Rama's victories interpretive

Ramadasu could have opened with Rāvaṇa. Instead he reaches for tāṭaka saṁharaṇa — a demoness killed by a boy on a forest road, long before the bow of Śiva, before Sītā, before exile, before the war. In the Rāmāyaṇa it is Rama's first killing, undertaken at Viśvāmitra's instruction on the way to Mithilā.

Two things follow. First, it establishes the earliest possible precedent: your protective work began before you had any of the equipment of a hero. Second — and this is how the commentarial tradition tends to read Tāṭakā — she stands for the crudest and most external layer of obstruction, the thing that blocks the road before any subtler struggle is possible. Grace begins its work at the coarse level, not the refined one. For a man in a cell, that is the relevant end of the scale.

Then the escalation: kōṭi rākṣhasa haraṇā, destroyer of a crore of demons. Hold on to that number.

The crore that answers a crore textual

The word kōṭi appears twice in this caraṇam, and the repetition is the whole point. You destroyed a crore of demons. I have written your name a crore of times. A number that describes divine power is answered by the same number describing human labour. It is the only arithmetic a prisoner has available.

And this is not a metaphor. Rāma-kōṭi — writing Śrī Rāma ten million times, in dedicated notebooks, over years, the completed volumes deposited at a temple — is a real and still-current sādhana, and Bhadrachalam is one of its principal destinations. When Ramadasu says rāmakōṭi vrāsiti, he is reporting a practice, not reaching for an image.

Look at what he chooses to offer. Not gold — he has been imprisoned over gold. Not merit, not austerity, not learning. Ink. The offering is deliberately worthless as property and enormous as labour: the one thing a man with nothing can still accumulate is repetitions.

And even that he refuses to take credit for. Nī tutō — “with your own help.” The crore of names was written with strength that was itself a gift. He presents the offering and in the same breath concedes it was never his to give.

Two past tenses, and what they imply textual

Vrāsiti — I have written. Vēḍiti — I have begged. Both completed. And sāre sāreku, again and again, time after time. The caraṇam is an account of work already done, over a long stretch, with no result yet reported.

The unstated sentence is audible underneath: and still nothing. It is the same silence that Paluke Bangāramāyenā calls gold and Ikshvāku Kula Tilaka times with the phrase ika naina, “even now.” Ramadasu is one of very few devotional poets who lets an unanswered prayer stay unanswered inside the song.

Dikku — the word that closes every other door traditional

“I trusted that you are my dikku.” The word means a compass direction and it means a refuge, and Telugu holds the two together so naturally that the translation has to choose and thereby loses something. The sense is roughly: you are the direction I face; there is no other bearing.

Formal doctrine has a name for this. Among the limbs of śaraṇāgati — the discipline of surrender — the sharpest is ananya-gatitva, the state of having no other recourse. It is important that this is not presented as a boast of exclusive loyalty. It is a confession of exhausted options, and the tradition holds those to be the same thing when they are honest. Ramadasu says the same thing in Ikshvāku Kula Tilaka as a bare question: if you do not protect me, who else is there?

Chikkulu — a knot, not a chain interpretive

Of all the words available for his condition, Ramadasu chooses chikkulu: tangles, snarls, the mess a thread gets into. Not fetters, not walls, not chains — a man actually in prison declines the obvious metaphor and reaches for something domestic and small.

The image changes what is being asked for. A chain needs a key, or force. A tangle needs patience — a careful hand, working slowly, not pulling. He is not asking to be broken out. He is asking to be untangled. Yogic literature calls the same operation granthi-bhēda, the undoing of the knot, and it is never described as violent.

And the request is negative: chikkula peṭṭaku, do not place me in tangles. Not “release me from” but “do not put me into.” It concedes, quietly, that the tangling is in the Lord's gift too.

The mudra, and the register that collapses with it textual

The signature — śrī rāmadāsuni, “Śrī Rāmadāsa” — arrives in the third person, as these mudras conventionally do: the poet names himself as though speaking of someone else, and in doing so hands the song over to whoever is singing it. See Part Fourteen.

But notice the language it sits in. Caraṇam 1 was almost pure Sanskrit — rājīva daḷa lōchanā, bhakta parādhīna, rājita vaibhava — the diction of court poetry and theology. Caraṇam 3 is plain, homely Telugu: dikku, makkuva, chikkulu, chakkaga, chakkani. Even the sound has softened into a chime of -kk-.

The drop in register is the drop in composure. He begins in the language of a man addressing a sovereign and ends in the language of a man talking to his mother — which, in the last line, is exactly what he is doing.

Why the final word is Sītā's, not Rama's traditional

Three caraṇams have addressed Rama. Every epithet, every argument, every past-tense accounting has been aimed at him. And the last line turns: chakkaga brōvavē chakkani jānaki — protect me well, O lovely Jānakī.

This is puruṣakāra, the principle of mediation, and Śrī Vaiṣṇava theology makes it structural: the soul approaches Nārāyaṇa through Lakṣmī, because the Mother's role is not to weigh the case but to plead it. Rama is the king, and a king must consider justice, precedent and desert — all of which Ramadasu has spent the song arguing about, and none of which is on his side. Sītā is under no such constraint.

So the dramatic shape of the kīrtana is this: a man argues his case before the judge for three verses, realises the case is not going to be won on its merits, and turns to the one person in the room who will simply ask on his behalf. That is why the song ends where it does.

Part Five

Beyond the Lyrics: What Is Ramadasu Really Asking?

A devotional text of this kind can be read at more than one depth without any of the readings cancelling the others. The traditional three-fold frame is a useful ladder.

A caution before we climb it. What follows are contemplative and Vedāntic readings — the way this song has been received and used, not a claim about what Ramadasu consciously intended. He was a Rama-bhakta in a cell, not a systematiser. The literal level is not a husk to be discarded once the “real” meaning is extracted; in bhakti it remains the truest level. The inner readings are offered as what the song opens into, not as what it secretly is.

The human level
Ādhibhautika ఆధిభౌతిక

A specific man, in a specific prison, in the 1670s, having spent public money on a temple, asks a specific deity for help. His offerings are enumerated; his labour is dated; his despair is real. Read only this way, the song is a document — and it should be read this way first, because the tradition never asked anyone to pretend the suffering was symbolic.

The divine level
Ādhidaivika ఆధిదైవిక

The jīva — the individual soul, bound and dependent — calls upon Īśvara for anugraha, grace. Here the prison is not Golconda. It is saṁsāra: the round of becoming, entered without consent and not leavable by effort. Bhava mōchana — the loosener of the bond of birth — is the epithet that makes this reading explicit inside the text itself.

The inner level
Ādhyātmika ఆధ్యాత్మిక

“Rama” is read as the divine presence within consciousness itself — the light by which anything is known at all. Then protect me is no longer a request for external rescue but a prayer about the mind: protect it from its own restlessness, its fear, its ego, its attachments — which are the jailers that travel with a man wherever he is sent. And look upon me with compassion becomes an appeal for the grace that removes spiritual blindness, since the one thing the eye cannot do unaided is see itself.

The chain — and why this song is built to circulate

Now return to the device noted twice in Part Three, because it belongs here.

The pallavi ends on sītā. The refrain returns to rāma daya jūḍavē. The seam reads sītā–rāma.

The anupallavi ends on jagadabhi, which is not a word. The refrain returns to rāma. The seam reads jagadabhi–rāma.

The song is stitched with the divine name at every join, so that the act of repeating it produces names that no individual line contains. This is the antādi principle — end becomes beginning — which Tamil devotion builds entire works on; the Abhirami Andhādhi chains a hundred verses that way. Here it is used in miniature and to a sharper purpose.

Consider what it means practically. A composition whose sense is incomplete until the refrain comes round cannot be performed once and set down. It is engineered for exactly the setting these songs actually live in — congregational repetition, where the pallavi returns after every section, for as long as the singing lasts. The theology of the Name is not stated in this kīrtana. It is built into its joinery.

How the readings on this page are labelled

Every close reading in Parts Three to Five carries one of three marks, so you can see exactly where the history stops and the contemplation begins:

textual — supported directly by the sāhityam; you can check it against the words.
traditional — supported by established theological or commentarial tradition, named where possible.
interpretive — a contemplative PaddySpeaks reading, offered as what the song opens into rather than as a claim about what Ramadasu intended.

The distinction is not a hedge. An interpretive reading is not weaker for being labelled — it is more trustworthy, because you know which kind of thing you are being handed.

Part Six

Dayā — Why Compassion, and Why First దయ

Ramadasu is not saying give me something. He is saying: see me.

That distinction is the composition. Everything a devotee might legitimately want — protection, release, welfare, liberation — is downstream of it, and the anupallavi says so in its own grammar: daya jūchi, having looked with compassion, and only then rakṣhiṁchi, having protected, and only then mēlu, good. Nothing begins until the glance.

The family of words

Dayā sits among near-relatives worth distinguishing, because the differences are where the meaning lives:

  • Karuṇā — compassion as a state, the quality of a heart that can be moved. It describes the one who feels.
  • Dayā — compassion as a relation, the tenderness of the greater toward the smaller. It requires two, and an asymmetry. You cannot show dayā to an equal.
  • Anugraha — grace as an act: literally “taking hold in favour of.” Compassion that has stopped being a feeling and done something.
  • Kṛpā — grace as disposition, the settled readiness to be gracious.

Ramadasu asks for dayā, and the choice is exact. He is not asking Rama to feel a general benevolence toward the world. He is asking for the specific, asymmetric tenderness of a superior toward a dependent who has no claim — and by asking for it in that word, he accepts the asymmetry. The request contains its own admission of rank.

Why bhakti begins in helplessness rather than certainty

It is worth asking why so many of the greatest devotional compositions open not from insight but from inadequacy. The Sanskrit and Telugu traditions are full of confident metaphysics; the songs people actually sing are mostly pleas.

The doctrinal answer is that surrender cannot be performed while alternatives remain. So long as there is one more thing to try, what looks like devotion is strategy. Śaraṇāgati becomes structurally possible only at the point where the devotee has genuinely run out — which is why the tradition treats calamity as an opportunity rather than an obstacle, and why a prison produces this song and a comfortable temple appointment would probably not have.

The human answer is simpler. Intellectual certainty does not need anybody. Helplessness does. And dayā is a word that only makes sense between two people.

We spend much of life asking the world to notice us.
Bhakti begins when we stop asking the world.

A caution about rāga and emotion. It is tempting, and wrong, to treat rāgas as having fixed emotional meanings. Bhairavi is not “the sad rāga.” The same rāga carries Śyāmā Śāstri's monumental Kāmākṣī svarajati and any number of brisk, celebratory pieces. What can honestly be said is narrower and more useful: Bhairavi affords a great deal of room — and in devotional performance that room is very often used for introspection, for tenderness, and for pleading that does not want to be hurried.

Why this rāga suits these words

It gives the singer somewhere to stay on “Rāma”

The composition opens on the name. Bhairavi's lower register is unusually habitable — the S–R₂–G₂ region admits slow, weighted oscillation without losing its shape, and the gāndhāra will bear almost unlimited dwelling. A singer can therefore treat that first rāma not as the opening word of a sentence but as an invocation held until it settles. In a rāga with a tighter lower register the same treatment would sound like mannerism.

The oscillated gāndhāra turns a sentence into a plea

Daya jūḍavē is, grammatically, just a request. What converts it into entreaty is the kampita gamaka — the shake — on the gāndhāra, which in Bhairavi is not decoration but the note's normal condition: G₂ is rarely sung plain. A wavering pitch on the syllable that carries dayā does something no lyric can do on its own. It makes the asking audibly unsteady. The word means compassion; the note means I am not certain you will give it.

The two dhaivatas build the plea and then let it fall

This is the most beautiful correspondence in the setting. Bhairavi rises through the higher dhaivata (D₂) and descends through the lower one (D₁) — so ascent and descent are not the same road. Going up, the rāga is bright and gathering; coming down, it passes through a flatter, more shadowed note.

Now recall the text's own shape. The anupallavi climbs into the martial epithets — raṇaraṅga bhīmā, terrible on the field of battle — and then subsides into jagadabhi, the broken word that must fall back into the refrain. The rāga's asymmetry gives that exactly the right gradient: the singer ascends on the brighter dhaivata to reach the epithets of power, and the return through the lower dhaivata is heard as surrender rather than as mere descent. The melodic architecture and the emotional architecture are the same architecture.

And it has the range for a text that changes register

Part Three traced the drop from the Sanskrit of caraṇam 1 to the plain Telugu of caraṇam 3. A sampūrṇa rāga with a wide tessitura can follow that: the courtly compounds can be set high and elaborately, and chakkani jānaki can be delivered low, plain and almost spoken. Bhairavi is large enough to hold both a throne room and a kitchen.

Why Sampradāya Bhajana matters to this question

It is easy to treat oral transmission as a lossy channel — the reason we don't have the “real” tune. That gets it backwards. The reason we have these songs at all is that the sampradāya bhajana tradition kept singing them for three centuries, in halls and courtyards, without notation and without needing any. Preservation and variation were the same process. A repertoire that is sung nightly by ordinary people survives; a repertoire that is written down and put in a library often does not.

So the multiplicity of rāgas is not damage. It is the fingerprint of a living transmission — evidence that this song was carried by communities rather than archived by institutions. Part Fifteen takes up what that means for anybody making claims about it.

Part Nine

Tāḷa and the Flow of the Words ఆది తాళం

Ādi tāḷa, in plain terms: a cycle of eight beats.

It is counted as a laghu of four (a clap and three finger-counts) followed by two drutams of two each (a clap and a wave, twice) — 4 + 2 + 2. Sung in rendu kaḷai (two kaḷai, each beat doubled), the cycle becomes sixteen, and everything has twice as much room; devotional kīrtanas of this kind are frequently taken that way.

Ādi is the most common cycle in the repertoire, and its evenness is the point here. Eight is divisible, symmetrical, unemphatic — it does not impose a character on the text the way a limping seven-beat miśra chāpu would. It gets out of the way.

On the eduppu

Where the song enters relative to the first beat — the eduppu — is not consistently documented across the sources consulted, and it varies between renditions in practice. It is worth listening for rather than asserting: a sama eduppu, landing squarely on the beat, makes the opening rāma declarative; a delayed entry, coming in after the beat, makes the same word sound like something the singer has been holding back. Both are sung. Sources differ on this point.

What the rhythm feels like

Not a procession, and not ecstatic nāma chanting — the cycle is too broad for either. Not a lullaby; there is too much argument in the text.

What Ādi at this breadth produces is closest to conversation, and then meditative repetition. The eight-beat cycle is long enough to let a full clause of Telugu unfold at a speaking pace, which is what a text made of addresses, arguments and past-tense accounting needs. The caraṇams in particular read as talk — I trusted, I bowed, I wrote, I begged — and a broad even cycle lets them stay talk rather than becoming chant.

And then the refrain returns, and returns, and the same evenness that allowed the conversation starts doing something else. This is where the antādi joinery of Part Five meets the rhythm: a cycle that is symmetrical and unemphatic is a cycle you can go round indefinitely without the shape of it wearing out. The tāḷa does not dramatise the plea. It makes it sustainable — which, for a song whose whole method is repetition, is the more useful property.

Part Ten

From Kṛti to Nāma

The same composition, sung in two settings, is doing two different things.

A Carnatic kṛti is a composed art object with a fixed architecture, offered by a performer to an audience. A saṅkīrtanam is something a congregation does together, standing, with cymbals and a drone and no soloist in the modern sense. Many of Ramadasu's songs work as either. But the second use is the older one, and these texts are built for it.

In concertIn Nāma Saṅkīrtanam
The rāga Explored. Ālāpana before the piece, the rāga's grammar laid out at length; Bhairavi in particular invites it. Held. The rāga is a vessel, kept steady so that attention can go elsewhere.
Elaboration Saṅgatis — successive melodic variations of the same line, each opening the phrase a little further. Niraval on a chosen line; kalpana svaras. Minimal. The line is sung as it is, and sung again. Variation would break what repetition is building.
Architecture Pallavi → anupallavi → caraṇams, shaped as a single arc with a planned climax. Cyclical. The refrain returns after every unit, indefinitely, for as long as the singing lasts.
Who sings One performer; the audience listens. The separation is the form. Everyone. The separation dissolves — there is no audience, only participants at different volumes.
The aim Bhāva conveyed. The listener is moved by the singer's understanding of the text. Absorption. Nāma-smaraṇa — the singer is changed by the repetition.

Neither is a degraded version of the other. But they answer different questions. A concert asks: what is in this composition? A bhajana asks: what happens to a person who stays inside it long enough?

Where this song sits in the paddhati

The southern sampradāya bhajana tradition traces itself through Bhagavannāma Bodhendra Sarasvatī and Śrīdhara Ayyāvāḷ in the 17th century and Marudanallur Sadguru Swāmigaḷ in the 19th, who fixed the order of the paddhati still followed. Its repertoire is deliberately multilingual — Marathi abhaṅgs, Tamil kīrtanais, Sanskrit stotras, Hindi bhajans and Telugu kīrtanas in a single sitting — and Ramadasu is one of its Telugu pillars. His keertanas are used in the guru dhyānam and Rama-bhajana segments, and his nāmāvalis in the divya-nāma saṅkīrtanam that follows.

The point of the repetition

It is easy to mistake congregational repetition for a lack of musical ambition. It is the opposite: it is a technique with a specific target.

Sung once, Rāma Dayajūḍavē is a petition — a man asking for something. Sung for the fortieth time, the asking has worn through. What is left is the name, and a rhythm, and the fact of still being there. The petition has not been answered; it has been outlasted. And this is precisely where the antādi joinery of Part Five does its work, because the song is built so the refrain must keep returning to complete the sense.

In Nāma Saṅkīrtanam the aim is not to sing about Rama.
Through repetition, the singer slowly begins to live inside the Name.
Part Eleven

The Power of the Name నామ మహిమ

This song offers the Name as its central act of devotion — śrī rāmakōṭi vrāsiti, I have written it a crore of times — so the doctrine behind that offering is worth stating plainly.

The southern bhakti schools hold that the Name is nāma-brahman: not a label attached to a deity but the deity in audible form, and therefore not less potent than the deity's presence. This is nāma-siddhānta, and it is a strong claim, not a devotional flourish. If it is true, then chanting is not a preliminary to something better. It is sufficient.

The related vocabulary is worth keeping distinct:

  • Nāma-smaraṇa — remembrance of the Name; the inward act, whether or not anything is uttered.
  • Japa — deliberate repetition, usually private, often counted. Ramadasu's rāma-kōṭi is japa carried out in ink.
  • Bhajana — devotional singing, generally in company.
  • Saṅkīrtana — the Name sung aloud and together, the congregational form. The distinguishing feature is that it is public and shared.
  • Tāraka nāma — the Name as ferry. The root is tṛ, to cross; a tāraka is what carries you over. The Advaya Tāraka Upaniṣad gives the etymology doctrinally: it is called Tāraka because it carries one across the fear of the womb, of birth, of old age, of death.

Read alongside his other songs

Ramadasu returns to the Name constantly, and the neighbouring compositions illuminate this one:

  • Tāraka Mantramu Kōrina Dorikenu — “I sought the Tāraka mantra and I found it.” The one composition in the corpus in the perfect tense. What completes his search is not a rescue or a vision; it is having been given the words.
  • Śrī Rāma Nāmamē Jihvaku Sthiramai — the Name “standing firm upon the tongue.” Sthira is the hinge: japa's recognised progression runs spoken → whispered → mental → ajapa, the repetition that continues without a repeater.
  • Śrī Rāmula Divyanāma Smaraṇa Chālu — “remembrance of the Name is enough.” Chālu is a flat, ordinary Telugu word: that's sufficient, stop there.

Ramadasu's Navaratna group — the nine most widely sung keertanas, performed as a set at Ramadasu Jayanti — includes Adigō Bhadrādri, Śrī Rāma Nāmamē, Paluke Bangāramāyenā, Śrī Rāmula Divyanāma, Rāmajōgi Mandu, Tāraka Mantramu, Hari Hari Rāma, Takkuvēmi Manaku and Kaṇṭinēḍu Mā Rāmula. All nine are in the collection index.

Set that whole cluster beside Rāma Dayajūḍavē and something clarifies. In those songs the Name is celebrated, prescribed, found, sold as medicine. Here it is spent — offered as the only remaining currency by a man who has nothing else, and offered with an admission (nī tutō, “with your own help”) that even that was on loan.

When the pallavi ends and loops back, listen to the join itself rather than the phrases either side of it. The last word of the line and the first word of the return make Sītā-Rāma. Once you have heard it you cannot un-hear it, and the repetitions stop feeling like repetitions.

5
The unfinished word: జగదభి in the text

The anupallavi ends on a fragment. Listen for the small suspension there — the phrase does not resolve, because it cannot until the refrain supplies rāma and completes jagadabhirāma. Good singers let that hang.

The Sanskrit wall of caraṇam 1 in the text

Rājīva daḷa lōchanā… rāja rāja kula rāja rājārchita. Stop following the meaning and just count the rā sounds. The name is being sounded underneath the sense — continuously, whether or not anyone is parsing the compounds.

The whole song has been addressed to Rama, and the final phrase turns to Sītā. Listen for how the singer handles the turn — most drop the volume and the ornamentation, and the line comes out almost plain. After three verses of argument, the sound of someone stopping arguing.

Part Thirteen

The Poet Is Talking to Rama

Read the song once more, quickly, and listen to the tone rather than the content.

Ramadasu is not standing before Rama with the careful vocabulary of a courtier petitioning a sovereign. He knows that vocabulary — caraṇam 1 is full of it, king among kings, worshipped by the kings of kings — and he uses it, and then abandons it.

By the third verse he is saying: I trusted you. I bowed. I wrote your name ten million times. Don't leave me in a mess. That is not how a subject addresses a monarch. It is how someone speaks inside a household.

He reminds Rama of a title (bhakta parādhīna — you made yourself dependent on your devotees). He produces a ledger. He notes, twice, in the past tense, that he has already been asking for a long time. In Paluke Bangāramāyenā he goes further and asks outright whether God's speech has turned to gold.

A stranger asks: Will you help me?

Ramadasu asks: Why haven't You helped me yet?

The difference is not politeness. It is standing. A stranger has no expectation and therefore cannot be disappointed; only someone who believes he belongs can be aggrieved by silence. The audacity in these songs is not irreverence — it is the evidence of the relationship. Which is also why the composition can end the way it does, turning to Sītā with a domestic endearment, without any change of key. He was never outside the family. He was arguing within it.

Part Fourteen

The Signature ముద్ర

A vāggēyakāra signs his work inside the work. The mudra is woven into the text — usually the final caraṇam — so that authorship travels with the song through purely oral transmission. It is a solution to a real problem: a name on a manuscript is lost when the manuscript is; a name inside the last verse survives as long as anyone sings it.

In this composition the mudra is in the penultimate line:

Caraṇam 3, line 3
చిక్కుల పెట్టకు శ్రీ రామదాసుని
chikkula peṭṭaku śrī rāmadāsuni
Do not leave Śrī Rāmadāsa in tangles

Ramadasu's signature forms cluster around his name and his place — Rāmadāsa, Bhadrāchala, Bhadrādri, Bhadragiri, and combinations of these. Here it is the plain rāmadāsuni, with the place-name already spent in the pallavi's bhadrāchala dhāmā.

What it means in this song

Two things are worth noticing.

First, the name is itself a claim. Rāma-dāsa means Rama's servant — it is not a personal name at all but a description of a relationship. Kancherla Gopanna signs himself with his status rather than his identity. So the line reads, literally, do not leave Rama's-own-servant in tangles — which converts the signature into the final argument. The plea and the byline are the same sentence.

Second, the grammatical person. He writes rāmadāsuni — the objective case, Rāmadāsa, in the third person — not me. Having spoken as I throughout (I trusted, I bowed, I wrote, I begged), he steps out at the last moment and refers to himself from outside.

That shift is what makes the song usable by everyone else. As long as the text says me, it belongs to a prisoner in Golconda. The moment it says protect Rāmadāsa, a hall full of people can sing it without pretending to be him — they are asking on his behalf, and finding, somewhere in the repetition, that they have been asking on their own.

Part Fifteen

A Note on Sources and Certainty

Research note — what this page does and does not claim

Ramadasu's repertoire has come down through manuscripts, printed collections, oral transmission, Harikathā and sampradāya bhajana. The corpus is not fixed, and neither are the musical settings. Specifically:

  • Attribution is genuinely unsettled at the edges. Several traditional biographical narratives of Ramadasu circulate, attributed to different authors, and they differ in how many songs they credit to him — figures in the region of 100 to 137 appear in different accounts, while the broader body of material associated with his name runs higher still. A song being sung as Ramadasu's is not by itself evidence that it is his.
  • The tunes are not his. Ramadasu left sāhitya. Systematic notation of Telugu devotional song came much later, and the melodies now attached to these texts were fixed by 19th and 20th century musicians — most consequentially by Mangalampalli Balamuralikrishna, whose 1950s settings are what most listeners mean when they say they know a Ramadasu song. Two Telugu films, Bhakta Ramadasu (1964) and Sri Ramadasu (2006), fixed some tunes further in popular memory.
  • Which is why rāga attributions conflict. The three rāgas in Part Eight are not competing claims about a lost original. They are records of different lineages, all of them real.
  • The text itself varies. The pallavi is printed both as bhadrāchala dhāmā and as bhadrāchala rāmā; transliterations of caraṇam 2 and 3 differ in detail between editions. This page follows the better-attested reading and says so where it does.
  • Legend and record are not the same thing. That Gopanna built the temple, was imprisoned, and was released is the traditional account and is broadly accepted. That Rama and Lakṣmaṇa appeared before Tana Shah as two young men and paid his debt in mohurs bearing Rama's stamp is devotional tradition. This page tells that story as a story, and does not present it as history.

Where sources disagree, this page says sources differ rather than picking a winner. No lyric, caraṇam, rāga, tāḷa or anecdote has been supplied to fill a gap. Where the evidence stops, the page stops.

On further reading: for anyone wanting to go deeper, the appropriate next step is archival and scholarly material rather than lyrics sites — the Sahitya Akademi's Makers of Indian Literature series, published editions of the keertanas with notation (including the Navaratna volume prepared under the guidance of Nedunuri Krishnamurthy), and the Bhadrachalam temple's own archival publications. Those were not directly accessible in preparing this page, and it would be dishonest to cite them as though they had been; the sources actually used are listed at the foot of this article.

Sampradāya bhajana

A different world from the concert platform, and the one these songs were shaped for. Bhajana-style rendition is less concerned with rāga elaboration and far more with collective absorption — steady tempo, plain line, the refrain returning and returning. If Part Ten's distinction between kṛti and nāma is to become audible rather than theoretical, this is where to hear it.

And a suggestion about how to listen
Two renditions, one song

Find the same composition in two different rāgas — Bhairavi and Dhanyāsi, say — and hear them back to back. Nothing in this article demonstrates Part Eight's argument as efficiently as the experience of recognising the same words as a different song. It is also the fastest way to understand what a rāga actually does, which is not to decorate a text but to decide what the text means.

PaddySpeaks Reflection

Perhaps the greatness of Rāma Dayajūḍavē lies in one word — dayā.

A man who had lost his office, his freedom and his reputation had every reason to ask for their return. He had built a temple; he could have asked to be vindicated. He was in a cell; he could have asked for the door. Instead the first thing out of his mouth is a request to be looked at — and the rest of the song follows from it as a consequence, because in his grammar nothing else can begin until that happens.

There is something uncomfortably familiar in that. We spend a great deal of life asking to be noticed — arranging the evidence, presenting the ledger, reminding whoever is listening of what we are owed. Ramadasu does all of that too, for three verses. And then, in the last line, he stops arguing his case and simply turns to the Mother.

After all the rāgams, tāḷas, manuscripts, disagreements, meanings and interpretations, Ramadasu eventually leaves us with something embarrassingly simple:

Rama.

Perhaps that is why these songs survived.

The scholarship helps us understand the words.

The music helps us feel them.

Nāma Saṅkīrtanam asks us to do one thing more:

Sing them until the distinction between singer, song and Name begins to disappear.
