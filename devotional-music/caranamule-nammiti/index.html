Caraṇamulē Nammiti

చరణములే నమ్మితి

When Repetition Becomes Surrender

Bhadrachala Ramadasu · Rāga Kāpi · Ādi tāḷa · Telugu
Prelude

Some Songs Are Understood Through Translation.
This One Is Understood Through Repetition.

You can translate Caraṇamulē Nammiti in about four minutes. The Telugu is not difficult, the images are familiar, and the theology is not obscure. Do that, and you will have the meaning of the song and almost none of it.

Because in each of its six verses, Ramadasu does the same strange thing. He arrives at a single word and says it three times.

వరదా వరదా వరదా అయ్యా అయ్యా అయ్యా చరణము చరణము చరణము పట్టితి పట్టితి పట్టితి అయ్యా అయ్యా అయ్యా దాసుడ దాసుడ దాసుడ

On paper these look like repeated words — the sort of thing a translator flattens into “O Lord, O Lord, O Lord” and moves past. Sung, they are not repetitions at all. They are stages.

This article walks the composition line by line: the Telugu, the transliteration, the literal meaning, and the devotional weight underneath. But the question it is really chasing is the one the text keeps asking. Why say a word three times? And why does the answer feel, by the last verse, like something other than emphasis?

Ramadasu repeats a word not because he has run out of vocabulary,
but because devotion has reached a place where vocabulary is no longer enough.

What nammiti is actually doing textual

The flat translation is I believed, and it is the least useful word available. English believe has drifted toward propositions — believing that something is the case. Telugu nammu has not. It is what you do with a person: you trust someone with money, with a child, with a secret. It presumes exposure.

So the devotional force sits closer to:

  • I have entrusted myself to You.
  • I depend on You.
  • I have nowhere else to put this.

And note the tense. Not I trust, not I will trust — nammiti, I trusted. Completed. Before the song has offered a single argument, the transaction has already happened. What follows in the six verses is not persuasion aimed at God. It is a man describing a position he has already taken and cannot now leave.

Caraṇa and śaraṇa — a resonance, not an etymology traditional

The song is built on caraṇa, foot. Everything it says about those feet is the vocabulary of śaraṇa, refuge. It is worth being exact about the relationship, because it is often overstated.

These are two distinct Sanskrit words with distinct roots. Caraṇa comes from car, to move or walk — the foot as the thing that goes. Śaraṇa means shelter, protection, a refuge. They are not the same word, they are not variants of one another, and no serious tradition claims they are.

What the tradition does is let them lean together. In devotional imagination the Lord's caraṇa is precisely where one takes śaraṇa — and the formal act of surrender, śaraṇāgati, is described quite literally as falling at the feet. The resonance is real and ancient. The etymology is not. Ramadasu is working the first and asserting nothing about the second.

Which gives the pallavi its plain reading:

Your feet are my refuge. Your feet alone.
The line that does not finish textual

Hold on to one structural fact before going further, because it governs everything below. Every one of the six caraṇams ends on the words nī divya — “your divine…” — and stops there.

That is not a phrase. It is an adjective with no noun, left hanging. It completes only when the singer returns to the pallavi and supplies caraṇamulē nammiti. Six times, the verse reaches for the feet and cannot name them without going back to the beginning.

This is the antādi principle — end becomes beginning — and readers of the first article in this series will recognise it: in Rāma Dayajūḍavē the seams manufacture Sītā-Rāma and jagadabhirāma. Here it does something simpler and more relentless. The song is engineered so that it cannot be sung once, and so that the refrain is not a return but a completion.

The largest deed, and then one word textual

The verse opens on the biggest thing in the Rāmāyaṇa's second half: the binding of the ocean, the causeway to Laṅkā, the moment the sea itself is made to submit. Then it names the hill where the temple stands — the temple Ramadasu built and was imprisoned for.

And then it stops describing and says one word, three times.

Why వరదా three times?

Here is the interpretive core of the article, so let it be labelled clearly before it begins.

What follows is bhāva reading, not grammar

Telugu does not grammatically encode a difference between the first, second and third varadā. All three are the same vocative of the same noun. Nothing in the text says “now plead.” The progression described below is a devotional and performative reading — what the repetition does emotionally, and what singers routinely make of it — not a claim about morphology.

First sounding
Recognition

You are the giver of boons. A statement about who Rama is. It could sit in a hymn of praise and nothing would be strange about it.

Second sounding
Appeal

Then hear me. Saying it again converts the description into a claim. Nothing has been added except that it is now being said to someone, twice.

Third sounding
The demand inside the praise

If You truly are Varadā — then bestow. By the third, the title has become an obligation. Ramadasu is not flattering; he is holding a name up and asking it to be honoured.

A possible rendering

Translating the triple flat — “O Bestower, O Bestower, O Bestower” — is accurate and useless. Something like this carries more of what happens:

“O Bestower… O Bestower! O Bestower — then bestow Your grace upon me!”

Note what has not changed: the dictionary entry for varadā. Nothing lexical moved. The pressure came entirely from saying it again.

Caraṇam 2 — అయ్యా

Word by word
TeluguTransliterationMeaning
ఆది పురుషādi puruṣathe primordial Being, the first Person
నన్నుnannume
అరమరaramarareserve, reticence, holding back, keeping at a distance
సేయకుముsēyakumudo not do, do not make
అయ్యాayyāLord, master, father, sir — see below
Textual variant — sources differ

Two things in this line are genuinely unsettled and are not being smoothed over here.

The epithet. Several Telugu sources read ādi puruṣa, the primordial Being. Others preserve or add ādiśēṣa — Ādiśeṣa, the cosmic serpent. Manuscript, print and oral traditions each carry small differences of this kind, and this one changes what is being invoked.

The direction of aramara. The word (అరమర) means reserve, reticence, a holding-back. Translations split on who is doing the holding: some render the line “do not keep any reserve from me”, others “do not doubt my devotion / do not hold me at arm's length.” Both come from the same word, and both are current.

Either way the plea is about distance rather than about a favour — which is worth noticing, since the next thing that happens is the word for Lord repeating until it stops sounding formal.

What ayyā actually carries textual

Ayyā is one of those words that a dictionary can only fail at. Depending on who says it, to whom, and how, it can mean:

  • Lord — the formal religious address
  • master — the one you serve
  • respected elder — deference to age or standing
  • father — in wide Telugu usage, plainly domestic
  • please — an appeal with almost no content, the way one says oh, please
  • O Lord! — an exclamation, closer to a cry than a noun

Which is why translating ayyā ayyā ayyā as “Sir, sir, sir” is worse than a mistranslation. It is comic, and the line is not.

The word breaks free of the sentence textual

This is the observation the rest of the article rests on, and it is visible in the transliteration if you look at where the word starts.

The line ends sēyakumayyā. That first ayyā is not a separate word at all — it is the ordinary vocative tail fused to the end of a Telugu verb, the way one says “don't do that, sir.” It is grammar. It is doing a job.

Then it repeats — ayyā, ayyā — and now it is doing no job whatsoever. The syllable has detached from the sentence that produced it and is standing on its own.

That is what these repetitions are. Not a word appended for emphasis, but a word extracted from its own grammar. Something that was a suffix becomes a call.

First
Address

Still attached to the verb. Formal, structural, almost automatic — the politeness particle of a request.

Second
Appeal

Free of the sentence now, and therefore aimed. The word has stopped modifying a request and become one.

Third
Something close to a cry

No grammar left to hold it up at all. What remains is the sound one makes toward a person one is certain is there.

A possible rendering

“My Lord… my Lord! O my Lord, surely You hear me?”

Theology is turning into intimacy in the space of three syllables. The verse opened by calling him ādi puruṣa, the primordial Being — about as abstract as address gets — and closes on the word a Telugu child uses for a father.

Ahalyā, briefly and carefully traditional

Ahalyā, wife of the sage Gautama, is cursed and left in the forest hermitage — in the telling this verse assumes, as a stone. When Rama comes to the hermitage on the road to Mithilā, his presence and the touch of his feet restore her.

The details differ across recensions and retellings — whether she is literally stone or invisible, how the curse is framed, what her culpability is taken to be — and those differences are real and much argued over. This song does not enter that argument, and neither will this page. What Ramadasu needs is only the shape: something inert was made living by contact with those feet.

Not naming — pointing textual

Notice the grammar of the verse. It does not say “you who made the stone a woman” and then address Rama. It builds a long relative clause and lands on a noun: caraṇamu, the foot. And then it says the noun twice more.

The effect is not description. It is a finger.

“Those feet. Those very feet. Those feet.”

And with each repetition the object gets nearer. The first caraṇamu is the one in the Ahalyā story, historical and far away. The third is the one the singer is claiming to have hold of right now. Nothing in the words moved it closer. Only the saying did.

The argument he is actually making interpretive

Read the first and third caraṇams together and a case is being built. Verse 1: those feet bound an ocean. Verse 3: those feet raised a stone into a living woman. The unstated third line writes itself:

“If those feet could restore Ahalyā — can they not restore me?”

This is very characteristic of him. Ramadasu argues with Rama by citing Rama's own record back to him. The squirrel of the Setu appears the same way in Paluke Bangāramāyenā — not as a charming story but as a precedent already ruled on. The ten avatāras of Takkuvēmi Manaku are ten pieces of evidence in sequence.

There is something faintly, affectionately legal about it — a devotee assembling case law and presenting it to the judge who set it. He had, after all, spent his working life as a revenue officer justifying accounts. But the humour should not be pressed too far: the man doing this was in prison, and the argument was not rhetorical to him.

Part Six

Ādhāra — not comfort, but load-bearing textual

Ādhāra is a structural word. It means the base, the foundation, the thing that holds something else up — the sense in which a pillar is the ādhāra of a roof. It is not the vocabulary of consolation. It is the vocabulary of weight-bearing.

So the claim is not your feet comfort me. It is: your feet are the thing preventing my collapse, and there is no second one.

Paṭṭiti — the verb that changes the song textual

Every other repeated word in this composition is something Ramadasu calls Rama — varadā, ayyā, caraṇamu, dāsuḍa. This one is different. It is something Ramadasu did.

And it is a physical verb. Paṭṭu is to seize, to catch, to take hold — what a hand does. Not touching. Not approaching. Gripping.

The image underneath is unmistakable once you see it. A drowning man does not touch a rope. He does not reverently place his palms on it. He closes his fist around it and does not open his hand again. That is the difference between reverence and ādhāra.

First
I took hold

The act, reported. A decision that has been made.

Second
I am holding on

The past tense starts behaving like a present one. Saying it again makes it ongoing.

Third
I will not let go

By the third the verb has stopped reporting and started insisting. There is no second support to move to, so the only thing left to do with the hand is keep it closed.

The repetition imitates the thing it describes interpretive

There is a small, satisfying rightness in this verse that the others do not have. Repeating a word for gripping is itself a kind of gripping — the mouth refusing to move on, the way a hand refuses to open. The form and the content are doing the same thing.

Whether Ramadasu planned that, nobody can say. It is marked interpretive for exactly that reason. But it is the one place in the composition where the device stops being a way of intensifying meaning and briefly becomes the meaning.

A note on veyyāru

The literal arithmetic — a thousand and six — is real but beside the point. Telugu, like most languages, has round numbers it uses to mean very many, and veyyāru vidhamula is one of them. Rendering it as “in one thousand and six ways” is technically defensible and tonally wrong: it makes a man in anguish sound like he has been keeping a tally.

The honest translation is the idiomatic one: in countless ways, in every way I could think of.

The sound of having tried everything textual

This verse carries an exhaustion the others do not. It is not asking for something new; it is reporting the history of the asking. I have called in every way there is.

Read against the other songs in this collection, that is a familiar note. It is the same silence that Paluke Bangāramāyenā accuses of having turned to gold, and the same duration that Ikshvāku Kula Tilaka measures with two small words — ika naina, even now.

And once more the syllable comes loose from its grammar: the line ends rāvayyā — come, Lord — and then ayyā, ayyā continue with nothing left to attach to.

Where the words are going interpretive

Set the five verses so far in order and something becomes visible that no single verse shows.

request→ persuasion→ pleading→ exhaustion→ a cry

The content of each verse is thinner than the one before it. Verse 1 had an ocean in it. Verse 3 had a story. This one has almost nothing — a report that asking has happened, and then the word ayyā with no sentence around it.

Language is not failing here. It is being set down, a piece at a time, the way someone running drops what they are carrying.

Part Eight

The signature verse — and the place where the signature comes apart.

Nannu ēlu — asking to be ruled textual

Ēlu is a sovereign's verb. It is what a king does to a country: rule, govern, hold dominion over. Applied to a person it softens into take me into your keeping, look after me — but the root sense of authority never quite leaves.

So the request in the final verse is not for rescue, or for a boon, or even for an answer. It is to be governed. Ramadasu is asking to be relieved of jurisdiction over himself.

The signature dissolves into the word textual

Now look carefully at what happens in the last line, because it is the most remarkable thing in the composition and it is easy to read straight past.

The mudra — the composer's signature, the mark that identifies the song as his — is Bhadrāchala Rāmadāsuḍa. That is the convention: a vāggēyakāra names himself in the final verse so that authorship survives oral transmission.

And the word that then repeats is not a new word. It is the last three syllables of his own name.

Bhadrāchala Rāmadāsuḍa
—dāsuḍa
—dāsuḍa

The place-name goes. The god's name goes. What is left standing, said twice more into the silence, is the ordinary Telugu word for servant.

Ramadasu does not sign the song and then say something else. His name comes apart, and the residue is a common noun. This is the same device as caraṇams 2 and 5 — a syllable cut loose from the word that produced it — but here what it is cut loose from is his own identity.

First
I am Your servant

Still attached to the name. A description of a role, and a conventional signature.

Second
I am only Your servant

Free of the name now. The role has stopped being one fact among others about him.

Third
I belong to You

Not a description at all any more. There is nothing else left in the sentence to be.

A man whose name was already a surrender traditional

Worth remembering who is singing. He was born Kancherla Gopanna. The name he is known by — the name in this signature — is Rāmadāsa: Rama's servant. It is not a personal name at all but a statement of relationship, adopted in place of one.

Which makes the last line quietly circular. A man who had already replaced his given name with the word servant reaches the end of a song about surrender, signs it, and then lets even that name fall away until only the word remains.

Part Nine

The Architecture of Repetition

Six verses, six repeated words, one movement.

Now read the left column downward and watch the grammatical person move.

  • Verse 1 — Ramadasu describes Rama. Third person: he who bound the ocean.
  • Verses 2 and 3 — he addresses Rama, and points at his feet.
  • Verse 4 — he takes hold of Rama. The first verb he performs himself.
  • Verse 5 — he has nothing left but the calling.
  • Verse 6 — the subject is no longer Rama at all. It is what Ramadasu is.

The song begins as theology and ends as identity. And the repeated words are the rungs of the ladder down.

Rama grows larger in the song while Ramadasu becomes smaller. That is the movement of śaraṇāgati
Part Ten

Why Repetition Works Musically కాపి

A word repeated in speech is a word repeated. A word repeated in Carnatic music is something else, because the second sounding does not have to be the same sounding.

Repetition in a kīrtana opens room that a single statement does not have:

  • Sangati — successive melodic variations of the same phrase, each opening it a little further. The classic engine of Carnatic elaboration, and it needs a repeated line to work on.
  • Gamaka — the oscillations and slides that give a note its character. The same syllable can be plain on one sounding and heavily inflected on the next.
  • Kālapramāṇa — the handling of tempo and space. A word can be given more room the second time simply by slowing around it.
  • Placement — where in the tāḷa cycle the word lands, and therefore how much weight it carries.
  • Register — the same word taken higher is not the same word.

So a singer has a real instrument here. The text supplies three identical words; the music supplies three different ones.

అయ్యా…recognition
అయ్యా!…appeal
అయ్యా!!…surrender

Told apart fromCarnatic Kāpi is not Hindustani Kāfi. The names are cognate; the rāgas are not. Hindustani Kāfi corresponds far more closely to Kharaharapriya itself. Confusing the two is among the commonest errors written about this song.

A caution about rāga and emotion. It would be easy, and wrong, to say that Kāpi means tenderness or pathos. Rāgas do not carry fixed emotional meanings; Kāpi is also perfectly at home in a brisk tillānā. What can be said honestly is narrower: in many devotional compositions and performance traditions, Kāpi lends itself readily to tenderness, yearning, intimacy and warmth — and that is a statement about how it has been used, not a property of the scale.

Why it suits these particular words

With that caveat in place, the pairing does something specific. Kāpi's borrowed notes — the raised gāndhāra and nishāda — are characteristically touched and left rather than settled on. The rāga brightens for an instant and falls back.

That is exactly the behaviour a repeated word needs. A singer can let the first ayyā sit plain, brush the second with an anya swara, and leave the third bare again — and the word will have changed temperature three times without changing at all. Kāpi gives a performer a way to make identical syllables non-identical, which is the whole technical problem this composition sets.

Part Eleven

Repetition as a Spiritual Method

In ordinary prose, saying a thing twice is a fault. In bhakti it is the technique.

What Ramadasu is doing here has close relatives, and naming them helps:

  • Nāma-japa — the deliberate repetition of the Name, counted or uncounted. Not a way of informing God, and not a way of persuading Him.
  • Mantra repetition — where the sound is held to work by being sounded, not by being parsed.
  • Calling a person you love — the plainest analogue, and the truest. Nobody says a name twice because the first was inaudible.
  • A child calling for a parent — where the repetition is not a request for information but an assertion of relationship. The content is: I am still here and you are still mine.

All four share a feature. The repetition does not add information. It changes the relation between the one speaking and the one addressed.

And there is a direction of travel in this song that is worth stating plainly. The closer the devotee gets to surrender, the fewer words he needs. At the start of the composition there are descriptions — oceans, causeways, a hermitage, a stone becoming a woman. By the end there is:

అయ్యా చరణము దాసుడ

Three words, and none of them says anything new. That is prayer moving out of language and into relationship — which is what the bhajana hall is for, and why a congregation can sing one line for twenty minutes without anybody feeling that it has been repeated.

Part Twelve

A Note on “Esoteric Meaning”

What this page is not going to claim

Readers of devotional literature will recognise a certain temptation. A word appears three times; surely the three must stand for something — the three guṇas, the three levels of speech, some tantric count, a code laid in by a saint for those with ears to hear.

Nothing in this text supports that, and reaching for it would cheapen what is actually happening. There is no evidence that Ramadasu encoded a system in these triples, and this page will not invent one.

The subtlety is elsewhere, and it is not lesser for being simpler. The repetitions are bhāva intensifiers. The lexical content of ayyā is identical on all three soundings. What changes is everything around it:

  • the melodic treatment it receives
  • the context the preceding verses have built
  • the breath it is delivered on
  • the emphasis and placement the singer gives it
  • the listener's expectation, now that it has been heard twice
  • and the distance — steadily closing — between the one calling and the one called

So the meaning that changes is not the semantic one. It is the experiential one.

The dictionary has not changed. The heart has.

This distinction matters for reasons beyond this one song. A page that claims hidden codes everywhere eventually cannot be trusted about anything, including the things it has right. Saying plainly “this is a devotional and performative reading, not a decoded secret” costs nothing and buys the reader a way to tell the difference.

It is also, as it happens, the more interesting claim. A hidden code would make the song a puzzle with a solution. What is actually going on is stranger: a word that means exactly what it meant, said again, and arriving somewhere else.

Part Thirteen

Closing Reflection

Ramadasu begins by saying: I trust Your feet.

By the end of the composition, trust has become something harder to name.

The Lord is no longer only Varadā, the giver of boons — a title, a function, a thing one might say about him in his absence. He has become Ayyā: the word a Telugu child uses for a father, said three times to someone standing close enough to hear it.

His feet are no longer a theological symbol. In verse 4 they are ādhāra — load-bearing, the thing between the singer and collapse — and a hand has closed around them.

And Ramadasu is no longer a poet explaining devotion. In the final line his own name comes apart in his mouth. The Bhadrāchala goes; the Rāma goes; and what remains is simply:

దాసుడ దాసుడ దాసుడ

A servant who refuses to let go.

Perhaps that is why Ramadasu repeats himself.
Not because he has nothing more to say — but because, at the deepest point of devotion, there is nothing more that needs to be said.

Literal translation vs interpretive reading

Two different kinds of claim appear on this page and they are marked differently throughout.

The word-by-word tables and the plain English renderings are literal translation. They can be checked against a dictionary and against the sources above, and where they are uncertain the page says so.

The three-stage readings of each repetition — recognition → appeal → cry, and so on — are interpretive and devotional. Telugu grammar does not distinguish the first varadā from the third. That progression is a reading of what the repetition does emotionally and of what performers make of it. It is offered as such, never as morphology.
