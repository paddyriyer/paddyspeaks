The poem

A hundred verses to the goddess Abhirami in the temple town of Thirukkadaiyur, composed in Tamil by the devotee remembered as Abhirami Bhattar. They are verses of address rather than of doctrine: he is talking to Her throughout, and much of what the poem knows about fear, debt, illness and attachment it knows from the inside.

The episode

Tradition holds that Bhattar, absorbed in the Mother, told a visiting king it was a full-moon night when it was in fact the new moon, and that he then sang these verses until what he had said turned out to be so. The story is told to explain the poem’s intensity. It is not the reason to read it.

The form

Antham is end; ādi is beginning. In an anthadhi the closing word of one verse opens the next, and the hundredth returns to the first. Nothing is left hanging and nothing concludes — the form itself is a statement that contemplation is unbroken.

From Need to Knowledge

A person may first come to Abhirami asking for health, peace, education, money, protection or relief from difficulty. There is nothing improper in that, and the poem never says there is.

But over a hundred verses Bhattar does something quieter. The object of the prayer keeps shifting. It begins as

“Mother, solve my problem.”

and slowly becomes

“Mother, let me recognise You as the ground beneath both the problem and the person having it.”

That movement — from seeking the Mother’s gifts to seeking the Mother — is the spine of the whole work. It is also why the verses about illness and debt are not, in the end, about illness and debt.
