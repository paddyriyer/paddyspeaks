सन्ध्यावन्दनम्Sandhyāvandanam

The daily rite of the three junctions of the day, step by step, for the morning, midday and evening services — every Vedic mantra verified against the Taittirīya Saṃhitā, Brāhmaṇa and Āraṇyaka, every other text against two printed prayogas, all of it divided into words and translated.

Sandhyāvandanam is the worship performed at the sandhyās, the junctions of the day: dawn, noon and dusk. This edition gives the rite as it is recited in the Kṛṣṇa Yajurveda Taittirīya tradition of the Āpastamba sūtra, in the Smārta form used in Tamil Nadu. Its Vedic mantras come from three layers of the Taittirīya canon: the Saṃhitā (āpo hi ṣṭhā, the Gāyatrī, the hymns to Mitra, Savitṛ and Varuṇa), the Brāhmaṇa (drupadād iva), and the Āraṇyaka — above all its tenth book, the Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad, which holds the prāṇāyāma mantra, the three prāśana mantras, the invocation and farewell of the Gāyatrī, and much else. Around them the rite sets traditional texts that are not Vedic: the names of Viṣṇu, the saṅkalpa, the viniyogas, the meditations and the closing verses. Each is labelled for what it is.

Why the rite exists: the Āraṇyaka’s account

The Taittirīya Āraṇyaka gives the reason for the water offered at the sandhyā (TA 2.2). Rākṣasas, it says, once performed fierce austerities, and Prajāpati offered them a boon; they chose to have the Sun as their opponent in battle. So they fight the Sun from its rising until its setting. They are quieted only by water over which the Gāyatrī has been spoken. That is why those who know the Veda face east at the sandhyā and throw up water consecrated by the Gāyatrī: the water becomes a thunderbolt and casts the rākṣasas away onto an island. By walking round sunwise, the passage continues, they shake off evil; and the knowing brāhmaṇa who meditates on the rising and setting Sun, thinking ‘that Sun is Brahman’, attains every good, and being Brahman goes to Brahman.

Sāyaṇa explains the passage in these terms: the rākṣasas are those called Mandeha, the island is named Aruṇa, and the account is given in praise of the water, to enjoin the arghya. The summary above is the editor’s; the passage itself is printed below with its svara.

The 29 steps

The rite has two halves. The first (steps 1–12) purifies: sipping and sprinkling water, breath-control, the arghya to the Sun. The second (steps 13–29) is the japa of the Gāyatrī with its invocation, meditation and farewell, the standing worship (upasthāna) of the god of the hour, and the salutations that close the rite. Most steps are the same at all three times; the table shows where they differ. Each step’s page gives every text in Sanskrit with IAST, the words separated, a word-by-word table, a faithful and a readable translation, and for the Vedic mantras the commentaries.

How the text was established

The Vedic mantras that are not in the Mahānārāyaṇa were cut from the Taittirīya Saṃhitā in its pañcāśat divisions, the Āraṇyaka of muralīdhara and the Drāviḍa Brāhmaṇa (all in the vishvAsa corpus), with their svara, and collated with a second, independent transcription of each. Their places are given in the source’s own numbering (TS 3.4.11.2 is kāṇḍa 3, prapāṭhaka 4, anuvāka 11, pañcāśat 2). The Ṛgveda parallel is printed beside each where one exists; it is a different recension and never replaces the Taittirīya text.

The order of the rite and its other texts come from two printed prayogas: the Sandhyāvandana prayoga of the Mylapore Veda Adhyayana Sabhā (M), as transcribed in the vishvAsa corpus, which is the base for the order and for the texts only it has; and the stotrasamhita Sandhyāvandanam (S), which writes the sandhi joined and is the base for the texts both have. Every difference between them is classified on the sources page. M has steps S lacks (the āghrāṇa with drupadād iva, the seat before the japa, the nyāsas); both are given, marked as M’s.

The Mahānārāyaṇa mantras are shown from that edition, where their text, word division, translation and commentaries are proven; each links to its page. Neither prayoga names the work a traditional verse comes from, so the edition names none.
