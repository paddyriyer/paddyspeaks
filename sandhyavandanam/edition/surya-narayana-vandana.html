1 Homage to Savitṛ, the one eye of the world Verse of the prayoga

नमः सवित्रे जगदेकचक्षुषे जगत्प्रसूति-स्थिति-नाश-हेतवे त्रयीमयाय त्रिगुणात्मधारिणे विरिञ्चि-नारायण-शङ्करात्मने

Homage to Savitṛ, the world’s single eye, by whom the world is born, sustained and dissolved; who is the three Vedas, who holds the three guṇas, and who is Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva in one.

Homage to Savitṛ, the one eye of the world, the cause of the world’s birth, continuance and destruction, made of the three Vedas, bearing the three guṇas as his self, whose self is Viriñci, Nārāyaṇa and Śaṅkara.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

What it says and does

The first of four verses to the Sun as Nārāyaṇa at the close of the rite. It gathers the Sun into one figure who is everything the rite has addressed: the eye by which the world is seen, the cause of its arising, lasting and passing away, the substance of the three Vedas, the bearer of the three guṇas, and Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva together — the same three that the dhyāna verses of the japa assigned to the morning, the evening and midday.

In the rite Step sūrya-nārāyaṇa-vandana, all three services, facing east (S marks the direction).

What it says and does

A meditation verse that places Nārāyaṇa at the centre of the Sun’s disc and describes him piece by piece: the lotus seat, the ornaments (armlets, earrings shaped like the makara, crown, necklace), the golden body, and the conch and discus in his hands. It follows namaḥ savitre and makes the same identification visible: the Sun that has been worshipped all through the rite is to be seen with Nārāyaṇa within it.

In the rite Step sūrya-nārāyaṇa-vandana, all three services, after namaḥ savitre.

What it says and does

A short prayer of refuge to Kṛṣṇa as Viṣṇu, made almost entirely of names in the vocative: the bearer of conch, discus and mace, the one who lives in Dvārakā, Acyuta, Govinda, the lotus-eyed. The one request — rakṣa mām, ‘protect me’ — is grounded in the worshipper’s condition: śaraṇāgatam, ‘come for refuge’.

In the rite Step sūrya-nārāyaṇa-vandana, all three services, after dhyeyaḥ sadā.

What it says and does

A well-known simile closes the round of salutations: whatever god has been greeted — Sandhyā, the directions, Yama, Hari-Hara, the Sun — the homage travels on, as rain falling anywhere ends in the ocean, to Keśava. The last words are repeated with om namaḥ iti, and the worshipper then says the abhivādana again.

In the rite Step sūrya-nārāyaṇa-vandana, all three services, the last of its four verses, followed by the abhivādana.

Faithful English translation

I salute: (I am) of the gotra (…), endowed with the pravara of three seers (…), of the (Āpastamba) sūtra, a student of the Yajus branch; (…) Śarmā by name I am, O sirs!

What it says and does

The abhivādana is the formal greeting in which one announces oneself by lineage: the seers of one’s pravara, the gotra, the sūtra of one’s rites, the Veda one studies, and one’s name. S prints it as a formula with blanks: the parentheses and the empty brackets mark the places where each performer puts his own seers, gotra and name (M fills them with a worked example for the Bhāradvāja gotra). The words in parentheses — trayārṣeya, āpastamba — are the common case and are replaced when they do not apply (pañcārṣeya for five seers, another sūtra or Veda).

In the rite Said with a bow, touching the ears, after kāmo ’kārṣīt (step samasti-abhivadana) and again at the end of the salutation to Sūrya-Nārāyaṇa, in all three services.
