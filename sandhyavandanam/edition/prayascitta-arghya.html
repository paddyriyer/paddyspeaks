What it says and does

The saṅkalpa, the statement of intent, for the extra arghya of atonement (prāyaścitta), offered because the proper moment may have been missed. The witness prints the opening of the saṅkalpa (mamopātta … prītyartham, ‘by way of destroying all the faults I have incurred, for the pleasure of the Supreme Lord’) once, and then only this closing part for each time of day; the opening is said before it. The verbs are first person: the worshipper speaks for himself, and the formula is the same for every performer.

In the rite Morning service, step prayascitta-arghya, after a prāṇāyāma. S prints the opening of the saṅkalpa (mamopātta … prītyartham, the morning sandhyā) in parentheses before it; the text here is S’s closing part.

Notes
Traditional Interpretation

A saṅkalpa is said with the breath held after the prāṇāyāma, or immediately after it; it names the act, the time, and the purpose. The Smārta form offers the act for the pleasure of the Supreme Lord (Parameśvara); Vaiṣṇava prayogas use other wording, which is outside this edition.

Traditional Interpretation

The arghya is due at a precise moment (sunrise, midday, sunset); the extra arghya atones in case that moment was missed. M words the morning form as प्रातःसन्ध्या-कालातीत-प्रायश्चित्तार्घ्य-प्रदानं करिष्ये, the same intent (corrections.json).

Source and witnesses

The other witness

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

What it says and does

The three ‘utterances’ (vyāhṛtis) — bhūḥ, bhuvaḥ, suvaḥ — name the three worlds, earth, the mid-region and heaven, and are said with the praṇava Om before them. In the Taittirīya tradition the third is suvaḥ, where other schools say svaḥ. In the Sandhyāvandanam they close the two mārjanas (the water is then sprinkled round oneself) and they open the Gāyatrī at the arghya and at the japa.

In the rite Said after āpo hi ṣṭhā at the mārjana and after the second mārjana, while water is sprinkled round oneself; and before the Gāyatrī at the arghya, at the arghya of atonement and at the japa (steps marjana, punar-marjana, arghya, prayascitta-arghya, japa).

What it says and does

The Gāyatrī, also called the Sāvitrī after its god: the verse given at the upanayana and repeated in every Sandhyā. It has three pādas of eight syllables. The worshipper holds in mind the radiance of Savitṛ, the god who sets everything in motion, and asks him to set the mind itself in motion — to impel and direct understanding. Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara reads it several ways: as a prayer for the god’s gifts, and finally as the instruction to meditate on the supreme light standing as the Sun, सूर्यात्मना स्थितं पारमेश्वरं तेज उपास्यत्वेन उपदिश्यते. In the Taittirīya Saṃhitā it occurs at 1.5.6.4 and 4.1.11.1; in the Ṛgveda it is 3.62.10, a hymn of Viśvāmitra.

In the rite The centre of the rite. Recited with the vyāhṛtis for each arghya (three in the morning and evening, fewer at midday, then one more for atonement), within the prāṇāyāma (with the praṇava, the seven vyāhṛtis and the ‘head’), and in the japa, repeated many times. The prayoga’s viniyoga: Viśvāmitra the seer, nicṛd gāyatrī the metre, Savitṛ the deity (vin-savitri; vin-arghya).

The commentators
Traditional Interpretation

Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara

Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara, on TS 1.5.6, first reads tat as a genitive and dhīmahi as ‘may we hold’: वरणीयं सर्वैः प्रार्थनीयं भर्गः तेजः अन्नं धनं वा वयं धीमहि धारयेम. The impelling of minds is either of rites (धियः कर्माणि) or of understandings bent on dharma (धर्मादिगोचराः बुद्धीः).

Traditional Interpretation

Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara

His last reading is meditative: the splendour is the Sun’s disc that burns away sin, पापानां तापनं तेजो मण्डलात्मकं, held in mind, धीमहि मनसा धारयामः, and what is taught is the supreme light standing as the Sun: सूर्यात्मना स्थितं पारमेश्वरं तेज उपास्यत्वेन उपदिश्यते.

Notes
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

Academic History

The same verse is Ṛgveda 3.62.10, in the third maṇḍala, whose hymns are ascribed to Viśvāmitra and his family; the prayoga accordingly names Viśvāmitra as its seer.
