What it says and does

A viniyoga is the statement said before a mantra of its seer (ṛṣi), its metre (chandas), its deity (devatā) and the use (viniyoga) it is put to. Here: the three ṛks to Mitra (TS 3.4.11.5; RV 3.59.6, 1, 2), the morning upasthāna. The first is a gāyatrī and the other two triṣṭubhs, which is why two metres are named. The commas of the witness mark the pauses between the parts; a word before a comma keeps its pausal form.

In the rite Morning service, step surya-upasthana, before mitrasya (M only).

Faithful English translation

mitrasya.1 Of Mitra, supporter of the peoples, of the god, the winnable fame, true, most brilliantly famed.

mitrasya.2 Mitra, knowing, sets people in order; Mitra has held up the earth and the sky; Mitra watches the peoples unblinking; to the true one let us offer an oblation with ghee.

mitrasya.3 Let that mortal stand forth rich in offerings, O Mitra, who strives for you, O Āditya, by your ordinance. He, helped by you, is not slain, not overpowered; distress does not reach him from near or from afar.

Word for word; words supplied for English are in [brackets].

What it says and does

Three ṛks to Mitra, the god of the morning and of agreements between men. The first praises his glory; the second says what he does — he sets each person to his work, holds up earth and sky, and watches over everyone without blinking; the third promises that the one who serves him by his ordinance is safe from harm near or far. In the Taittirīya Saṃhitā the first verse is used in making the fire-pan (Sāyaṇa: Mitra is chosen to calm it, वारुण्युखाऽसीद्धा मैत्रियोपैति शान्त्यै), and the three together stand among the offering verses of TS 3.4.11; in the Ṛgveda they are 3.59.6, 3.59.1 and 3.59.2. In the morning Sandhyā the worshipper stands before the rising Sun and greets him as Mitra.

In the rite Morning sūryopasthāna: the three ṛks are recited standing, facing the rising Sun, after the Gāyatrī has been given leave. The Mylapore prayoga gives the viniyoga: the All-gods as seers, gāyatrī and triṣṭubh the metres, Mitra the deity, used in the upasthāna of the morning Sandhyā (vin-mitrasya).

The commentators
Traditional Interpretation

Sāyaṇa

Sāyaṇa, on the first verse at TS 4.1.6, supplies the verb ‘is’: मित्रस्य देवस्य श्रवः श्रोतुं योग्यं यशो महदस्तीति शेषः, and explains the choice of Mitra: the blazing fire-pan is fierce, Varuṇa’s, and Mitra’s verse calms it — अभिद्धा प्रज्वलिता येयमुखा सेयमुग्रत्वाद्वारुणी, तस्या मैत्र्यच शान्तिर्भ-वति.

Traditional Interpretation

Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara

Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara, on TS 3.4.11: Mitra moves people to act knowing what each is fit for — जनान् सर्वान् यातयति चेष्टयति प्रजानन् तत्तदधिकारानुरूपविधानज्ञः — and ‘watching’ means helping: आभिमुख्येन पश्यति उपकरोतीत्यर्थः.

Notes
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The first ṛk is a gāyatrī, the other two triṣṭubhs — hence the viniyoga’s ‘gāyatrī and triṣṭubh’. The Ṛgveda (3.59.6) reads the first verse differently: मित्रस्य चर्षणीधृतो ऽवो देवस्य सानसि द्युम्नं चित्रश्रवस्तमम् — avaḥ ‘help’ for śravaḥ ‘fame’, and dyumnam ‘splendour’ for satyam. The Taittirīya and the prayogas have श्रवः and सत्यम्.

Academic History

What it says and does

The first verse of the midday upasthāna, recited standing and facing the Sun. It pictures the Sun as Savitṛ, the god who sets everything going: he travels the sky on a golden chariot, and as he goes he puts gods and mortals each in their place and looks over all the worlds. In the Taittirīya Saṃhitā it is the yājyā verse for an offering to Savitṛ in the Rājasūya (TS 3.4.11.2, with Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara’s viniyoga); the same verse is the second of a hymn to Savitṛ in the Ṛgveda (1.35.2). At midday the worshipper stands before the Sun at the top of its course, and the verse describes exactly that: the god overseeing everything below.

In the rite Midday sūryopasthāna, first of the five mantras (ā satyena, ud vayam, ud u tyam, citraṃ devānām, tac cakṣuḥ). The Mylapore prayoga gives its viniyoga as Hiraṇyagarbha the seer, triṣṭubh the metre, Savitṛ the deity, used in the upasthāna of the Sun (vin-a-satyena).

What it says and does

A single anuṣṭubh of ascent: from darkness to a higher light, and from that to the highest light, the Sun, ‘god among gods’. The verse is said by people who have come through darkness and now see the light above them. In the Taittirīya Saṃhitā (4.1.7.4) it is one of the verses of the fire-altar rite; Sāyaṇa takes the light there as the fire’s light which has become the Sun. In the Ṛgveda it is 1.50.10 (with the words of the first line in another order), in the great hymn to Sūrya whose first verse (ud u tyam) follows it in the rite. At midday it continues the upasthāna begun with ā satyena.

In the rite Midday sūryopasthāna, the second of its five mantras (after ā satyena, before ud u tyam), recited standing and facing the Sun; it shares the viniyoga given for ā satyena in the Mylapore prayoga (vin-a-satyena).

What it says and does

Two famous verses on the rising Sun, recited together. In the first, the rays carry the Sun up into the sky so that everyone can see him. In the second, the Sun’s disc is the bright face of the gods and the eye of Mitra, Varuṇa and Agni; its light fills heaven, earth and the space between, and the Sun is called the self (ātman) of everything moving and unmoving. In the Taittirīya Saṃhitā they stand together at 1.4.43.1; in the Ṛgveda they open two separate hymns to Sūrya, 1.50.1 and 1.115.1. Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara reads the second verse as teaching meditation on the Sun’s disc: भवति मण्डलोपासनमिति.

In the rite Midday sūryopasthāna: the third mantra (after ā satyena and ud vayam, before tac cakṣuḥ), recited standing and facing the Sun; it shares the viniyoga given for ā satyena in the Mylapore prayoga (vin-a-satyena).

Notes
Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The first verse is a gāyatrī, the second a triṣṭubh. The Ṛgveda (1.50.1, 1.115.1) has the same words; the Taittirīya spelling joins them (त्यञ्जातवेदसन्देवंवहन्ति).

Academic History

Ṛgveda 1.50 and 1.115 are the two great hymns to Sūrya in the first maṇḍala; ud vayam (1.50.10), recited just before in the rite, comes from the first of them.

What it says and does

The close of the midday upasthāna, from the fourth prapāṭhaka of the Taittirīya Āraṇyaka (4.42.5 in the muralīdhara text; Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara comments on it in his seventh prapāṭhaka, among the Pravargya mantras). The first part is the long prayer for a full life: the worshipper looks at the Sun rising in the east, the eye set in place by the god, and asks to see, live, rejoice, flourish, hear and speak, and stand unconquered, ‘for a hundred autumns’. The second part asks the Sun, risen from the great ocean, to purify the one who prays. The Ṛgveda has the beginning of the first part (7.66.16) but not the long series of wishes or the second verse.

In the rite Midday sūryopasthāna, the fourth and fifth of its mantras, recited standing and facing the Sun after ud u tyam and citraṃ devānām. The Mylapore prayoga writes the series line by line (पश्येम शरदः शतम्। जीवेम …), and gives no separate viniyoga; the upasthāna’s viniyoga is the one stated for ā satyena (vin-a-satyena).

Traditional Interpretation

Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara

Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara reads each wish as a wish for whole faculties: तत्प्रसादाद् अविकलचक्षुषः स्यास, ‘by its grace may we have unimpaired eyes’ — शृणवाम अविकलश्रोत्रा भवाम, ‘unimpaired ears’; ‘unconquered’ is अनभिभूतास्स्याम. What it says and does

The first two verses of the evening upasthāna, to Varuṇa, the god of cosmic order who sees and punishes wrongdoing and is approached in the evening. The first is a short cry for help: hear me today and be gracious. The second, a triṣṭubh, asks him not to be angry and not to cut life short. In the Taittirīya Saṃhitā (2.1.11.6) they are the invitatory and offering verses of a rice-offering to Varuṇa (Bhāskara’s viniyoga); in the Ṛgveda they are 1.25.19 and 1.24.11, from the hymns of Śunaḥśepa — the man saved from being sacrificed, whose story makes them prayers for release.

In the rite Evening sūryopasthāna, recited standing and facing west, followed by yac cid dhi and the two verses after it. The Mylapore prayoga gives the viniyoga for the evening verses: Śunaḥśepa the seer, gāyatrī and triṣṭubh the metres, Varuṇa the deity, used in the upasthāna of the evening Sandhyā (vin-imam-me).

yac-cid-dhi.1 Whatever ordinance of yours, O god Varuṇa, we violate day after day, as people do —

What it says and does

Three ṛks to Varuṇa that close the evening upasthāna. They are a confession: we break your laws day after day, as people will; we offend against the gods, knowingly or unknowingly; we are cheated by others, as in gambling. Varuṇa, who binds wrongdoers with his fetters, is asked not to punish but to loosen every knot, so that the worshipper may be dear to him again. In the Taittirīya Saṃhitā they stand together at the end of 3.4.11 among the offering verses; in the Ṛgveda they are scattered: 1.25.1, 7.89.5 and 5.85.8. Bhāskara reads the gamblers as priests who serve their own ends (ऋत्विजः), and the knowingly done sin as sin from lack of faith.

In the rite Evening sūryopasthāna, after imaṃ me varuṇa and tat tvā yāmi, recited standing and facing west; the viniyoga is the one the Mylapore prayoga gives for the evening verses (vin-imam-me).

The commentators
Traditional Interpretation

Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara

Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara, on TS 3.4.11, reads the first verse as a promise of service rather than a confession: यद्व्रतं यागादिकं कर्म वयं विशः मनुष्यमात्रभूताः द्यविद्यवि दिनेदिने प्रमिनीमसि प्रतर्कयामः अविच्छेदेनानुष्ठातुमिच्छामः — though he also allows the plain sense, यद्व्रतं प्रमिनीमः हिंसितवन्तः.

Traditional Interpretation

Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara

On the second, the wrong is human carelessness, मनुष्या वयं मनुष्यस्वभावात्संभाव्यमानप्रमादाः, and the prayer ends in grace: मा रीरिषः, अपि तु अनुगृहाणैव.

Traditional Interpretation

Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara

On the third: कितवासः स्वार्थसाधनतत्पराः — the cheats are those bent on their own gain; the sins are of three kinds, done by others to us, done deliberately (सत्यं बुद्धिपूर्वमेव कृतं पापमश्रद्धादिना), and done without our knowing (कृतमपि न जानीमः); untying them means forgiving them, क्षमस्वेति यावत्.

Traditional Interpretation

Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara’s viniyoga

In the Saṃhitā kitavāsaḥ is an alternative offering verse: तत्रैव याज्याविकल्पः - कितवास इति त्रिष्टुप्.

Linguistic and Textual Analysis

The first verse is a gāyatrī, the second a jagatī (Bhāskara: यत्किं चेदमिति जगती), the third a triṣṭubh. The Ṛgveda has the same words (1.25.1; 7.89.5; 5.85.8, where it reads देवाधा for देवाथा).

Direct Textual Meaning

The base witness breaks यच्चिद्धि at a pañcāśat marker (यत् [45] चिद्धि); the edition joins it as sandhi requires (corrections.json).
