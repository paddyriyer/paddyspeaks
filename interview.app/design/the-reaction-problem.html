The Reaction Problem

India needs eleven off the last over. Twenty-five million people are watching, every one of them can tap an emoji, and the product has promised the collective mood on every screen within two seconds. Everyone reaches for Kafka, Flink, Redis and WebSockets in the first ninety seconds. This walk-through starts with the question that comes before any technology — what exactly are we promising, and what happens when that promise breaks — and then builds the whole thing: the clocks, the schema, the ingest, the aggregation, the fan-out, the integrity checks, the SLO, the failure ladder, and the payment ledger it must never be mistaken for.

The senior move

Key the mood on media time — where in the broadcast each viewer was — not on the wall clock. Then a second's mood is a revision that fills in as viewers further behind reach it, and nobody is ever shown a moment they have not seen.

Grains

  • Batch — one per session per media second, idempotent on (session_id, batch_seq).
  • Voice — one per person per second; taps are intensity, not headcount.
  • Snapshot revision — what was known about a second, as of a time.

Key code

  • ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING ingest
  • Edge pre-aggregation: additive deltas
  • Latest-only fan-out slot per socket
  • percentile_cont SLO with a spoiler count

The trap

Measuring "two seconds" on the wall clock. Live streams run many seconds behind the stadium, unevenly, so a wall-clock-fresh mood spoils the six for everyone who has not seen it yet.

§ 01 — THE QUESTIONTwenty-five million thumbs and one number

The ball disappears over long-on. In Mumbai a software engineer hammers 🔥 eleven times. In Coimbatore a retired schoolteacher who has watched India lose from this exact position before taps 🙏 once and keeps her eyes shut. In a hostel in Kanpur a student who backed the other side in a group-chat bet throws the remote at the wall and taps nothing. He is still part of the audience. The system's job is to turn all of that into one picture of the crowd, on every screen, almost at once.

Interview Prompt

"A live cricket stream has 25 million concurrent viewers. Each can react with one of six emojis. Show the collective mood on every screen within two seconds. Design it."

LEVEL · SENIOR / STAFFDURATION · 45–60 MINFORMAT · WHITEBOARD

The prompt reads like a throughput question, and the predictable answer is a throughput answer: Kafka for ingest, Spark or Flink for windows, Redis for the latest state, WebSockets for the push. None of that is wrong. It is early. It answers how before anyone has asked what exactly are we promising, to whom, measured how, and what happens when the promise breaks? Technology selection is the last interesting question in this problem. The interviewer is listening for whether you notice the three hidden ones: which clock "two seconds" is measured on, what a single tap is worth, and how wrong the number is allowed to be.

HistoricalThe scale is not invented. Hotstar reported a peak of 25.3 million concurrent viewers during the India–New Zealand World Cup semi-final on 10 July 2019, verified by its CDN, Akamai, according to its year-end report; Disney reported 59 million concurrent viewers for the 2023 World Cup final. Hotstar's engineers also published how their 2019 emoji feature worked: a Go service writing reactions to Kafka asynchronously, Spark Streaming aggregating in two-second micro-batches, and results pushed through an in-house, MQTT-based PubSub their engineers presented as built for 50 million concurrent connections. The write-up reports about five billion emojis from 55.8 million users across that World Cup. Everything below is a hypothetical design for today, not a description of anyone's current system.

Scoping out loud

Out of scope, said explicitly: video encoding and the CDN, the player, authentication, and the emoji picker's UI. In scope: how a tap becomes a counted voice, how the mood is aggregated and pushed to 25 million sockets, which clock the two seconds are measured on, what the system refuses to count, how it degrades, and how we prove it is working. Then the envelope, volunteered rather than extracted.

Read the table for the row that does the architectural work. It is not ingest. Batching on the phone and pre-aggregation at the edge shrink twenty million taps to a few thousand records a second. The load that cannot be shrunk is the one going out: twelve and a half million messages a second to people who each need the same few hundred bytes. The aggregate itself is absurdly small — six counters per region per second. This is a fan-out problem and a timing problem wearing a big-data costume.

§ 02 — WHAT “TWO SECONDS” MEANSFive questions wearing a stopwatch

"Within two seconds" sounds like a requirement. It is five unanswered questions: two seconds from what, to what, for whom, at what percentile, and measured on whose clock. Each answer produces a different system.

FROM WHAT
The tap, the edge receiving it, the window closing, the publish, or the pixels changing? Each is a different SLI. Batching on the phone saves the backend and spends the user's time; a two-second window spends the whole budget by itself.
FOR WHOM
The median viewer, p95, p99, or everyone? Averages hide the people having the worst time, which is why serious latency objectives are written as percentiles — and why fanning out to more machines makes the tail worse, not better.
ON WHICH CLOCK
The one most candidates never ask about. The video itself is not live. A stream can run many seconds behind the stadium, and different viewers sit at different points behind it: cable and app, Wi-Fi and 4G. A mood that is fresh against the wall clock shows the celebration of a six to someone whose stream has not yet shown the ball being bowled. You have built a spoiler engine with excellent p99 latency.

So the promise has to be restated on the viewer's own clock. Players already know where they are in the broadcast: HLS playlists can carry EXT-X-PROGRAM-DATE-TIME, which ties a segment's first sample to an absolute date and time. Every reaction carries that position — its media time — and the mood is keyed to it. Each screen shows the mood for the moment it is showing.

The promise, written so it can be tested

  1. Never spoil. A screen never shows a mood for a media second it has not finished playing. Target: zero, not a percentile.
  2. Freshness. At p95, the mood shown describes a media second that ended at most 2,000 ms earlier on that viewer's own timeline.
  3. Honesty. A mood older than 5 s on the viewer's timeline is shown as "catching up", never as live.
  4. Accuracy. The settled mood counts one voice per person per second, from sessions that passed integrity; loss below 1% is acceptable and measured.

The consequence nobody draws: who gets to see the crowd

Keying on media time has a strange and important consequence. A viewer two seconds behind live watches the six before almost everyone else has. When they get there, the only reactions that exist are from viewers even closer to live — few or none. A viewer thirty seconds behind sees the whole crowd's answer. The mood for a given second is therefore not a value; it is a series of revisions, growing as viewers further behind reach that moment. This query measures it on the synthetic match: the six is bowled at media second 125, and each cohort is shown second 129 with a two-second hold-back.

SQL · POSTGRESQL 16 · EXECUTED — WHO SEES HOW MUCH OF THE CROWD
-- Who gets to see the crowd? A viewer D seconds behind live watches media -- second 129 at starts_at + 129 + D. Shown with a 2-second hold-back, the mood -- they can see is whatever had reached the edge by then. The viewers closest -- to live can only ever see the reactions of viewers even closer: few or none. WITH settled AS ( SELECT count(*) AS voices FROM reaction_voice WHERE match_id = 1 AND media_sec BETWEEN 125 AND 129 ), cohort AS ( SELECT unnest(ARRAY[2, 5, 10, 20, 30]) AS behind_live_s ) SELECT c.behind_live_s, count(v.session_id) AS voices_visible, round(100.0 * count(v.session_id) / s.voices, 1) AS pct_of_settled FROM cohort c CROSS JOIN settled s CROSS JOIN dim_match m LEFT JOIN reaction_voice v ON v.match_id = 1 AND v.media_sec BETWEEN 125 AND 129 AND v.edge_received_at <= m.starts_at + make_interval(secs => 129 + c.behind_live_s + 2) WHERE m.match_id = 1 GROUP BY c.behind_live_s, s.voices ORDER BY c.behind_live_s;

That table changes the product, not just the pipeline. The viewers closest to live — often the most engaged, on the best connections — see a mood built from about one twentieth of the crowd. A design that ignores this shows them a noisy, thin number and calls it real-time. A design that understands it either sets a minimum voice count before showing anything, holds the mood a little further back for the fastest cohort, or labels the early number for what it is. Those are product decisions, and they belong on the whiteboard before any technology does.

§ 03 — DATA FLOWFollowing a tap through the building

Two tempos. The live path turns taps into voices at the edge, sums tiny deltas, and pushes snapshots down millions of sockets in well under a second. The analytics path keeps every batch, recomputes the settled mood, and measures the promise. Integrity sits between the edge and the sum, because once a fake voice has been summed it cannot be taken out again.

FIG. 1 — The live path never waits for completeness: it sums deltas and publishes revisions. The log keeps every batch, so anything summed on the live path can be recounted.

Three properties of this picture carry the interview. First, the expensive reductions happen as early as possible: the phone turns a flurry of taps into one batch per second, and the edge turns batches into voices and voices into deltas, so the component everyone draws in the middle — the stream processor — has almost nothing to do. Second, there is no window on the live path. The mood for a second is never "final"; the aggregator emits a new revision whenever its count changes, and screens show the latest revision for the moment they are showing. Third, integrity verdicts flow back to the edge, because the only place a fake voice can be cheaply refused is before it is summed.

§ 04 — DATA MODELTwo clocks on every row

The schema falls out of the grain question. The batch is what arrives. The tap row is what it contained. The voice — one per person per media second — is what counts. The snapshot is a revision: what was known about a second, as of a time. Every fact carries both clocks, media time for meaning and wall time for operations.

Dimensions, with the ball-by-ball on the media clock

The delivery dimension is aligned to the broadcast's media clock, not to the stadium's. That single choice is what lets analytics ask "how did the crowd answer this ball?" with a range join instead of a guess about stream delay.

SQL · POSTGRESQL 16 · EXECUTED — DIMENSIONS
-- The Reaction Problem — schema. PostgreSQL 16. -- Two clocks on every row: MEDIA time (where in the broadcast the viewer was) -- and WALL time (when a machine saw it). The mood is keyed on media time. CREATE TABLE dim_match ( match_id INT PRIMARY KEY, title TEXT NOT NULL, starts_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL ); -- Ball-by-ball, already aligned to the broadcast's media clock. CREATE TABLE dim_delivery ( match_id INT NOT NULL REFERENCES dim_match, over_no SMALLINT NOT NULL, ball_no SMALLINT NOT NULL, media_sec INT NOT NULL, -- when the ball is bowled, in media time outcome TEXT NOT NULL CHECK (outcome IN ('dot','run','four','six','wicket','wide')), PRIMARY KEY (match_id, over_no, ball_no) ); CREATE TABLE dim_account ( account_id BIGINT PRIMARY KEY, created_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL );

The facts — a batch and what it held

One row per client batch: one session's taps for one media second. The primary key is the idempotency key. The admission verdict is stored, not filtered, because the funnel of what was refused is itself a metric. Device fingerprint and network ride along because integrity needs them later and cannot get them back.

SQL · POSTGRESQL 16 · EXECUTED — BATCH AND TAP FACTS
-- One row per client batch: a session's taps for ONE media second. -- (session_id, batch_seq) is the idempotency key: a retried batch is a no-op. CREATE TABLE reaction_batch ( session_id UUID NOT NULL, batch_seq INT NOT NULL, account_id BIGINT REFERENCES dim_account, -- NULL = anonymous device_fp TEXT NOT NULL, -- coarse device fingerprint asn INT NOT NULL, -- network the batch came from region TEXT NOT NULL, match_id INT NOT NULL REFERENCES dim_match, media_sec INT NOT NULL, -- ★ the second the viewer was WATCHING client_sent_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL, edge_received_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL, admission TEXT NOT NULL CHECK (admission IN ('admitted','throttled','unauthenticated')), PRIMARY KEY (session_id, batch_seq) ); CREATE TABLE reaction_tap ( session_id UUID NOT NULL, batch_seq INT NOT NULL, emoji TEXT NOT NULL CHECK (emoji IN ('fire','clap','pray','shock','sad','angry')), taps SMALLINT NOT NULL CHECK (taps BETWEEN 1 AND 50), PRIMARY KEY (session_id, batch_seq, emoji), FOREIGN KEY (session_id, batch_seq) REFERENCES reaction_batch );

What we serve, and what we measure

The snapshot is keyed by revision because the same media second is published many times as it fills in. The render receipt is the SLO's raw material: a 1% sample of clients reporting which second's mood they showed and where their own player was at that instant.

SQL · POSTGRESQL 16 · EXECUTED — SNAPSHOTS AND RECEIPTS
-- What we actually broadcast. A media second's mood keeps growing as viewers -- further behind reach it, so a snapshot is a REVISION: what was known about -- second media_sec as of published_at. Screens never render a stale one. CREATE TABLE mood_snapshot ( match_id INT NOT NULL REFERENCES dim_match, region TEXT NOT NULL, -- 'ALL' for the merged view media_sec INT NOT NULL, revision INT NOT NULL, published_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL, voices INT NOT NULL CHECK (voices >= 0), is_partial BOOLEAN NOT NULL, -- a region missing from the merge shares JSONB NOT NULL, PRIMARY KEY (match_id, region, media_sec, revision) ); -- 1% of clients report what they rendered and where their player was. CREATE TABLE render_receipt ( session_id UUID NOT NULL, match_id INT NOT NULL, snapshot_media_sec INT NOT NULL, -- the second the mood describes player_media_ms BIGINT NOT NULL, -- where THIS viewer's player was at render rendered_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL, PRIMARY KEY (session_id, rendered_at) ); CREATE INDEX ON reaction_batch (match_id, media_sec); CREATE INDEX ON reaction_batch (account_id);
Why the voice is a view, not a table

One person, one voice per media second, and the dominant emoji of that second is the voice. Eleven taps on 🔥 is one fire voice with intensity 10, not eleven votes. Headcount and intensity are different questions — "how many people feel this" and "how strongly" — and conflating them hands the mood to whoever has the fastest thumb or the best script. The voice is derived, so the rule can change without rewriting history.

§ 05 — INGESTBatch on the phone, decide at the edge, never count twice

Three small programs carry the inbound side: the client batcher that groups taps by the second the viewer was watching, the edge's admission decision, and the idempotent write. Each is short; the judgement is in what they refuse to do.

1 · The client — group by media second, keep the sequence on retry

The app knows where its player is, so it is the only place the media clock is free. It groups taps by that second, sends at most once a wall-clock second, and keeps batch_seq unchanged through every retry. It gives up after five attempts, because this is a mood, not money.

PYTHON · TESTED (test_reaction.py) — CLIENT BATCHER
"""Client side (shown in Python; the app runs the same logic natively). Taps are grouped by the MEDIA second the viewer was watching, not by wall time, and leave the phone at most once per second. A batch keeps its batch_seq through every retry, so the server can drop duplicates. """ from dataclasses import dataclass, field MAX_TAPS_PER_EMOJI = 50 @dataclass class Batch: batch_seq: int media_sec: int taps: dict[str, int] attempts: int = 0 @dataclass class Coalescer: session_id: str _seq: int = 0 _open: dict[int, dict[str, int]] = field(default_factory=dict) # media_sec -> taps _unacked: dict[int, Batch] = field(default_factory=dict) # batch_seq -> batch def tap(self, emoji: str, player_media_ms: int) -> None: sec = player_media_ms // 1000 # where the viewer IS, not what time it is bucket = self._open.setdefault(sec, {}) bucket[emoji] = min(bucket.get(emoji, 0) + 1, MAX_TAPS_PER_EMOJI) def flush(self) -> list[Batch]: """Called once per wall-clock second. New batches, then any still unacked.""" for sec in sorted(self._open): self._seq += 1 self._unacked[self._seq] = Batch(self._seq, sec, self._open[sec]) self._open.clear() due = sorted(self._unacked.values(), key=lambda b: b.batch_seq) for b in due: b.attempts += 1 return [b for b in due if b.attempts <= 5] # give up quietly: it is a mood, not money def ack(self, batch_seq: int) -> None: self._unacked.pop(batch_seq, None)

2 · Admission — two buckets, and the order matters

Token buckets per account and per device fingerprint, so a farm of fresh accounts on one device still shares one bucket. Anonymous batches are stored for analysis and never counted. A batch with more than sixteen taps in a second is not a thumb. The order of the two checks is a design decision the tests pin down: a throttled account must not spend the tokens its device shares with others.

PYTHON · TESTED — EDGE ADMISSION
"""Edge admission: decide before anything is counted. Token buckets per account AND per device fingerprint (a farm of fresh accounts on one device still shares one bucket). In production the buckets live in the edge node's memory, keyed so a session always lands on the same node; nothing here needs a network round-trip on the hot path. """ from dataclasses import dataclass from typing import Callable MAX_TAPS_PER_BATCH = 16 # more than this in one second is not a thumb @dataclass class TokenBucket: rate: float # tokens per second burst: float tokens: float = -1.0 updated: float = 0.0 def take(self, now: float) -> bool: if self.tokens < 0: self.tokens, self.updated = self.burst, now self.tokens = min(self.burst, self.tokens + (now - self.updated) * self.rate) self.updated = now if self.tokens >= 1: self.tokens -= 1 return True return False class Admission: def __init__(self, clock: Callable[[], float], per_account=(2.0, 4.0), per_device=(6.0, 12.0)): self.clock = clock self.per_account, self.per_device = per_account, per_device self.accounts: dict[int, TokenBucket] = {} self.devices: dict[str, TokenBucket] = {} def decide(self, account_id: int | None, device_fp: str, taps: dict[str, int]) -> str: if account_id is None: return "unauthenticated" # stored for analysis, never counted in the mood if sum(taps.values()) > MAX_TAPS_PER_BATCH: return "throttled" now = self.clock() acct = self.accounts.setdefault(account_id, TokenBucket(*self.per_account)) dev = self.devices.setdefault(device_fp, TokenBucket(*self.per_device)) # both must have a token. Account first: an account that is already # throttled must not spend the tokens its device shares with others. if not acct.take(now) or not dev.take(now): return "throttled" return "admitted"

3 · The write — idempotent by construction

Retries are certain: phones lose signal on trains. The batch's primary key is the idempotency key, so the second arrival inserts nothing and the tap rows, chained in the same statement, are never written twice. The function returns whether anything was stored, which is how the pipeline counts duplicates without a second table.

SQL · POSTGRESQL 16 · EXECUTED — IDEMPOTENT INGEST
-- Idempotent ingest. The stream sink calls this once per client batch. -- A retry carries the same (session_id, batch_seq), so the second call -- inserts nothing and returns FALSE: duplicates cannot inflate the mood. CREATE FUNCTION ingest_batch(p JSONB) RETURNS BOOLEAN LANGUAGE sql AS $$ WITH b AS ( INSERT INTO reaction_batch (session_id, batch_seq, account_id, device_fp, asn, region, match_id, media_sec, client_sent_at, edge_received_at, admission) VALUES ((p->>'session_id')::uuid, (p->>'batch_seq')::int, (p->>'account_id')::bigint, p->>'device_fp', (p->>'asn')::int, p->>'region', (p->>'match_id')::int, (p->>'media_sec')::int, (p->>'client_sent_at')::timestamptz, (p->>'edge_received_at')::timestamptz, p->>'admission') ON CONFLICT (session_id, batch_seq) DO NOTHING RETURNING session_id, batch_seq ), t AS ( INSERT INTO reaction_tap (session_id, batch_seq, emoji, taps) SELECT b.session_id, b.batch_seq, e.key, LEAST(e.value::int, 50) FROM b CROSS JOIN jsonb_each_text(p->'taps') AS e RETURNING 1 ) SELECT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM b); $$;

§ 06 — AGGREGATIONDeltas, revisions, and the hot key that is not there

The aggregation is where the obvious answer spends its effort, and where this design spends almost none. The edge does the reduction; the aggregator adds up deltas; the publisher emits revisions. The hard parts are the hot key and the clock, and both are settled before a stream processor is chosen.

The hot partition, measured

Key the stream by match and every reaction in the country lands on one partition — a hot partition with a cricket score. Key it by session and the same load spreads. The query below hashes the same synthetic batches both ways into 64 partitions.

SQL · POSTGRESQL 16 · EXECUTED — PARTITION SKEW BY KEY
-- The hot partition, measured. Same batches, two candidate keys, 64 partitions. -- Keyed by match, one partition takes everything; keyed by session, load spreads. WITH k AS ( SELECT abs(hashtext(match_id::text)) % 64 AS by_match, abs(hashtext(session_id::text)) % 64 AS by_session FROM reaction_batch ), load AS ( SELECT 'match_id' AS key, by_match AS p, count(*) AS n FROM k GROUP BY by_match UNION ALL SELECT 'session_id', by_session, count(*) FROM k GROUP BY by_session ) SELECT key, count(*) AS partitions_used, max(n) AS hottest, round(max(n) / (sum(n) / 64.0), 1) AS hottest_vs_fair_share FROM load GROUP BY key ORDER BY key;

Edge pre-aggregation — additive deltas

Sessions are routed to edge nodes by a hash of the session, so per-session state — dedup and one voice per second — is local. Every 250 ms a node flushes deltas: counts since the last flush, keyed by match, region, media second and emoji. Deltas are additive, so any number of nodes merge by summing, in any order, and a media second can keep receiving revisions while slower viewers reach it. A batch more than ninety media seconds behind the newest is counted in the statistics and kept out of the mood.

PYTHON · TESTED — EDGE PRE-AGGREGATOR
"""Edge pre-aggregation: millions of batches in, thousands of tiny records out. Sessions are routed to edge nodes by hash(session_id), so the per-session state (dedup and one-voice-per-second) is local. Every 250 ms the node flushes DELTAS — counts since the last flush — keyed by (match, region, media_sec, emoji). Deltas are additive, so any number of nodes merge by summing, in any order, and a media second can keep receiving revisions while viewers who are further behind reach it. """ from collections import Counter, OrderedDict from dataclasses import dataclass ALLOWED_LATENESS_S = 90 # the slowest stream we serve, plus margin SEEN_CAPACITY = 2_000_000 # (session, batch_seq) pairs remembered for dedup @dataclass(frozen=True) class Delta: match_id: int region: str media_sec: int emoji: str voices: int class PreAggregator: def __init__(self, seen_capacity: int = SEEN_CAPACITY): self.counts: Counter = Counter() self.seen_batches: OrderedDict = OrderedDict() # bounded LRU self.voiced: OrderedDict = OrderedDict() # (session, match, media_sec) self.capacity = seen_capacity self.newest_sec: dict[int, int] = {} self.stats = Counter() def _remember(self, lru: OrderedDict, key) -> bool: """True if key was new. Oldest keys fall out when the LRU is full.""" if key in lru: lru.move_to_end(key) return False lru[key] = None if len(lru) > self.capacity: lru.popitem(last=False) return True def accept(self, b: dict) -> None: if b["admission"] != "admitted": self.stats[b["admission"]] += 1 return if not self._remember(self.seen_batches, (b["session_id"], b["batch_seq"])): self.stats["duplicate"] += 1 # a retry: already counted return m, sec = b["match_id"], b["media_sec"] newest = self.newest_sec[m] = max(self.newest_sec.get(m, sec), sec) if sec < newest - ALLOWED_LATENESS_S: self.stats["too_late"] += 1 # counted in stats, not in the mood return if not self._remember(self.voiced, (b["session_id"], m, sec)): self.stats["second_voice"] += 1 # one voice per person per second return emoji = max(sorted(b["taps"]), key=lambda e: b["taps"][e]) # dominant, ties by name self.counts[(m, b["region"], sec, emoji)] += 1 self.stats["voiced"] += 1 def flush(self) -> list[Delta]: out = [Delta(m, r, s, e, n) for (m, r, s, e), n in sorted(self.counts.items())] self.counts.clear() return out def merge(*node_deltas: list[Delta]) -> Counter: """The aggregator's whole job, given deltas: add them up.""" total: Counter = Counter() for deltas in node_deltas: for d in deltas: total[(d.match_id, d.region, d.media_sec, d.emoji)] += d.voices return total

The same job as Flink SQL

If the organisation already runs Flink well, the aggregation is one job. Note what is absent: no window and no watermark. A window would hold each second's result until its slowest viewer arrived — thirty seconds or more — which defeats the purpose. An updating GROUP BY emits a change whenever a count changes, an upsert sink turns every change into a revision, keep-first deduplication enforces one voice per person-second, and two-phase aggregation does on the cluster what the edge does in the Python above.

§ 07 — FAN-OUTOne slot per socket, never a queue

Twelve and a half million messages a second is the real load, and the design choice that keeps it survivable is small: each connection holds one slot, not a queue. A new snapshot overwrites whatever the connection has not sent yet. A slow phone skips revisions instead of falling further and further behind.

That is backpressure that drops the past rather than delaying the present — exactly right for a mood meter and exactly wrong for anything where each message matters, such as chat or payments. The publisher never awaits a socket, so one slow client blocks only itself. The client applies the last rule: never show a moment the player has not finished, and never pass an old mood off as live.

PYTHON · TESTED — LATEST-ONLY FAN-OUT AND THE CLIENT'S RENDER RULE
"""Fan-out: millions of open connections, one tiny snapshot each. Each connection holds ONE slot, not a queue. A new snapshot overwrites whatever the connection has not sent yet, so a slow phone skips revisions instead of falling further and further behind: backpressure that drops the past rather than delaying the present. (Right for a mood meter; it would be wrong for anything where each message matters.) """ import asyncio from dataclasses import dataclass @dataclass(frozen=True) class Snapshot: match_id: int media_sec: int revision: int shares: dict class Connection: def __init__(self, send): self._send = send # async callable: the socket write self._latest: Snapshot | None = None self._ready = asyncio.Event() self.sent = 0 self.skipped = 0 def offer(self, snap: Snapshot) -> None: if self._latest is not None: self.skipped += 1 # overwritten before it could be sent self._latest = snap self._ready.set() async def pump(self) -> None: while True: await self._ready.wait() snap, self._latest = self._latest, None self._ready.clear() await self._send(snap) # a slow client blocks only itself self.sent += 1 class Hub: """One per edge process, per match. Region hubs subscribe to the merged feed.""" def __init__(self): self.connections: set[Connection] = set() def publish(self, snap: Snapshot) -> None: for c in self.connections: # O(connections), no awaits: never blocks c.offer(snap) def renderable(snap: Snapshot, player_media_ms: int, max_age_ms: int = 5000) -> str: """The client's rule: never spoil, never pass stale off as live.""" ends_ms = (snap.media_sec + 1) * 1000 if ends_ms > player_media_ms: return "hold" # describes a moment this viewer has not seen yet if player_media_ms - ends_ms > max_age_ms: return "stale" # show 'catching up', not old excitement as live return "show"
What the test proves

Five revisions are published to a fast client and a slow one. The fast client receives all five. The slow client receives the first and the last, skips the rest, and always ends on the newest; received plus skipped equals published. The render rule holds a snapshot while the viewer is still inside the second it describes, shows it once that second is over, and marks it stale five seconds later.

Two operational notes belong on the whiteboard. Reconnect storms after a regional blip are the fan-out tier's most dangerous moment, so clients reconnect with jittered backoff and are served the latest revision on connect rather than a backlog. And the hub tier is sized for the fan-out math, not the ingest math: a design that scales the stream processor and forgets the sockets has scaled the wrong thing.

§ 08 — INTEGRITYA tap is a claim, not a fact

Every box so far treated a tap as a fact. It is a claim: someone, or something, would like you to believe the crowd feels this way. A script taps faster than any fan. A fan club can be asked to flood 😡 during a rival's innings. And a flood during the final over is a cheap way to knock over a highly visible feature.

The synthetic match contains one scripted cluster: 180 new accounts on a single device fingerprint and network, sending 😡 every second for a minute of quiet play, perfectly on time. Three signals, each weak alone, are combined, and a session is excluded only on two independent signals — because a six makes an honest crowd synchronous too.

SQL · POSTGRESQL 16 · EXECUTED — THREE SIGNALS, TWO REQUIRED
-- Three signals, each weak alone. Computed BEFORE the mood is summed: once a -- scripted voice is in a total, only the raw batches can take it out again. CREATE TABLE session_integrity AS WITH cadence AS ( -- humans jitter; scripts keep perfect time SELECT session_id, count(*) AS batches, stddev_samp(gap_ms) AS gap_jitter_ms FROM ( SELECT session_id, extract(epoch FROM client_sent_at - lag(client_sent_at) OVER (PARTITION BY session_id ORDER BY client_sent_at)) * 1000 AS gap_ms FROM reaction_batch ) g WHERE gap_ms IS NOT NULL GROUP BY session_id ), fp_fanin AS ( -- one device fingerprint, many "people" SELECT device_fp, count(DISTINCT session_id) AS sessions_on_fp FROM reaction_batch GROUP BY device_fp ), s AS ( SELECT DISTINCT ON (b.session_id) b.session_id, b.account_id, b.device_fp, b.asn, m.starts_at - a.created_at AS account_age FROM reaction_batch b JOIN dim_match m USING (match_id) LEFT JOIN dim_account a USING (account_id) ORDER BY b.session_id ) SELECT s.session_id, s.asn, COALESCE(c.batches >= 20 AND c.gap_jitter_ms < 5, false) AS metronomic, f.sessions_on_fp >= 25 AS shared_device, s.account_age < interval '1 day' AS new_account, -- two independent signals, never one: a six makes honest crowds synchronous too ( (COALESCE(c.batches >= 20 AND c.gap_jitter_ms < 5, false))::int + (f.sessions_on_fp >= 25)::int + (s.account_age < interval '1 day')::int ) >= 2 AS excluded FROM s JOIN fp_fanin f USING (device_fp) LEFT JOIN cadence c USING (session_id);

SQL · POSTGRESQL 16 · EXECUTED — THE ANGRY MINUTE, RAW AND CLEANED

On the synthetic data the rule excluded all 180 scripted sessions and none of the 3,000 genuine ones — a property verify.py asserts, not one this page assumes. On real traffic the trade-off between missed scripts and wrongly excluded fans has to be measured and tuned; two-of-three is a starting rule, not a law.

Participation is not sentiment

Even with perfect integrity, the meter measures only the people who chose to tap. The schoolteacher with her eyes shut is not in it, nor the student whose remote is in pieces. A mood meter is a weather vane, not a census: it shows which way expressed participation is blowing among self-selected users, not how a country feels. Label it that way in the product.

And the display changes what it measures. In Salganik, Dodds and Watts' 2006 MusicLab experiment, people who could see what others had chosen produced more unequal and less predictable outcomes than those who could not. A mood meter broadcasts a social signal back to the crowd it is measuring. That makes it a feedback loop, and feedback loops deserve as much design attention as partitions.

§ 10 — THE MONEY CONTRASTThe same boxes with money in them

For a mood meter, losing half a percent of reactions is invisible, and so is counting a few twice. Put money in the same boxes and every one of those tolerances becomes a defect. The same architecture that makes one business successful can make another bankrupt.

DimensionReaction moodPayment platform
Unit of truthThe aggregateEach individual transaction
LossSmall, bounded, measuredNo accepted payment may be lost
DuplicatesBounded inflation tolerablePrevented by design: idempotency keys, unique ids
FreshnessSeconds, staleness labelledThe decision is synchronous; reporting may lag
Under overloadDrop the past, keep the presentRefuse new work explicitly; never accept and lose
AuditUsually noneMandatory, durable, reconciled

Be precise about eventual consistency, because payments use it on purpose. Card authorisation and settlement complete at different times, which is why reconciliation exists at all. Dashboards, reports, fraud features and replicated balance views can lag, provided they say so. The decision cannot: the check that funds exist and the debit that consumes them, the uniqueness of a payment, the atomic pair of ledger entries. Eventual consistency is acceptable for knowing about money. It is unacceptable for moving it.

SQL · POSTGRESQL 16 · EXECUTED — A LEDGER THAT REFUSES TO BE UNBALANCED
-- The same boxes with money in them. Here the record, not the aggregate, is -- the product. Double entry: every transaction's entries sum to zero, checked -- by the database at COMMIT, not by a nightly job. CREATE TABLE wallet ( customer_id BIGINT PRIMARY KEY, balance_minor BIGINT NOT NULL CHECK (balance_minor >= 0) ); CREATE TABLE payment_intent ( intent_id BIGSERIAL PRIMARY KEY, idempotency_key TEXT NOT NULL UNIQUE, -- ★ one charge per intent, forever order_id BIGINT NOT NULL, customer_id BIGINT NOT NULL REFERENCES wallet, amount_minor BIGINT NOT NULL CHECK (amount_minor > 0), created_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now() ); CREATE TABLE ledger_entry ( entry_id BIGSERIAL PRIMARY KEY, txn_id BIGINT NOT NULL REFERENCES payment_intent (intent_id), account TEXT NOT NULL, -- 'customer:42', 'merchant' amount_minor BIGINT NOT NULL CHECK (amount_minor <> 0) ); CREATE FUNCTION assert_balanced() RETURNS trigger LANGUAGE plpgsql AS $$ BEGIN IF (SELECT sum(amount_minor) FROM ledger_entry WHERE txn_id = NEW.txn_id) <> 0 THEN RAISE EXCEPTION 'txn % does not balance', NEW.txn_id; END IF; RETURN NULL; END $$; CREATE CONSTRAINT TRIGGER ledger_balances AFTER INSERT ON ledger_entry DEFERRABLE INITIALLY DEFERRED FOR EACH ROW EXECUTE FUNCTION assert_balanced();
SQL · POSTGRESQL 16 · EXECUTED — CHARGE EXACTLY ONCE
-- Charge exactly once. A retry with the same key returns the original intent -- and moves no money; the same key with a different amount is a client bug, -- refused loudly. The funds check and the debit happen under one row lock. CREATE FUNCTION charge(p_key TEXT, p_order BIGINT, p_customer BIGINT, p_amount BIGINT) RETURNS BIGINT LANGUAGE plpgsql AS $$ DECLARE v_id BIGINT; v_prev payment_intent%ROWTYPE; BEGIN INSERT INTO payment_intent (idempotency_key, order_id, customer_id, amount_minor) VALUES (p_key, p_order, p_customer, p_amount) ON CONFLICT (idempotency_key) DO NOTHING RETURNING intent_id INTO v_id; IF v_id IS NULL THEN -- a replay SELECT * INTO v_prev FROM payment_intent WHERE idempotency_key = p_key; IF v_prev.amount_minor <> p_amount OR v_prev.customer_id <> p_customer THEN RAISE EXCEPTION 'idempotency key % reused with a different request', p_key; END IF; RETURN v_prev.intent_id; END IF; UPDATE wallet SET balance_minor = balance_minor - p_amount WHERE customer_id = p_customer AND balance_minor >= p_amount; IF NOT FOUND THEN RAISE EXCEPTION 'insufficient funds for customer %', p_customer; -- rolls back the intent too END IF; INSERT INTO ledger_entry (txn_id, account, amount_minor) VALUES (v_id, 'customer:' || p_customer, -p_amount), (v_id, 'merchant', p_amount); RETURN v_id; END $$;

§ 11 — AI AGENTSIntelligence is not reliability

The fashionable next step is to put an AI agent in charge of the stadium. Some of that is sensible: an agent reads dashboards faster than a sleepy on-call engineer, correlates a latency spike with a deploy, suggests pre-scaling before the toss, and flags patterns that look coordinated.

Now the final over. Reactions jump forty-fold in three seconds, perfectly synchronised, from every region. An agent taught that synchronised surges look like attacks concludes, in flawless prose, that the platform is under a bot attack, and throttles ingest. It is not an attack. It is a six. The system has suppressed the most genuine moment of the match, and the post-incident summary will be beautifully written.

The answer is not a better prompt. It is a deterministic layer between the agent and production that gives the same verdict for the same inputs, logs every verdict with the evidence it saw, and never calls a model. The agent proposes; this code decides what may happen without a person. It knows something the agent's pattern-matching does not: the ball-by-ball feed. A synchronised surge within twenty media seconds of a boundary or a wicket is expected, and throttling then needs a human.

The rules, in one line each

Agents recommend; bounded deterministic policies act; a human approves anything irreversible or wide in blast radius; every action is logged with the hash of the evidence it saw; and the kill switch never depends on the agent. A system that is usually brilliant and occasionally confidently wrong needs a supervisor, not a promotion. (The longer argument is in The Supervised Agentic Enterprise.)

§ 12 — FAILUREWhen the stadium goes silent

Each of these will happen. The only choice is whether the response was designed or improvised live, in the final over, by whoever is on call.

The degradation ladder — written before the match

LevelTrigger IllustrativeBehaviourApproval

The principles are few. Degrade gracefully: the meter is a garnish and must never take down the meal. Isolate faults so one region or tier fails alone. Keep the log wherever you might need to recount. Set recovery objectives per feature; the meter's honest one may be "lose it and move on". And rehearse. Netflix built Chaos Monkey in 2011 to terminate production instances on purpose, because the only failure plan you can trust is one that has already met failure. Before a tournament, the drills write themselves: kill a region during a replayed final over, inject a scripted flood, stall the aggregator for thirty seconds, and drop a third of the hubs. Resilience is designed in. You cannot buy it as a managed service any more than you can buy fitness with a gym membership.

§ 14 — THE RUBRICWhat was actually being tested

The architect's six questions, before any technology

1 · OUTCOME
What is the business outcome? "Make the match feel shared" and "move money exactly once" are different systems: one sells the aggregate, the other the record.
2 · CORRECTNESS
What exactly must be correct? Name the invariant. Here: one voice per person-second, no spoilers, stale never shown as live. For payments: one charge per intent, a balanced ledger.
3 · FRESHNESS
How fresh, and against which clock? Wall time, event time, or the viewer's own place in the stream.
4 · TOLERANCE
How much inconsistency or loss is tolerable? Put a number on it. A number can be tested; "minimal" cannot.
5 · FAILURE
What happens during failure? Write the degradation ladder before the happy path, or the system will choose one for you, live.
6 · COST
What does the guarantee cost? Exactly-once, multi-region state and durable logs cost money and pager hours. Pay where the invariant demands it, nowhere else.

If you get sixty seconds, say this

"Before tech: two seconds on which clock? Streams are many seconds behind live and unevenly so, so I key the mood on media time — the position each viewer was watching — and promise freshness on the viewer's own timeline, with zero spoilers. Phones batch taps by media second; the edge admits, deduplicates on session and sequence, makes one voice per person per second, and flushes additive deltas every 250 ms, so the aggregator just sums. No window on the live path: each second's mood is a revision that fills in as later viewers arrive. Fan-out is the real load — 12.5 million messages a second — so each socket gets a latest-only slot, not a queue. Integrity runs before the sum on two independent signals. The log keeps every batch for recounts, and the SLO is measured from render receipts. And if this were payments, I would throw half of this away and start from an idempotent, double-entry ledger."

The probes that decide it

"WHY NOT A 2 S WINDOW?"
It spends the whole budget by itself, and in media time it never closes until the slowest viewer arrives. Updating aggregates with revisions give the fast viewers something now and everyone the full answer later.
"KAFKA KEY?"
Never the match: one partition takes the country (64× fair share in the measurement above). Key by session, which also gives each edge node the per-session state it needs.
"EXACTLY-ONCE?"
Not end to end, and not needed. Idempotent ingest on the batch key gives effectively-once counting where it matters; the rest is measured loss. For the ledger, yes — enforced by the database at commit.
"WHAT IF A REGION DIES?"
The region's hubs and edges fail alone; the merged mood is marked partial; the video is untouched; clients see "paused", not a fake number.
"HOW DO YOU KNOW IT WORKS?"
Render receipts: p95 freshness on the viewer's timeline and a spoiler count that must be zero, plus the conservation funnel. Not CPU graphs.

Common mistakes

Naming the stack in the first minute. The interviewer learns what you have used, not how you decide.
Measuring two seconds on the wall clock. Builds a spoiler engine with excellent latency.
Counting taps as votes. Hands the mood to the fastest thumb and the cheapest script.
Sizing ingest and forgetting fan-out. The load going out is several times the load coming in.
A queue per socket. Slow phones fall further behind forever; latest-only is the right backpressure here.
Letting an agent throttle on one signal. A six is a synchronised surge.
A good engineer knows how to move twenty-five million events. A good architect knows which events matter, how much truth can be sacrificed for speed, and when sacrificing truth is unacceptable.— Closing argument

So the question to leave with every team proud of its latency charts: as our systems get faster, are they becoming more trustworthy — or just quicker at being confidently wrong?
