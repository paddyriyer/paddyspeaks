फलस्तुतिःThe Fruits of Recitation

phalastutiḥ

41 mantras · 12.1–12.41

❁

Overview

The Devī, still before the gods who have just praised her, speaks (12.1). Whoever praises her daily with these hymns, with a collected mind, she will rid of every affliction (12.2). Those who recite the three stories — Madhu and Kaiṭabha, Mahiṣa, Śumbha and Niśumbha (12.3) — or hear her Māhātmya with devotion on the eighth, fourteenth and ninth lunar days (12.4) will know no sin, no misfortune born of sin, no poverty, no parting from those they love (12.5), and no fear of enemies, robbers, the king, weapons, fire or flood (12.6). The text is therefore to be recited and heard: it is the highest svastyayana (12.7). It quells the calamities of pestilence and the threefold portent (12.8); where it is rightly recited every day in her shrine she will never leave (12.9). It is to be recited at offerings, worship, the fire rite and festivals (12.10), and she accepts worship done with knowledge or without it (12.11). Heard at the great yearly worship of autumn, it frees a man from every trouble and gives him wealth, grain and sons (12.12–13); it makes him fearless, destroys his enemies and makes his family flourish (12.14–15). It is to be heard at rites of pacification, after evil dreams and under planetary affliction, and turns the evil dream into a good one (12.16–17); it brings peace to children seized by bālagrahas and reconciles men who have fallen apart (12.18), breaks the power of the wicked and, by its recitation alone, destroys rākṣasas, bhūtas and piśācas (12.19). The whole Māhātmya makes her present (12.20); the delight a year of offerings gives her — animals, flowers, arghya, incense, perfume, lamps, feeding of brāhmaṇas, homa, sprinklings and gifts — one hearing of this story gives her too, and, heard, it takes away sins and grants health (12.20–22). The telling of her births protects from evil beings, the story of her battles removes the fear of enemies, and the hymns of the gods, of the brahmarṣis and of Brahmā grant an auspicious mind (12.23–25). In a forest fire, among robbers or enemies, pursued by beasts, condemned by a king, in chains, in a storm at sea, in battle or in pain, the one who remembers her story is set free; beasts, robbers and enemies flee from him from afar (12.25–30). The seer resumes (12.31): Caṇḍikā vanishes as the gods watch (12.32–33); the gods take up their offices and their shares of the sacrifice again (12.33–34), and, with Śumbha and Niśumbha slain, the Daityas who are left go down to Pātāla (12.34–35). Medhas then gives King Suratha the lesson: eternal as she is, the Goddess comes forth again and again to protect the world (12.36); she deludes the universe and gives it birth, and grants knowledge when entreated and prosperity when pleased (12.37); the cosmic egg is pervaded by her as Mahākālī, who at the great time takes the form of Mahāmārī (12.38); she is dissolution, creation and preservation (12.39), Lakṣmī in good times and Alakṣmī in decline (12.40); and, praised and worshipped, she gives wealth, sons, a mind set on dharma and a good destiny (12.41).

About this chapter
Forms of the Devī
The Devī, speaking in the first person through the whole phalaśruti (12.1–30): the one who destroys affliction (12.2), who abides where her text is recited (12.9, 12.20), and who accepts worship with love (12.11, 12.22). In the narrator’s voice she is the Blessed Caṇḍikā, fierce in valour (भगवती चण्डिका चण्डविक्रमा, 12.32), and the Blessed Goddess, eternal and still arising again and again (भगवती देवी … नित्या अपि पुनः पुनः सम्भूय, 12.36). Medhas’s closing verses name her powers: she who deludes the universe and brings it forth (12.37); Mahākālī, whose form at the great time is Mahāmārī, the great Death (12.38; the same word names the pestilence in 12.8); the unborn (अजा) and the eternal (सनातनी), who is creation and preservation as well as dissolution (12.39); Lakṣmī, giver of increase, and Alakṣmī, for ruin (12.40). Her ‘births’ (जन्मनाम्, 12.23) and ‘auspicious manifestations’ (उत्पत्तयः शुभाः, 12.14) are her appearances in the three stories. Mahākālī here is a name used in its own sense, not the liturgical presiding form of the first carita.
Significance of the events
The chapter takes its name from its content (colophon: फलस्तुतिः). Its distinction is that the phalaśruti is spoken by the Devī herself, not by a narrator or a later editor: in her own voice she names the three stories (12.3), the days (12.4, 12.12), the occasions (12.10, 12.16) and the fruits of reciting, hearing and remembering them. It thus defines the text as a practice and lays the foundation on which the Saptaśatī is used in Śākta worship — daily pāṭha, the autumn Navarātra, rites of pacification. The narrative then closes the third story: the gods regain their offices and their shares of the sacrifice (12.33–34), and the surviving Daityas go to Pātāla (12.35), the terms the Devī sent to Śumbha in 8.26. Finally Medhas turns back to the king and restates the teaching he began with in chapter 1 — the Goddess deludes the world and gives it birth (12.37, as 1.55–56), and is eternal though she ‘arises’ (12.36, as 1.64–66) — so that the three stories stand between two statements of the same doctrine. Chapter 13 returns to Suratha and the merchant.
Sanskrit patterns to watch
A first-person speech of promise, and so a chapter of futures: स्तोष्यते, नाशयिष्यामि (12.2), कीर्तयिष्यन्ति (12.3), श्रोष्यन्ति (12.4), भविष्यति (12.5, 12.13), सम्भविष्यति (12.6), विमोक्ष्यामि (12.9) and the epic future प्रतीच्छिष्यामि (12.11), mostly in relative–correlative sentences (यः … तस्य, 12.2; ये … तेषाम्, 12.3–5; या … सा, 12.21–22). Gerundives command the practice (पठितव्यम्, श्रोतव्यम्, with an instrumental agent, 12.7; उच्चार्यम्, श्राव्यम्, 12.10) and optatives prescribe it or state its power (शमयेत्, 12.8; शृणुयात्, 12.16; मुच्येत, 12.29). Passives (पठ्यते, 12.9; क्रियते, 12.12, 12.22; मोह्यते, 12.37), locatives of occasion (12.4, 12.10, 12.16), locative absolutes (सुचरिते श्रुते, 12.22; तस्मिन् श्रुते, 12.24; पतत्सु शस्त्रेषु, 12.28; शुम्भे निहते, 12.34–35) and ablatives in -तः (शत्रुतः, दस्युतः, राजतः, 12.6) are frequent. Taddhita abstracts (दारिद्र्यम्, 12.5; सांनिध्यम्, 12.9; आरोग्यम्, 12.22) and agent compounds (शान्तिकारकम्, 12.18; बलहानिकरम्, 12.19; सन्निधिकारकम्, 12.20) describe the text’s effects. The learner also meets epic forms (नन्दते, 12.15; the neuter दुःस्वप्नम्, 12.17; the nominative उत्पत्तयः after श्रुत्वा, 12.14), the irregular stems पुंस् (पुमान्, 12.14; पुंसाम्, 12.15, 12.24) and नृ (नृभिः, नृणाम्), and, in the closing narrative, the imperfect अन्तरधीयत (12.33), the perfects चक्रुः and आययुः (12.34–35) and the absolutive सम्भूय (12.36).
The teaching, as distinct from the narrative
Literally, the chapter is a list of promises: recite, hear or remember this text, and sin, poverty, fear, disease, evil dreams and danger will leave you; then the Goddess vanishes, the gods return to their places, and Medhas tells the king what she is. The traditional uses of the chapter — the Navarātra pāṭha (12.4, 12.12), the recitation within every rite and the caṇḍī-homa (12.10), 12.13 recited as a sampuṭa — are the tradition’s, and the commentary presents them as such. As teaching, the chapter shifts the weight of worship from the rite to attention: she accepts what is offered in ignorance (12.11); one hearing equals a year of offerings (12.22); and in danger, where no rite can be performed, what saves is remembering (स्मरन्, 12.29). Medhas’s close joins this to the doctrine of chapter 1: the power that deludes the world is the power that gives knowledge when asked (12.37), and fortune and misfortune, creation and dissolution, are all hers (12.38–40). Reading the ‘enemies’ of 12.15 as inner passions is a modern reflection, not the verse’s literal sense.
Carita
Uttama Carita (the third episode, chapters 5–13), of which this is the twelfth chapter of the text and the second-last of the carita. In the recitation tradition its presiding form is Mahāsarasvatī; this is the Gita Press viniyoga (printed before chapter 5), not a statement of the Purāṇa text. In the Gita Press pāṭha chapter 12 has no viniyoga of its own, only a dhyāna, of Durgā: radiant as a garland of lightning, seated on the shoulder of the lion, terrible, attended by maidens with sword and shield in hand, holding in her hands the discus, mace, sword, shield, arrows, bow, bowstring (गुणम्) and the threatening gesture (तर्जनीम्), fiery in nature, bearing the moon, three-eyed. The dhyāna is liturgy; the chapter itself names Caṇḍikā, Mahākālī, Lakṣmī and Alakṣmī, not Durgā.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

The Devī answers the gods. At the end of chapter 11 she has granted them a boon and promised to return whenever demons oppress the world (11.37–55); now, still before them, she adds her own account of what the recitation of her deeds will bring. The speech runs to 12.30, after which Medhas resumes the narrative (12.31). In recitation this mantra carries the bīja ॐ, which belongs to the pāṭha, not to the verse.

    Future of the causative stem नाशय- (√नश् with vṛddhi + णिच्) + इष्य + मि: ‘I will cause to perish’.

A relative–correlative sentence: the relative clause (यः … स्तोष्यते) describes the devotee; the main clause, with अहम् as subject, gives the promise.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

The first promise of the phalaśruti. ‘These hymns’ (एभिः स्तवैः) are the praises the gods have just sung — above all the hymn of chapter 11 — and, by extension, the other hymns of the text (chapters 1, 4 and 5). The condition is twofold: regularity (नित्यम्) and attention (समाहितः).

Traditional interpretation

In the Śākta tradition this verse is cited to justify the daily recitation of the hymns of the Saptaśatī on their own, apart from the whole text, when time does not allow the full pāṭha.

Modern reflection

A modern reading: the promise is made not to the learned but to the attentive. What the verse asks for is steadiness of mind, not ritual elaboration.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

A second list of promises, of dangers from outside: enemies, robbers, the king’s anger, weapons, fire, water. The subject moves from the plural तेषाम् of 12.5 to the generic singular तस्य, ‘(any) such person’.

Modern reflection

A modern reading: the list is the list of an ancient household’s real fears — war, theft, the arbitrary ruler, fire, flood. The promise answers the insecurity of ordinary life, not only of the soul.

Direct textual meaning

The Devī promises her permanent presence (सांनिध्य) wherever the text is recited correctly and regularly. The condition is again twofold, as in 12.2: correctness (सम्यक्) and regularity (नित्यम्).

A modern reading: the promise is attached to a practice, not to a building. Presence is said to follow faithful, regular attention, wherever it is given.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

The Devī singles out the great annual worship in autumn. Hearing the text then, with devotion, brings the fruits listed in the next verse.

Traditional interpretation

This verse is the classic textual warrant for the Śāradīya Navarātra (Durgā Pūjā), the autumn festival in which the Saptaśatī is recited over nine nights; the aṣṭamī and navamī of 12.4 are its central days.

Direct textual meaning

Completing 12.12: the devotee who hears the text at the autumn worship is freed from afflictions and prospers in wealth, grain and offspring — and the Devī adds her own guarantee, न संशयः.

A modern reading: the blessing is concrete — freedom from trouble, food, family — and it is said to come ‘by my grace’ (मत्प्रसादेन), not as the automatic wage of a rite.

Three locatives of occasion and one prescriptive optative, with an unexpressed generic subject (‘one’).

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

The Devī prescribes her Māhātmya for three occasions of śānti (averting harm): propitiatory rites in general, bad dreams, and planetary afflictions. The next verse gives the result for each.

Traditional interpretation

On this verse the tradition prescribes the Saptaśatī pāṭha as a remedy in the rites of pacification connected with dreams and planets, in place of or alongside other propitiatory rites.

Direct textual meaning

Two nominal predicates of an unexpressed subject, the Māhātmya (named in 12.16): ‘(it is) a bringer of peace … and the best means of friendship …’.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

Two further uses of the text: for children afflicted by bālagrahas, and for reconciling men whose union has broken. The subject, the Māhātmya, is carried over from the previous verses.

Traditional interpretation

बालग्रह, ‘seizers of children’, are a class of beings named in the medical and Purāṇic literature as the cause of children’s illnesses; rites of protection against them were part of household religion, and the Devī here offers her Māhātmya as such a rite.

The first line closes the relative sentence of 12.20–21: ‘the satisfaction (या प्रीतिः) that is produced in me by a year of offerings — that (सा) is produced when this story is heard once’; क्रियते is understood again with सा. The second line is a new sentence with श्रुतम् as subject and two verbs.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

The Devī completes the comparison begun in 12.20: animal offerings, flowers, arghya, incense, perfume, lamps, the feeding of brāhmaṇas, homa, sprinklings, and gifts kept up for a year please her no more than one hearing (सकृत्) of this story. The second line adds two further fruits of hearing: sins removed, health granted.

Traditional interpretation

The recitation tradition draws from verses like this its high estimate of the pāṭha itself: hearing or reciting the text is counted as worship in its own right, not as an accompaniment to ritual. The verse does not dismiss the offerings; it measures the hearing by them.

Modern reflection

A modern reading: the verse sets a year of outward effort beside one moment of attentive listening and finds them equal. What it values is the attention, not the quantity.

A modern reading: the verse says ‘remembering’, not ‘reciting’. In a burning forest or a storm at sea no book or ritual is to hand; what one carries inside is what is left.

The first line finishes the sentence of 12.33b with its verb चक्रुः. The second line begins a new sentence: its subject दैत्याः, a chain of locative absolutes (शुम्भे निहते … निशुम्भे च …), and the verb आययुः in 12.35.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

The cosmic order is restored: each god holds his office again and receives his share of the sacrifice (यज्ञभाग), the right Śumbha had seized (5.2) and boasted of (5.108). The second line turns to the defeated: the Daityas, whose fate is told in 12.35.

Traditional interpretation

The verse fulfils, almost word for word, the terms the Devī sent to Śumbha through Śiva as her messenger (8.26): ‘let Indra regain the three worlds, let the gods enjoy the oblations (हविर्भुजः); you go to Pātāla if you wish to live.’ The return of the sacrificial shares is the sign, in Purāṇic thought, that the world is in order again.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

The surviving demons are not destroyed: they go to Pātāla, the netherworld. This is the alternative the Devī offered them before the battle, in the message carried by Śiva (8.26): ‘go to Pātāla, if you wish to live’. With 12.34 the terms of that message are carried out in full.

Traditional interpretation

That the remnant survives in Pātāla is read as why the Goddess must come again (12.36; 11.54–55): adharma is driven back, not abolished.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

Medhas turns from the story to King Suratha (भूप) and states its lesson. It answers the paradox he set out at the beginning: she is eternal (नित्यैव सा, 1.64), yet is said to arise (सा नित्याप्यभिधीयते, 1.66). The same words return here: नित्या अपि … सम्भूय.

Traditional interpretation

The verse joins the three caritas into one pattern: each time the world is threatened she appears, acts and withdraws — the pattern the Devī herself promised in 11.54–55 (‘whenever trouble born of the demons arises, then I shall descend’).

Comparative Śrīvidyā notes

Comparative Śākta / Śrīvidyā

Lalitā Sahasranāma (Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa) has Nityā as a name (name 136, नित्या). The two texts share the idea of a Goddess who is eternal and still manifests.

Morphology & anvaya

  • मोह्यते mohyate

    Passive of the causative मोहयति ‘causes to be bewildered’: the causative -अय- is dropped before the passive -य-: मोह्-य-ते. ‘Is made deluded (by her)’. Compare 1.55, तया सम्मोह्यते जगत्.

  • प्रसूयते prasūyate

    From the divādi root सू ‘to bring forth’, which is ātmanepada (सूयते): an active verb with विश्वम् as object. Read as the passive of the adādi root (सूते, passive सूयते) it would mean ‘is born’, which does not fit the nominative सा with the accusative विश्वम्.

  • याचिता yācitā

    √याच् takes two objects (‘to ask someone for something’); in the passive participle the person asked becomes the subject (सा याचिता, ‘she, being asked’), and विज्ञानम् may be taken either as the thing asked for or as the object of प्रयच्छति. Either way she gives knowledge when asked.

Two balanced pairs: a passive and an active sentence about the universe (deluded by her, brought forth by her), and two participles of condition (याचिता, तुष्टा) each with its gift, sharing the verb प्रयच्छति.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

Medhas gathers the Goddess’s powers in four clauses: she deludes the world, she brings it forth, she gives knowledge when asked, and prosperity when pleased. The same power that binds (मोह्यते) also releases (विज्ञानम्).

Traditional interpretation

The verse repeats Medhas’s first teaching in chapter 1: Mahāmāyā deludes the world (तया सम्मोह्यते जगत्, 1.55), by her the universe is emitted (तया विसृज्यते विश्वम्, 1.56), and when gracious she becomes the cause of liberation (1.57). The story has been told between the two statements.

Comparative Śrīvidyā notes

Comparative Śākta / Śrīvidyā

Lalitā Sahasranāma (Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa) names her as the one who deludes and the one who gives birth to the world: मोहिनी (name 562) and वियदादिजगत्प्रसूः, ‘mother of the world beginning with space’ (name 550). The parallel is of idea, not of text.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

The three works of the Goddess, each introduced by सा एव, ‘she alone’: dissolution (महामारी), creation (सृष्टिः) and preservation (स्थितिः). The list begins with destruction, continuing from 12.38. Traditional interpretation

The gods’ hymn named her the power (शक्ति) of creation, preservation and destruction (11.11), and Brahmā’s hymn said she is creation in emission and preservation in sustaining (1.76). Here Medhas states the same as plain doctrine to Suratha.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

Fortune and misfortune are both her forms. The two lines mirror each other word by word: the time of flourishing and the time of want, Lakṣmī and Alakṣmī, increase and ruin.

Traditional interpretation

The gods’ hymn in chapter 4 had said the same: she is Śrī herself in the homes of the virtuous and Alakṣmī in the homes of the wicked (या श्रीः स्वयं सुकृतिनां भवनेष्वलक्ष्मीः पापात्मनाम्, 4.5). There the difference is the householder’s conduct; here it is the season of fortune.

Modern reflection

A modern reading: the verse refuses to divide the world into a good power and a hostile one. Gain and loss come from the same source; what changes is the time.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

The chapter of the fruits of recitation ends with a list of gifts that rises in order: wealth, children, a mind turned to dharma, and a good destiny. Praise (स्तुता) and worship (सम्पूजिता) are named together, as hymn and offering were in 12.20–24.

Traditional interpretation

The closing ॐ (bīja) printed after this mantra belongs to the Gita Press pāṭha, not to the Purāṇa. The next chapter repeats the promise in Medhas’s last counsel to Suratha: worshipped, she grants enjoyment, heaven and final release (13.5).

Modern reflection

A modern reading: the list does not stop at possessions. The last two gifts — a mind inclined to what is right, and a good end — are the ones no one can take away.

Reflecting on the chapter

Before she leaves, the Goddess tells the gods what the story of her deeds will do for those who keep it. Whoever praises her with these hymns, recites the three stories or hears them on the sacred days is freed from sin, poverty, fear, pestilence, evil dreams and the malice of spirits; where it is recited she is present; one hearing pleases her as much as a year of costly worship; and anyone who remembers her story in fire, flood, prison, battle or pain is set free. Then she vanishes before their eyes. The gods take back their offices and their shares of the sacrifice, and the surviving Daityas withdraw to Pātāla. Medhas gives the king the meaning of everything he has heard: the eternal Goddess comes forth again and again to protect the world; she deludes it and gives it birth, ends it, creates and sustains it; she is fortune and misfortune; and, praised and worshipped, she gives wealth, children, a mind set on dharma and a good end.

Śākta insights

  • The Goddess vouches for her own text: the phalaśruti is spoken in her voice, and its condition is attention and devotion — समाहितः, भक्त्या, एकचेतसः (12.2, 12.4, 12.7) — not learning.
  • Worship is accepted for love, not for correctness: she takes what is offered knowingly or unknowingly (12.11), and one hearing of her story gives her the delight of a year of offerings (12.20–22).
  • Remembrance is the last refuge: in fire, captivity, storm, battle and pain, where no rite is possible, the one who remembers her story is freed (12.25–30).
  • Manifestation is her way of protecting: eternal, she comes forth again and again (12.36), and withdraws when the work is done (12.33) — the pattern of all three stories.
  • One power binds and frees: she deludes the universe and gives knowledge when asked (12.37), returning to Medhas’s first teaching (1.55–57).
  • Nothing is outside her: dissolution, creation and preservation (12.39), Lakṣmī and Alakṣmī (12.40) are all her forms.

Śrīvidyā connections

The chapter shares names with the Lalitā Sahasranāma (Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa), noted on the verses: for the promised fruits पापनाशिनी (167) and सर्वापद्विनिवारिणी (913, 12.5), भयापहा (121, 12.6), स्वस्तिमती (448, 12.7) and सर्वव्याधिप्रशमनी (551, 12.8); for the Goddess herself चण्डिका (755) and भगवती (279, 12.32), नित्या (136, 12.36), मोहिनी (562) and वियदादिजगत्प्रसूः (550, 12.37), महाकाली (751, 12.38), and the triad सृष्टिकर्त्री (264), गोप्त्री (266), संहारिणी (268) with अजा (866, 12.39). These are shared Śākta names and ideas, not evidence that one text depends on the other. The chapter states no specifically Śrīvidyā doctrine, and its dhyāna in the Gita Press pāṭha is of Durgā, not of a Śrīvidyā form.

  • Visarga sandhi throughout: -aḥ → -o before voiced sounds (शत्रुतो न, 12.6; नरो मुच्येत, 12.29; आघूर्णितो वा, 12.27); visarga → ś/s before c/t (स्तवैश्च, 12.2; ग्रहपीडाश्च, 12.17; कृतास्तास्तु, 12.25; वैरिणस्तथा, 12.29); visarga after other vowels → r before voiced sounds (प्रीतिर्मे, 12.22; नृभिर्दृष्टम्, 12.17; सृष्टिर्भवति, 12.39; चक्रुर्विनिहतारयः, 12.34); and visarga kept before a sibilant (रिपवः संक्षयम्, 12.15; उपसर्गाः शमम्, 12.17).
  • Vṛddhi in recurring formulas: ममैतन्माहात्म्यम् (12.7, 12.12, 12.14, 12.20), ममैतच्चरितम् (12.10, 12.29), सैव (12.37, 12.39, 12.40), चैव and चैक- (12.4, 12.5).
  • Final t → n before a nasal (तस्मान्मम, 12.7; शमयेन्मम, 12.8; शृणुयान्मम, 12.16; यन्मे, 12.23), → c before c (एतच्चरितम्, 12.10), voiced before voiced sounds (तद्वद् वधम्, 12.3; पठनादेव, 12.19; दूरादेव, 12.30); n + ś → ñch (तस्मिञ्छ्रुते, 12.24); n → ṃs/ṃś before t/c (उपसर्गानशेषांस्तु, 12.8; पुत्रांश्च, 12.41); k → ṅ before n (सम्यङ् नित्यम्, 12.9).
  • Pūrvarūpa with avagraha (तेऽपि, 12.33; बन्धगतोऽपि, 12.27; वेदनाभ्यर्दितोऽपि, 12.28; महोग्रेऽतुलविक्रमे, 12.35); the print also marks some savarṇa-dīrgha junctions with avagraha (जानताऽजानता, 12.11; तथाऽऽरोग्यम्, 12.22; तथाऽलक्ष्मीः, 12.40).
  • One declared hiatus: तुष्टा | ऋद्धिम् (12.37), required by the metre.
Samāsa constructions
  • Dvandvas for the lists of the phalaśruti: itaretara (शुम्भनिशुम्भयोः, 12.3; रक्षोभूतपिशाचानाम्, 12.19; पशुपुष्पार्घ्यधूपैः, गन्धदीपैः, 12.20) and samāhāra (शस्त्रानलतोयौघात्, 12.6; अहर्निशम्, 12.21), and dvandvas nested in tatpuruṣas (मधुकैटभनाशम्, 12.3; धनधान्यसुतान्वितः, 12.13; सिंहव्याघ्रानुयातः, 12.26).
  • Tatpuruṣas with participles for the states of the hearer: tṛtīyā (भक्तिसमन्वितः, 12.12; सर्वाबाधाविनिर्मुक्तः, 12.13; दावाग्निपरिवारितः, 12.25; वेदनाभ्यर्दितः, 12.28), dvitīyā (बन्धगतः, 12.27), pañcamī (दुष्कृतोत्थाः, 12.5), caturthī (शान्तिकर्मणि, 12.16), saptamī (वह्निहोमम्, 12.11; वनहस्तिभिः, 12.26).
  • Agent compounds for the text’s effects and the actors: ṣaṣṭhī tatpuruṣas in -कारक (शान्तिकारकम्, 12.18; सन्निधिकारकम्, 12.20) and upapada tatpuruṣas (बलहानिकरम्, 12.19; यज्ञभागभुजः, 12.34; जगद्विध्वंसिनि, 12.35; भूप, 12.36; वृद्धिप्रदा, 12.40).
  • Prādi and nañ compounds of good and ill (दुष्कृतम्, 12.5; दुःस्वप्नम्, सुस्वप्नम्, 12.17; सुचरिते, 12.22; अलक्ष्मीः, 12.40), an avyayībhāva (असंशयम्, 12.2), bahuvrīhis of state and epithet (एकचेतसः, 12.4; निर्भयः, 12.14; चण्डविक्रमा, 12.32; निरातङ्काः, 12.33; विनिहतारयः, 12.34; अतुलविक्रमे, महावीर्ये, 12.35; महामारीस्वरूपया, 12.38), and karmadhārayas of the divine names (महाकाल्या, 12.38; महामारी, 12.39).
Grammatical observations
  • Several sentences run across verses (12.3–5, 12.12–13, 12.20–22, 12.23–24, 12.24–25, 12.25–29, 12.29–30, 12.32–33, 12.33–34, 12.34–35); every such anvaya says so.
  • Epic (ārṣa) forms are kept and explained, not corrected: the future प्रतीच्छिष्यामि on the present stem इच्छ- (12.11), ātmanepada नन्दते (12.15), the nominative उत्पत्तयः as object of श्रुत्वा (12.14), and the neuter दुःस्वप्नम् / सुस्वप्नम् (12.17).
  • वार्षिकी (12.12) is read as ‘annual’ (from वर्ष ‘year’), not ‘of the rainy season’; प्रसूयते (12.37) as the active ātmanepada of the divādi root, not a passive.
  • स्मरतः (12.30) is read as the ablative with a verb of fleeing; a genitive is possible. महाकाले (12.38) is taken with महामारीस्वरूपया; it could go with व्याप्तम्.
  • The three epithets of 12.35a are taken with तस्मिन् = Śumbha, resuming 12.34; grammatically they could qualify निशुम्भे.
  • 12.30 and 12.32 are half-ślokas (ardha) counted as mantras of their own, as the colophon count requires; the ॐ of 12.1 and 12.41 is the pāṭha’s bīja, not part of the verse.
Philosophical themes
  • The Devī’s phalaśruti in her own voice
  • Hearing and remembrance as worship
  • The restoration of the gods and the end of the third story
  • The eternal Goddess who arises again and again
Open questions
  • 12.7: स्वस्त्ययनं हि तत् (Gita Press) / स्वस्त्ययनं महत् (GRETIL, Freund) — Textual Verification Required; the sense is hardly affected.
  • 12.13: धनधान्यसुतान्वितः (Gita Press) / धनधान्यसमन्वितः (GRETIL, Freund, without ‘sons’) — Textual Verification Required.
  • 12.33: पश्यतामेव देवानां (Gita Press) / पश्यतां सर्वदेवानां (GRETIL, Freund) — Textual Verification Required; the sense is the same.
  • 12.35: जगद्विध्वंसिनि (Gita Press) / जगद्विध्वंसके (GRETIL, Freund) — Textual Verification Required; the variant gives the regular anuṣṭubh cadence, the Gita Press reading a light sixth syllable.
  • Interpretive doubts, none about the reading itself, are flagged on 12.11 (तथा कृताम्: ‘performed thus’ or ‘likewise performed’), 12.12 (वार्षिकी), 12.14 (nominative उत्पत्तयः), 12.17 (neuter दुःस्वप्नम्), 12.21 (या as the relative of प्रीतिः in 12.22) and 12.35 (the referent of the epithets); 12.7, 12.13, 12.33 and 12.35 carry the variants above.
