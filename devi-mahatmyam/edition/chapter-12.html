फलस्तुतिः

phalastutiḥ

The Fruits of Recitation · Complete with Noted Variants

Introduction

Summary
The Devī, still before the gods who have just praised her, speaks (12.1). Whoever praises her daily with these hymns, with a collected mind, she will rid of every affliction (12.2). Those who recite the three stories — Madhu and Kaiṭabha, Mahiṣa, Śumbha and Niśumbha (12.3) — or hear her Māhātmya with devotion on the eighth, fourteenth and ninth lunar days (12.4) will know no sin, no misfortune born of sin, no poverty, no parting from those they love (12.5), and no fear of enemies, robbers, the king, weapons, fire or flood (12.6). The text is therefore to be recited and heard: it is the highest svastyayana (12.7). It quells the calamities of pestilence and the threefold portent (12.8); where it is rightly recited every day in her shrine she will never leave (12.9). It is to be recited at offerings, worship, the fire rite and festivals (12.10), and she accepts worship done with knowledge or without it (12.11). Heard at the great yearly worship of autumn, it frees a man from every trouble and gives him wealth, grain and sons (12.12–13); it makes him fearless, destroys his enemies and makes his family flourish (12.14–15). It is to be heard at rites of pacification, after evil dreams and under planetary affliction, and turns the evil dream into a good one (12.16–17); it brings peace to children seized by bālagrahas and reconciles men who have fallen apart (12.18), breaks the power of the wicked and, by its recitation alone, destroys rākṣasas, bhūtas and piśācas (12.19). The whole Māhātmya makes her present (12.20); the delight a year of offerings gives her — animals, flowers, arghya, incense, perfume, lamps, feeding of brāhmaṇas, homa, sprinklings and gifts — one hearing of this story gives her too, and, heard, it takes away sins and grants health (12.20–22). The telling of her births protects from evil beings, the story of her battles removes the fear of enemies, and the hymns of the gods, of the brahmarṣis and of Brahmā grant an auspicious mind (12.23–25). In a forest fire, among robbers or enemies, pursued by beasts, condemned by a king, in chains, in a storm at sea, in battle or in pain, the one who remembers her story is set free; beasts, robbers and enemies flee from him from afar (12.25–30). The seer resumes (12.31): Caṇḍikā vanishes as the gods watch (12.32–33); the gods take up their offices and their shares of the sacrifice again (12.33–34), and, with Śumbha and Niśumbha slain, the Daityas who are left go down to Pātāla (12.34–35). Medhas then gives King Suratha the lesson: eternal as she is, the Goddess comes forth again and again to protect the world (12.36); she deludes the universe and gives it birth, and grants knowledge when entreated and prosperity when pleased (12.37); the cosmic egg is pervaded by her as Mahākālī, who at the great time takes the form of Mahāmārī (12.38); she is dissolution, creation and preservation (12.39), Lakṣmī in good times and Alakṣmī in decline (12.40); and, praised and worshipped, she gives wealth, sons, a mind set on dharma and a good destiny (12.41).
Forms of the Devī
The Devī, speaking in the first person through the whole phalaśruti (12.1–30): the one who destroys affliction (12.2), who abides where her text is recited (12.9, 12.20), and who accepts worship with love (12.11, 12.22). In the narrator’s voice she is the Blessed Caṇḍikā, fierce in valour (भगवती चण्डिका चण्डविक्रमा, 12.32), and the Blessed Goddess, eternal and still arising again and again (भगवती देवी … नित्या अपि पुनः पुनः सम्भूय, 12.36). Medhas’s closing verses name her powers: she who deludes the universe and brings it forth (12.37); Mahākālī, whose form at the great time is Mahāmārī, the great Death (12.38; the same word names the pestilence in 12.8); the unborn (अजा) and the eternal (सनातनी), who is creation and preservation as well as dissolution (12.39); Lakṣmī, giver of increase, and Alakṣmī, for ruin (12.40). Her ‘births’ (जन्मनाम्, 12.23) and ‘auspicious manifestations’ (उत्पत्तयः शुभाः, 12.14) are her appearances in the three stories. Mahākālī here is a name used in its own sense, not the liturgical presiding form of the first carita.
Significance of the events
The chapter takes its name from its content (colophon: फलस्तुतिः). Its distinction is that the phalaśruti is spoken by the Devī herself, not by a narrator or a later editor: in her own voice she names the three stories (12.3), the days (12.4, 12.12), the occasions (12.10, 12.16) and the fruits of reciting, hearing and remembering them. It thus defines the text as a practice and lays the foundation on which the Saptaśatī is used in Śākta worship — daily pāṭha, the autumn Navarātra, rites of pacification. The narrative then closes the third story: the gods regain their offices and their shares of the sacrifice (12.33–34), and the surviving Daityas go to Pātāla (12.35), the terms the Devī sent to Śumbha in 8.26. Finally Medhas turns back to the king and restates the teaching he began with in chapter 1 — the Goddess deludes the world and gives it birth (12.37, as 1.55–56), and is eternal though she ‘arises’ (12.36, as 1.64–66) — so that the three stories stand between two statements of the same doctrine. Chapter 13 returns to Suratha and the merchant.
Sanskrit patterns to watch
A first-person speech of promise, and so a chapter of futures: स्तोष्यते, नाशयिष्यामि (12.2), कीर्तयिष्यन्ति (12.3), श्रोष्यन्ति (12.4), भविष्यति (12.5, 12.13), सम्भविष्यति (12.6), विमोक्ष्यामि (12.9) and the epic future प्रतीच्छिष्यामि (12.11), mostly in relative–correlative sentences (यः … तस्य, 12.2; ये … तेषाम्, 12.3–5; या … सा, 12.21–22). Gerundives command the practice (पठितव्यम्, श्रोतव्यम्, with an instrumental agent, 12.7; उच्चार्यम्, श्राव्यम्, 12.10) and optatives prescribe it or state its power (शमयेत्, 12.8; शृणुयात्, 12.16; मुच्येत, 12.29). Passives (पठ्यते, 12.9; क्रियते, 12.12, 12.22; मोह्यते, 12.37), locatives of occasion (12.4, 12.10, 12.16), locative absolutes (सुचरिते श्रुते, 12.22; तस्मिन् श्रुते, 12.24; पतत्सु शस्त्रेषु, 12.28; शुम्भे निहते, 12.34–35) and ablatives in -तः (शत्रुतः, दस्युतः, राजतः, 12.6) are frequent. Taddhita abstracts (दारिद्र्यम्, 12.5; सांनिध्यम्, 12.9; आरोग्यम्, 12.22) and agent compounds (शान्तिकारकम्, 12.18; बलहानिकरम्, 12.19; सन्निधिकारकम्, 12.20) describe the text’s effects. The learner also meets epic forms (नन्दते, 12.15; the neuter दुःस्वप्नम्, 12.17; the nominative उत्पत्तयः after श्रुत्वा, 12.14), the irregular stems पुंस् (पुमान्, 12.14; पुंसाम्, 12.15, 12.24) and नृ (नृभिः, नृणाम्), and, in the closing narrative, the imperfect अन्तरधीयत (12.33), the perfects चक्रुः and आययुः (12.34–35) and the absolutive सम्भूय (12.36).
The teaching (as distinct from the narrative)
Literally, the chapter is a list of promises: recite, hear or remember this text, and sin, poverty, fear, disease, evil dreams and danger will leave you; then the Goddess vanishes, the gods return to their places, and Medhas tells the king what she is. The traditional uses of the chapter — the Navarātra pāṭha (12.4, 12.12), the recitation within every rite and the caṇḍī-homa (12.10), 12.13 recited as a sampuṭa — are the tradition’s, and the commentary presents them as such. As teaching, the chapter shifts the weight of worship from the rite to attention: she accepts what is offered in ignorance (12.11); one hearing equals a year of offerings (12.22); and in danger, where no rite can be performed, what saves is remembering (स्मरन्, 12.29). Medhas’s close joins this to the doctrine of chapter 1: the power that deludes the world is the power that gives knowledge when asked (12.37), and fortune and misfortune, creation and dissolution, are all hers (12.38–40). Reading the ‘enemies’ of 12.15 as inner passions is a modern reflection, not the verse’s literal sense.

Themes

  • The Devī’s own phalaśruti: reciting, hearing, remembering
  • One hearing outweighs a year of offerings
  • Remembrance in danger
  • The gods restored, the remnant in Pātāla
  • Eternal, yet arising again and again
  • Delusion and knowledge, Lakṣmī and Alakṣmī: one power

    vowel sandhi (yaṇ) · इको यणचि (6.1.77)
    Final i before a dissimilar vowel becomes y.

B. Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • असंशयम् asaṃśayam — avyayībhāva (in the sense of absence)
    Members: अ (नञ्) + संशय · Vigraha: संशयस्य अभावः saṃśayasya abhāvaḥ
    Literally “absence of doubt”; in the verse: without doubt, certainly
    Alternative: Also analysed as a nañ tatpuruṣa, न संशयः, whose accusative neuter is used as an adverb; the sense is the same.
  • सकलाम् sakalām — bahuvrīhi (saha-bahuvrīhi)
    Members: स (सह) + कला · Vigraha: कलाभिः सह वर्तते इति kalābhiḥ saha vartate iti
    Literally “together with (all its) parts”; in the verse: whole, entire — a lexicalised adjective

C. Morphology

  • स्तोष्यते stoṣyate — Simple future of √स्तु: guṇa of the root vowel (स्तो-) + स्य + ते. √स्तु takes both padas (स्तौति / स्तुते); the ātmanepada future is regular, and the verb is active: its object is माम्.
  • नाशयिष्यामि nāśayiṣyāmi — Future of the causative stem नाशय- (√नश् with vṛddhi + णिच्) + इष्य + मि: ‘I will cause to perish’.
  • समाहितः samāhitaḥ — Past participle of सम् + आ + √धा; धा is replaced by हि before क्त, giving -हित. ‘Put together’, hence ‘collected, concentrated’.

D. Anvaya — prose order

यः समाहितः (सन्) एभिः स्तवैः च मां नित्यं स्तोष्यते, तस्य सकलां बाधाम् अहम् असंशयं नाशयिष्यामि।

yaḥ samāhitaḥ (san) ebhiḥ stavaiḥ ca māṃ nityaṃ stoṣyate, tasya sakalāṃ bādhām aham asaṃśayaṃ nāśayiṣyāmi |

A relative–correlative sentence: the relative clause (यः … स्तोष्यते) describes the devotee; the main clause, with अहम् as subject, gives the promise.

6Translation

LiteralAnd whoever, with a collected mind, will constantly praise me with these hymns — every affliction of his I will destroy, without doubt.

ReadableWhoever praises me daily with these hymns, with a mind at rest in me, every trouble of his I will surely destroy.

‘With a mind at rest in me’ renders समाहितः, ‘collected, concentrated’; the object of the concentration is implied by the context.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningThe first promise of the phalaśruti. ‘These hymns’ (एभिः स्तवैः) are the praises the gods have just sung — above all the hymn of chapter 11 — and, by extension, the other hymns of the text (chapters 1, 4 and 5). The condition is twofold: regularity (नित्यम्) and attention (समाहितः).

Traditional interpretationIn the Śākta tradition this verse is cited to justify the daily recitation of the hymns of the Saptaśatī on their own, apart from the whole text, when time does not allow the full pāṭha.

Modern reflectionA modern reading: the promise is made not to the learned but to the attentive. What the verse asks for is steadiness of mind, not ritual elaboration.

    Members: मधुकैटभ + नाश · Vigraha: मधुकैटभयोः नाशः, तम् madhukaiṭabhayoḥ nāśaḥ, tam
    Literally “the destruction of Madhu and Kaiṭabha”; in the verse: the story of the first carita (chapter 1)
  • महिषासुरघातनम् mahiṣāsuraghātanam — ṣaṣṭhī tatpuruṣa (महिषासुर is a karmadhāraya: महिषश्चासौ असुरश्च)
    Members: महिषासुर + घातन · Vigraha: महिषासुरस्य घातनम् mahiṣāsurasya ghātanam
    Literally “the slaying of Mahiṣa the asura”; in the verse: the story of the middle carita (chapters 2–4)
  • शुम्भनिशुम्भयोः śumbhaniśumbhayoḥ — itaretara dvandva
    Members: शुम्भ + निशुम्भ · Vigraha: शुम्भश्च निशुम्भश्च śumbhaśca niśumbhaśca
    Literally “Śumbha and Niśumbha”; in the verse: the two brothers of the last carita (chapters 5–10); dual because two are named

C. Morphology

  • महिषासुरघातनम् mahiṣāsuraghātanam — घातन is an action noun (ल्युट्, -अन) from the causative of √हन्, whose causative stem is घातय-: ‘causing to be slain, slaughter’.
  • तद्वत् tadvat — The taddhita affix वति (-vat) makes an indeclinable of likeness: तद्वत् ‘like that, in the same way’.
  • कीर्तयिष्यन्ति kīrtayiṣyanti — Future of a curādi root: the present stem कीर्तय- is kept and इष्य is added. The future in a relative clause states the condition of the promise to come.

D. Anvaya — prose order

ये मधुकैटभनाशं च महिषासुरघातनं (च) तद्वत् शुम्भनिशुम्भयोः वधं (च) कीर्तयिष्यन्ति, …

A relative clause with three objects, one for each carita; its correlative (तेषाम्) comes in 12.5, after a second relative clause in 12.4.

6Translation

LiteralThose who will proclaim the destruction of Madhu and Kaiṭabha and the slaying of the asura Mahiṣa, and likewise the killing of Śumbha and Niśumbha —

ReadableThose who recite the destruction of Madhu and Kaiṭabha, the slaying of Mahiṣāsura, and likewise the killing of Śumbha and Niśumbha —

The sentence is completed in 12.5.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningThe Devī names the three stories of the text in order: Madhu and Kaiṭabha (chapter 1), Mahiṣa (chapters 2–4), Śumbha and Niśumbha (chapters 5–10). What is to be recited is the whole narrative, not the hymns alone.

Traditional interpretationThe verse is read as the Goddess’s own authority for the three-carita structure of the pāṭha. The Gita Press pāṭha assigns the three caritas to Mahākālī, Mahālakṣmī and Mahāsarasvatī as presiding forms; that is liturgical tradition, not stated here.

  • दारिद्र्यम् dāridryam — Abstract noun from दरिद्र ‘poor’ by the taddhita affix ष्यञ् (-य) with vṛddhi of the first syllable: ‘the state of being poor’.

ReadableFor them no sin will remain, nor the misfortunes that sin brings; they will know neither poverty nor parting from those they love.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningThe fruit promised to those of 12.3–4 is negative: four things will not befall them — sin (दुष्कृतम्), its consequences (दुष्कृतोत्थाः आपदः), poverty, and separation from the beloved. The order moves from the moral cause to its worldly results.

Traditional interpretationThe pairing of दुष्कृत with दुष्कृतोत्थाः आपदः is read in the light of the doctrine of karma: the recitation is said to remove not only present misfortunes but their root in past deeds.

    Members: शस्त्र + अनल + तोयौघ · Vigraha: शस्त्रं च अनलश्च तोयौघश्च, एतेषां समाहारः, तस्मात् śastraṃ ca analaśca toyaughaśca, eteṣāṃ samāhāraḥ, tasmāt
    Literally “from weapon, fire and flood of water”; in the verse: from any of the three dangers: weapons, fire, floods
    Alternative: Also readable as a ṣaṣṭhī tatpuruṣa over a dvandva, शस्त्रानलतोयानाम् ओघः, ‘an onrush of weapons, fire and water’; the singular ending fits either. The dvandva gives three distinct dangers, as the verse lists distinct sources of fear.

C. Morphology

  • शत्रुतः śatrutaḥ — The taddhita affix तसिल् (-तः) forms indeclinables with the sense of the ablative: शत्रुतः = शत्रोः ‘from an enemy’. Likewise दस्युतः and राजतः (राजन् loses its n before the affix).
  • सम्भविष्यति sambhaviṣyati — Future of सम् + √भू, ‘will come about, will arise’; one verb for every clause of the verse.

D. Anvaya — prose order

tasya śatrutaḥ bhayaṃ na (sambhaviṣyati), dasyutaḥ vā na, rājataḥ na, śastrānalatoyaughāt (ca bhayaṃ) kadācit na sambhaviṣyati |

One subject (भयम्), one verb (सम्भविष्यति, given at the end), and four negated sources of fear in the ablative, three of them indeclinables in -तः.

6Translation

LiteralFor him there will be no fear from enemies, or from robbers, nor from the king; nor will (fear) ever arise from weapons, fire or floods of water.

ReadableHe need never fear enemies, robbers or the king, nor weapons, fire or flood.

The future ‘there will be no fear’ is rendered as ‘he need never fear’; कदाचित् with न (‘never’) is taken with the whole verse.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningA second list of promises, of dangers from outside: enemies, robbers, the king’s anger, weapons, fire, water. The subject moves from the plural तेषाम् of 12.5 to the generic singular तस्य, ‘(any) such person’.

Comparative Śākta / ŚrīvidyāLalitā Sahasranāma (Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa) calls the Goddess भयापहा (name 121), ‘remover of fear’ — the title this verse spells out danger by danger.

Modern reflectionA modern reading: the list is the list of an ancient household’s real fears — war, theft, the arbitrary ruler, fire, flood. The promise answers the insecurity of ordinary life, not only of the soul.

D. Anvaya — prose order

सर्वत्र शान्तिकर्मणि तथा दुःस्वप्नदर्शने उग्रासु ग्रहपीडासु च मम माहात्म्यं शृणुयात्।

Three locatives of occasion and one prescriptive optative, with an unexpressed generic subject (‘one’).

6Translation

LiteralIn every rite of pacification, likewise on seeing a bad dream, and in severe afflictions from the planets, one should hear my Māhātmya.

ReadableAt every rite for averting harm, after an evil dream, and when the planets afflict severely, let my glory be heard.

The optative ‘one should hear’ is rendered as an impersonal ‘let … be heard’.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningThe Devī prescribes her Māhātmya for three occasions of śānti (averting harm): propitiatory rites in general, bad dreams, and planetary afflictions. The next verse gives the result for each.

Traditional interpretationOn this verse the tradition prescribes the Saptaśatī pāṭha as a remedy in the rites of pacification connected with dreams and planets, in place of or alongside other propitiatory rites.

Direct textual meaningThe recitation is said to break the power of evil-doers and, by the mere act of reading (पठनादेव), to destroy three classes of malevolent beings: rākṣasas, bhūtas and piśācas.

Direct textual meaningTwo kinds of telling are distinguished: the recital of the Devī’s births (जन्मनाम्) and the story of her battles. Her births are the appearances told in the three caritas and the future ones she has just foretold in 11.41–55 (Vindhyavāsinī, Raktadantikā, Śatākṣī, Śākambharī, Durgā, Bhīmā, Bhrāmarī).

Traditional interpretationThe tradition hears ‘births’ (जन्मन्) as manifestations: the Goddess is not born as beings are, which is why Medhas calls her eternal even as she ‘arises’ (1.64–66, and again in 12.36).

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningThe cosmic order is restored: each god holds his office again and receives his share of the sacrifice (यज्ञभाग), the right Śumbha had seized (5.2) and boasted of (5.108). The second line turns to the defeated: the Daityas, whose fate is told in 12.35.

Direct textual meaningMedhas gathers the Goddess’s powers in four clauses: she deludes the world, she brings it forth, she gives knowledge when asked, and prosperity when pleased. The same power that binds (मोह्यते) also releases (विज्ञानम्).

Traditional interpretationThe verse repeats Medhas’s first teaching in chapter 1: Mahāmāyā deludes the world (तया सम्मोह्यते जगत्, 1.55), by her the universe is emitted (तया विसृज्यते विश्वम्, 1.56), and when gracious she becomes the cause of liberation (1.57). The story has been told between the two statements.

Two participles of condition (स्तुता, सम्पूजिता) with instruments of worship, then the verb ददाति with four objects.

6Translation

LiteralPraised, and worshipped with flowers and likewise with incense, perfume and the rest, she gives wealth and sons, a mind (set) on dharma, and an auspicious course.

ReadablePraised and worshipped with flowers, incense, perfume and the like, she gives wealth and children, a mind set on dharma, and a blessed destiny.

पुत्रान्, ‘sons’, is rendered ‘children’; गतिं शुभाम्, literally ‘an auspicious going’, is rendered ‘a blessed destiny’ (the state one reaches, in this life or after it).

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningThe chapter of the fruits of recitation ends with a list of gifts that rises in order: wealth, children, a mind turned to dharma, and a good destiny. Praise (स्तुता) and worship (सम्पूजिता) are named together, as hymn and offering were in 12.20–24.

Traditional interpretationThe closing ॐ (bīja) printed after this mantra belongs to the Gita Press pāṭha, not to the Purāṇa. The next chapter repeats the promise in Medhas’s last counsel to Suratha: worshipped, she grants enjoyment, heaven and final release (13.5).

Modern reflectionA modern reading: the list does not stop at possessions. The last two gifts — a mind inclined to what is right, and a good end — are the ones no one can take away.

Chapter conclusion

Narrative summary

Before she leaves, the Goddess tells the gods what the story of her deeds will do for those who keep it. Whoever praises her with these hymns, recites the three stories or hears them on the sacred days is freed from sin, poverty, fear, pestilence, evil dreams and the malice of spirits; where it is recited she is present; one hearing pleases her as much as a year of costly worship; and anyone who remembers her story in fire, flood, prison, battle or pain is set free. Then she vanishes before their eyes. The gods take back their offices and their shares of the sacrifice, and the surviving Daityas withdraw to Pātāla. Medhas gives the king the meaning of everything he has heard: the eternal Goddess comes forth again and again to protect the world; she deludes it and gives it birth, ends it, creates and sustains it; she is fortune and misfortune; and, praised and worshipped, she gives wealth, children, a mind set on dharma and a good end.

  • Promises in the future within relative–correlative sentences: यः … स्तोष्यते, तस्य … नाशयिष्यामि (12.2); two relative clauses (ये … कीर्तयिष्यन्ति, 12.3; ये … श्रोष्यन्ति, 12.4) taken up by one correlative (तेषाम्, 12.5).

  • The optative of prescription and of capacity: शृणुयात् ‘one should hear’ (12.16), शमयेत् ‘would quell’ (12.8), मुच्येत ‘would be freed’ (12.29).
  • Locatives of occasion in series (बलिप्रदाने पूजायाम् अग्निकार्ये महोत्सवे, 12.10; शान्तिकर्मणि … दुःस्वप्नदर्शने … ग्रहपीडासु, 12.16) and locative absolutes (सकृत् सुचरिते श्रुते, 12.22; पतत्सु शस्त्रेषु, 12.28; शुम्भे निहते … निशुम्भे च, 12.34–35).

Śākta insights

  • The Goddess vouches for her own text: the phalaśruti is spoken in her voice, and its condition is attention and devotion — समाहितः, भक्त्या, एकचेतसः (12.2, 12.4, 12.7) — not learning.
  • Worship is accepted for love, not for correctness: she takes what is offered knowingly or unknowingly (12.11), and one hearing of her story gives her the delight of a year of offerings (12.20–22).
  • Remembrance is the last refuge: in fire, captivity, storm, battle and pain, where no rite is possible, the one who remembers her story is freed (12.25–30).
  • Manifestation is her way of protecting: eternal, she comes forth again and again (12.36), and withdraws when the work is done (12.33) — the pattern of all three stories.
  • One power binds and frees: she deludes the universe and gives knowledge when asked (12.37), returning to Medhas’s first teaching (1.55–57).
  • Nothing is outside her: dissolution, creation and preservation (12.39), Lakṣmī and Alakṣmī (12.40) are all her forms.

Śrīvidyā connections

The chapter shares names with the Lalitā Sahasranāma (Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa), noted on the verses: for the promised fruits पापनाशिनी (167) and सर्वापद्विनिवारिणी (913, 12.5), भयापहा (121, 12.6), स्वस्तिमती (448, 12.7) and सर्वव्याधिप्रशमनी (551, 12.8); for the Goddess herself चण्डिका (755) and भगवती (279, 12.32), नित्या (136, 12.36), मोहिनी (562) and वियदादिजगत्प्रसूः (550, 12.37), महाकाली (751, 12.38), and the triad सृष्टिकर्त्री (264), गोप्त्री (266), संहारिणी (268) with अजा (866, 12.39). These are shared Śākta names and ideas, not evidence that one text depends on the other. The chapter states no specifically Śrīvidyā doctrine, and its dhyāna in the Gita Press pāṭha is of Durgā, not of a Śrīvidyā form.
