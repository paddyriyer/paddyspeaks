शुम्भवधःThe Slaying of Śumbha

śumbhavadhaḥ

32 mantras · 10.1–10.32

❁

Overview

Seeing his brother Niśumbha, dear to him as life, lying dead and his army being cut down, Śumbha taunts the Goddess: let wicked Durgā not swell with pride in her power, for she fights leaning on the strength of others (10.1–3). She answers that she is alone in the world, and that there is no second beside her; the goddesses he sees are her own powers, and they are entering her as he watches (10.4–5). Brahmāṇī and the others dissolve into her body, and Ambikā stands alone (10.6). She tells him that she had stood there in many forms through her power, has withdrawn them, and now faces him alone: let him stand firm (10.7–8). The duel begins before the watching gods and asuras (10.9–11). Śumbha breaks her divine missiles with counter-weapons; she breaks his as if in play, with a fierce cry of ‘huṃ’ and the like (10.12–13). One by one she cuts away his bow, his spear, his sword and shield, and his mace; he has lost his horses and his charioteer, and comes on with his bare fist (10.14–19). His blow lands on her heart; the flat of her hand fells him, and he is up again at once (10.20–21). He seizes her and rises into the sky, where she fights without any support, and the two wrestle there, to the wonder of the siddhas and sages (10.22–23). She lifts him, whirls him round and hurls him to earth; he charges once more, fist raised, and she pierces his chest with her trident (10.24–26). He falls lifeless, and the whole earth shakes, with its oceans, continents and mountains (10.27). With him dead, the world grows clear and whole again: the sky clears, the portents vanish, the rivers keep their courses, the gods rejoice, the gandharvas sing, the apsarases dance, pure winds blow, the sun shines, the fires burn calm and the noise of the quarters is stilled (10.28–32).

About this chapter
Forms of the Devī
The Devī, through the narrative and the duel (10.6, 10.10, 10.14–16, 10.20, 10.26–27); Durgā, the name Śumbha uses in his taunt (10.3); Ambikā, ‘Mother’, as she stands alone and as she fights (10.6, 10.12, 10.18, 10.24); Caṇḍikā, ‘the fierce one’, in the close fight (10.17, 10.22–23, 10.25); and Parameśvarī, ‘the supreme sovereign’, as she breaks his missiles in play (10.13). The Mothers, ‘Brahmāṇī and the rest’ (10.6), appear only to disappear: she calls them her own vibhūtis, powers (10.5), and her many forms, assumed through her power (10.8), and draws them back into herself.
Significance of the events
The chapter is named for the second brother’s death (colophon: शुम्भवधः), and with it the battles of the Devī Māhātmya end. Its centre is not the duel but the exchange before it. Śumbha’s charge, that she fights by the strength of others (10.3), is answered by the claim ‘I alone am here in the world’ (10.5), and the narrative makes the claim visible: the Mothers who fought in chapters 8–9 dissolve into her body, and Ambikā stands alone (10.6). The duel that follows takes from Śumbha his weapons one at a time (10.14–19), then the ground under him (10.22–24), then his life (10.26–27), and the chapter ends not with the victory but with its effect on the world: the clearing of sky, rivers, winds, sun and fires (10.28–32). Chapter 11, the gods’ hymn to Nārāyaṇī, follows.
Sanskrit patterns to watch
A battle narrative in the perfect again: reduplicated forms (मुमुचे, बभञ्ज, चिच्छेद, जग्राह, निपपात, युयुधे, चिक्षेप, पपात), a dual with two subjects joined by च (चक्रतुः, 10.23), the plural perfects of the rejoicing (बभूवुः, जगुः, ननृतुः, ववुः, जज्वलुः, 10.30–32) and the periphrastic perfect of causatives (पातयामास, 10.20, 10.26; भ्रामयामास, 10.24). Imperfects (अब्रवीत्, आच्छादयत, अभ्यधावत्/अभ्यधावत, अताडयत्, अवादयन्) and the aorist अभूत् (10.11, 10.31) stand beside them. Absolutes set the turns: a genitive absolute (पश्यतां सर्वदेवानाम्, 10.10) and locative absolutes (छिन्ने धनुषि, 10.15; हते तस्मिन् दुरात्मनि, 10.28; पातिते, 10.29; निहते तस्मिन्, 10.30). The dialogue gives a prohibition with मा and the imperative (मा … आवह, 10.3), a rhetorical question (द्वितीया का ममापरा, 10.5), present participles as predicates (विशन्त्यः, 10.5) and a past participle with an object (यद् आस्थिता, 10.8). Prefixed absolutives in -य run through the fight (उपादाय, उद्यम्य, प्रगृह्य, उत्पत्य, उत्पात्य, प्राप्य), and bahuvrīhis strip Śumbha of what he had (हताश्वः, छिन्नधन्वा, विसारथिः, 10.18; गतासुः, 10.27).
The teaching, as distinct from the narrative
Literally, the last asura king is killed in single combat, and the world, freed of him, returns to order. As teaching, the Śākta tradition reads 10.5–8 as the doctrinal heart of the third carita: the many goddesses are one Goddess, her manifestations are her own power and not another’s, and what she has put forth she can take back (संहृतम्, 10.8, the verb of cosmic withdrawal). Śumbha’s error is to see many where there is one (10.3). The duel that strips him of every means of attack, and the restoration of nature that follows his death (10.28–32), are read as the outward side of that truth; reading the fires of 10.32 as the sacrificial fires is traditional interpretation, and the commentary offers it as such. The chapter’s own words are narrative; the non-dual reading is the tradition’s, and it is presented that way.
Carita
Uttama Carita (the third episode, chapters 5–13), of which this is the second and last of the two battles with the asura brothers. In the recitation tradition its presiding form is Mahāsarasvatī; this is the Gita Press viniyoga (printed before chapter 5), not a statement of the Purāṇa text. In the Gita Press pāṭha chapter 10 has no viniyoga of its own, only a dhyāna, of Kāmeśvarī: bright as heated gold, with the sun, moon and fire for her eyes, holding in her lovely arms the bow with arrows, the goad, the noose and the trident, the form of Śiva and Śakti (शिवशक्तिरूपां), wearing the crescent moon. The dhyāna is liturgy; the chapter itself never names Kāmeśvarī.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

Medhas resumes for King Suratha and the merchant Samādhi. Niśumbha has fallen at the end of chapter 9; this chapter tells the last duel, between the Goddess and Śumbha, and closes the third story (Uttama Carita, chapters 5–13).

Traditional interpretation

In the Gita Press pāṭha the chapter is preceded by its own dhyāna, which meditates on the Goddess as Kāmeśvarī, ‘the form of Śiva and Śakti’ (शिवशक्तिरूपां कामेश्वरीं). The dhyāna and the bīja ॐ that opens this mantra in recitation belong to the pāṭha, not to the Purāṇa.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

The link from chapter 9: Niśumbha is dead and the remaining army is being destroyed. Śumbha, now alone, addresses the Goddess directly.

Modern reflection

A modern reading: the text gives the demon a human grief — the brother was ‘equal to his life’. The anger that follows grows out of loss as well as pride.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

Śumbha’s taunt: the Goddess has not fought alone. The Mothers (Brahmāṇī and the others, 8.13 ff.) and Śivadūtī fought beside her in chapters 8–9, and Śumbha himself came to the field after they had fought (9.8). He calls this borrowed strength.

Traditional interpretation

The charge prepares the answer of 10.5: what Śumbha sees as ‘others’ is the Goddess herself. The tradition reads the taunt as the demon’s error in its purest form — he sees many where there is one.

Modern reflection

A modern reading: Śumbha’s accusation is the one we make of anyone whose strength we cannot explain — that it must come from somewhere else.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

The Goddess answers the charge of 10.3. The ‘others’ (अन्यासाम्) are not others: they are her own vibhūtis, and as she speaks they merge back into her (10.6). Śumbha has been fighting her all along.

Traditional interpretation

In the Śākta tradition this verse is a central statement of the text’s non-dualism: the many goddesses are one Goddess, and the world holds no second power beside her. The narrative first called them the śaktis of the gods, come out of the gods’ bodies (8.13); here the Goddess claims them as her own, and the Kaumārī of 8.17 is already called ‘Ambikā in the form of Guha’ (अम्बिका गुहरूपिणी).

Modern reflection

A modern reading: there is an echo of Śumbha’s own message in 5.111, which claimed that every jewel among gods, gandharvas and serpents rests ‘in me alone’ (मय्येव). The Goddess uses the same words truthfully: what seemed to be many is gathered into one.

Comparative Śrīvidyā notes

Comparative Śākta / Śrīvidyā

Lalitā Sahasranāma (Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa) has names that state the same idea: एकाकिनी (665, ‘the one who is alone’), निर्द्वैता (667) and द्वैतवर्जिता (668, ‘free of duality’). The hymns are separate works; the shared doctrine is common Śākta thought.

    आ + √स्था ‘to take one’s stand on, assume’ is transitive; its past participle can be active in sense and take an object (यत्): ‘(I) who had assumed’.

  • संहृतम् saṃhṛtam

    सम् + √हृ ‘to draw together, withdraw’ — the same verb that names the dissolution of the world (saṃhāra). मया is its agent.

  • तिष्ठामि tiṣṭhāmi

    √स्था forms its present stem by reduplication (ष्ठा → तिष्ठ-), listed with the bhvādi class.

Anvaya · prose order

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

The Goddess explains what Śumbha has seen: the many forms were her own power (विभूति) displayed; she has withdrawn them, and now meets him alone. स्थिरो भव is a challenge: ‘hold your ground’.

Traditional interpretation

The verb संहृतम् belongs to the vocabulary of cosmic dissolution (saṃhāra), and in the Śākta tradition the verse is read in that light: what the Goddess projects she can take back, and the manifold is her own, not another’s. The word विभूति is the one the Bhagavad Gītā uses for the divine manifestations in its tenth chapter.

Modern reflection

A modern reading: the Goddess answers the charge of dependence not by argument but by demonstration — she withdraws every helper and stays.

Lalitā Sahasranāma (Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa) has the same word as a name, निराधारा (132), where it means ‘she who depends on nothing’ — a metaphysical attribute. Here the word is literal: she fights in mid-air. The two senses meet, but the texts are separate.

Morphology & anvaya

  • चक्रतुः cakratuḥ

    Perfect of √कृ, third person dual: reduplicated च-क्र-अतुः. The dual verb takes the two subjects joined by च (दैत्यः चण्डिका च).

  • प्रथमम् prathamam

    Taken as an adjective of नियुद्धम्: ‘a first (fight)’, i.e. one never seen before. It could also be adverbial, ‘first’, but no second fight follows, so the first sense fits.

Anvaya · prose order

तदा दैत्यः चण्डिका च खे परस्परं प्रथमं सिद्धमुनिविस्मयकारकं नियुद्धं चक्रतुः।

tadā daityaḥ caṇḍikā ca khe parasparaṃ prathamaṃ siddhamunivismayakārakaṃ niyuddhaṃ cakratuḥ |

Two subjects joined by च with a dual verb (चक्रतुः); the object नियुद्धम् carries two qualifiers (प्रथमम्, सिद्धमुनिविस्मयकारकम्).

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

The duel becomes a wrestling match (नियुद्ध, close combat without weapons) in the open sky, watched by the siddhas and sages.

Modern reflection

A modern reading: at the end, all instruments are gone; the struggle is body to body. The text leaves nothing between the demon and the Goddess.

Morphology & anvaya

  • उत्पात्य utpātya

    Absolutive of the causative of उद् + √पत्: the causative suffix (-अय-) is dropped before -य: उद् + पात् + य. ‘Having made (him) fly up’, i.e. lifting him bodily.

  • भ्रामयामास bhrāmayāmāsa

    Periphrastic perfect of the causative of √भ्रम् ‘to whirl’: भ्रामय + आम् + आस. ‘Made (him) whirl’, i.e. swung him round.

  • चिक्षेप cikṣepa

    Perfect of √क्षिप्: reduplicated चि-क्षेप (the guttural of the reduplicated syllable becomes a palatal).

Anvaya · prose order

tataḥ ambikā tena saha suciraṃ niyuddhaṃ kṛtvā (tam) utpātya bhrāmayāmāsa, (ca) dharaṇītale cikṣepa |

An absolutive phrase (नियुद्धं … कृत्वा तेन सह), a second absolutive (उत्पात्य), and two finite verbs (भ्रामयामास, चिक्षेप) sharing the understood object ‘him’.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

The Goddess ends the sky-fight: three verbs in sequence — lifting, whirling, hurling — bring Śumbha back to earth. The object (him) is understood throughout.

Modern reflection

A modern reading: Śumbha had carried the Goddess into the sky (10.22); now she carries him and throws him down. The verse reverses his action exactly.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

Modern reflection

A modern reading: the narrative gives the demon the same gesture three times — raising his fist. Persistence without insight is the shape of his whole story.

    Present participle of आ + √या (stem आयात्, strong stem आयान्त्), accusative singular: ‘(him) coming on’.

  • भित्त्वा bhittvā

    Absolutive of √भिद् ‘to split, pierce’: the root’s d becomes t before -त्वा (खरि च, 8.4.55).

    The same causative periphrastic perfect as in 10.20, here with the Goddess as subject: ‘made (him) fall’.

Subject देवी, verb पातयामास, object तम् (with आयान्तम् and the epithet), the absolutive phrase शूलेन वक्षसि भित्त्वा, and the locative जगत्याम्.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

The death blow. After the weapons, the sky-fight and the wrestling, the Goddess ends the battle with her trident (शूल) through his chest, as she pierced Niśumbha’s heart with her trident (9.34) before beheading the figure that came out of it (9.35–36).

Modern reflection

A modern reading: the epithet given at the moment of death — ‘lord of all the Daitya people’ — states what falls with him. The king is the last of his army; with him the whole order of the asuras ends.

Reflecting on the chapter

Śumbha, the last of the asura brothers, sees Niśumbha dead and accuses the Goddess of fighting on the strength of others. She answers that there is no one else: the goddesses who fought beside her are her own powers, and as she speaks they enter her body, and Ambikā stands alone. In single combat she breaks his missiles as if in play, cuts away his bow, spear, sword, shield and mace, fells him with the flat of her hand, fights him in the sky with nothing to stand on, throws him down and pierces him with her trident. He falls and the earth shakes; then the sky, the rivers, the winds, the sun and the fires return to their nature, and the gods rejoice. The battles of the text are over; chapter 11 is the gods’ hymn of thanks.

  • विभूतिःvibhūtiḥmanifested power; the goddesses as her own powers (10.5, 10.8)
  • लयःlayaḥdissolution, merging back into the source (10.6)
  • संहृतम्saṃhṛtamdrawn together, withdrawn: the verb of cosmic dissolution (10.8)
  • अतिमानिनीatimāninīexceedingly proud: Śumbha’s charge (10.3)
  • हुङ्कारःhuṅkāraḥthe cry ‘huṃ’, the Goddess’s sound used as a weapon (10.13)
  • लीलाlīlāplay, sport: action without effort (10.13)
  • परमेश्वरीparameśvarīthe supreme sovereign (10.13)
  • निराधाराnirādhārāwithout support; here, fighting in mid-air (10.22)
  • नियुद्धम्niyuddhamclose combat, wrestling without weapons (10.23–24)
  • शूलम्śūlamtrident or spear: the weapon of the death blow (10.26–27)
  • स्वास्थ्यम्svāsthyamhealth, soundness; lit. ‘being established in oneself’ (10.28)
  • उत्पातःutpātaḥan ominous portent (उत्पातमेघाः, 10.29)

Important grammatical constructions

  • The prohibitive मा with the imperative, the Epic and Purāṇic idiom (मा … गर्वम् आवह, 10.3).
  • A rhetorical question as a denial (द्वितीया का मम अपरा, 10.5), and a present participle as predicate after पश्य (पश्य एताः … विशन्त्यः, 10.5).
  • The genitive absolute of spectators (पश्यतां सर्वदेवानाम् असुराणां च, 10.10) and locative absolutes for each turn and for the aftermath (छिन्ने धनुषि, 10.15; हते तस्मिन् दुरात्मनि, 10.28; पातिते, 10.29).
  • The perfect dual with two subjects joined by च (दैत्यः चण्डिका च … चक्रतुः, 10.23).
  • The periphrastic perfect of causative stems (पातयामास, 10.20, 10.26; भ्रामयामास, 10.24) beside a string of absolutives (उत्पात्य भ्रामयामास चिक्षेप, 10.24).
  • Bahuvrīhis of loss (हताश्वः, छिन्नधन्वा, विसारथिः, 10.18; गतासुः, 10.27) and with स- for सह (साब्धिद्वीपां सपर्वताम्, 10.27; सोल्काः, 10.29).
  • Agent nouns in compounds that govern their object (तत्प्रतीघातकर्तृभिः, 10.12; सिद्धमुनिविस्मयकारकम्, 10.23).
  • One participle in two functions: शान्ताः qualifying the fires and then predicating the stillness of the sounds (10.32).

Śākta insights

  • The Goddess is one without a second: the powers that fought beside her are not others but her own vibhūtis, and they return into her (10.5–6).
  • Manifestation and withdrawal are both her act: what she assumed through her power in many forms she has drawn back (संहृतम्), and she stands alone (10.8).
  • The demon’s error is to see many where there is one; his taunt of borrowed strength (10.3) is answered not by argument but by the visible dissolution of the Mothers into her (10.6).
  • Her sovereignty acts as play: as Parameśvarī she breaks his divine missiles with a cry of ‘huṃ’, as if in sport (10.13), and fells him with the flat of her hand (10.20–21).
  • She depends on nothing: when Śumbha carries the fight into the sky she fights there without support (निराधारा, 10.22).
  • Her victory heals the world itself, not only the gods: sky, rivers, winds, sun and fires return to their nature when the asura falls (10.28–32).

Śrīvidyā connections

The chapter shares names and ideas with the Lalitā Sahasranāma (Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa). The Goddess’s claim to be alone (10.5) is stated there as names: एकाकिनी (665), निर्द्वैता (667) and द्वैतवर्जिता (668). The dissolution of the Mothers into her (10.6) recalls लयकरी (739); her breaking his missiles in play (10.13) recalls परमेश्वरी (396) and लीलाविनोदिनी (966); and निराधारा (132), a metaphysical name there, is a literal word here (10.22). The duel of weapon and counter-weapon (10.12) has its parallel in Lalitā’s war with Bhaṇḍāsura (79, भण्डासुरेन्द्रनिर्मुक्तशस्त्रप्रत्यस्त्रवर्षिणी), but the stories are different. The one specifically Śrīvidyā element is liturgical, not textual: the Gita Press dhyāna before this chapter meditates on Kāmeśvarī, the form of Śiva and Śakti (compare शिवशक्त्यैक्यरूपिणी, 999), holding the bow and arrows, the goad and the noose — the four weapons the Lalitā Sahasranāma gives Lalitā (8–11, रागस्वरूपपाशाढ्या, क्रोधाकाराङ्कुशोज्ज्वला, मनोरूपेक्षुकोदण्डा, पञ्चतन्मात्रसायका) — with a trident added. The chapter itself states no Śrīvidyā doctrine; these are shared Śākta names and images, not evidence that one text depends on the other.

Grammatical observations
  • युद्ध्यसे (10.3) is the ordinary युध्यसे of √युध् (divādi, ātmanepada); the doubled dh is the Gita Press orthography.
  • एताः (10.5) is nominative, agreeing with विशन्त्यः and मद्विभूतयः; after पश्य the clause is a direct exclamation.
  • यत् in 10.8 is taken as the relative object of आस्थिता with तत् as correlative; an adverbial यत् = यदा gives the same sense.
  • √धाव् appears in both padas: अभ्यधावत् (10.16, 10.19) and अभ्यधावत (10.25).
  • In the Gita Press text Śumbha’s loss of horses and charioteer is told only by the compounds of 10.18; the line found at 10.17 in other witnesses narrates it.
  • प्रथमम् (10.23) is read as an adjective of नियुद्धम्, ‘a fight never seen before’; ललितम् (10.30) is an adverb, ‘sweetly’.
  • The first शान्ताः of 10.32 is read as ‘(burning) calm’; ‘(the fires) that had been put out’ is the alternative.
