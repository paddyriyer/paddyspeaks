Uttama Carita · Chapter 10 of 13 · 32 mantras (10.1–10.32)

शुम्भवधः

śumbhavadhaḥ

The Slaying of Śumbha · Complete with Noted Variants

Introduction

Summary
Seeing his brother Niśumbha, dear to him as life, lying dead and his army being cut down, Śumbha taunts the Goddess: let wicked Durgā not swell with pride in her power, for she fights leaning on the strength of others (10.1–3). She answers that she is alone in the world, and that there is no second beside her; the goddesses he sees are her own powers, and they are entering her as he watches (10.4–5). Brahmāṇī and the others dissolve into her body, and Ambikā stands alone (10.6). She tells him that she had stood there in many forms through her power, has withdrawn them, and now faces him alone: let him stand firm (10.7–8). The duel begins before the watching gods and asuras (10.9–11). Śumbha breaks her divine missiles with counter-weapons; she breaks his as if in play, with a fierce cry of ‘huṃ’ and the like (10.12–13). One by one she cuts away his bow, his spear, his sword and shield, and his mace; he has lost his horses and his charioteer, and comes on with his bare fist (10.14–19). His blow lands on her heart; the flat of her hand fells him, and he is up again at once (10.20–21). He seizes her and rises into the sky, where she fights without any support, and the two wrestle there, to the wonder of the siddhas and sages (10.22–23). She lifts him, whirls him round and hurls him to earth; he charges once more, fist raised, and she pierces his chest with her trident (10.24–26). He falls lifeless, and the whole earth shakes, with its oceans, continents and mountains (10.27). With him dead, the world grows clear and whole again: the sky clears, the portents vanish, the rivers keep their courses, the gods rejoice, the gandharvas sing, the apsarases dance, pure winds blow, the sun shines, the fires burn calm and the noise of the quarters is stilled (10.28–32).
Forms of the Devī
The Devī, through the narrative and the duel (10.6, 10.10, 10.14–16, 10.20, 10.26–27); Durgā, the name Śumbha uses in his taunt (10.3); Ambikā, ‘Mother’, as she stands alone and as she fights (10.6, 10.12, 10.18, 10.24); Caṇḍikā, ‘the fierce one’, in the close fight (10.17, 10.22–23, 10.25); and Parameśvarī, ‘the supreme sovereign’, as she breaks his missiles in play (10.13). The Mothers, ‘Brahmāṇī and the rest’ (10.6), appear only to disappear: she calls them her own vibhūtis, powers (10.5), and her many forms, assumed through her power (10.8), and draws them back into herself.
Significance of the events
The chapter is named for the second brother’s death (colophon: शुम्भवधः), and with it the battles of the Devī Māhātmya end. Its centre is not the duel but the exchange before it. Śumbha’s charge, that she fights by the strength of others (10.3), is answered by the claim ‘I alone am here in the world’ (10.5), and the narrative makes the claim visible: the Mothers who fought in chapters 8–9 dissolve into her body, and Ambikā stands alone (10.6). The duel that follows takes from Śumbha his weapons one at a time (10.14–19), then the ground under him (10.22–24), then his life (10.26–27), and the chapter ends not with the victory but with its effect on the world: the clearing of sky, rivers, winds, sun and fires (10.28–32). Chapter 11, the gods’ hymn to Nārāyaṇī, follows.
Sanskrit patterns to watch
A battle narrative in the perfect again: reduplicated forms (मुमुचे, बभञ्ज, चिच्छेद, जग्राह, निपपात, युयुधे, चिक्षेप, पपात), a dual with two subjects joined by च (चक्रतुः, 10.23), the plural perfects of the rejoicing (बभूवुः, जगुः, ननृतुः, ववुः, जज्वलुः, 10.30–32) and the periphrastic perfect of causatives (पातयामास, 10.20, 10.26; भ्रामयामास, 10.24). Imperfects (अब्रवीत्, आच्छादयत, अभ्यधावत्/अभ्यधावत, अताडयत्, अवादयन्) and the aorist अभूत् (10.11, 10.31) stand beside them. Absolutes set the turns: a genitive absolute (पश्यतां सर्वदेवानाम्, 10.10) and locative absolutes (छिन्ने धनुषि, 10.15; हते तस्मिन् दुरात्मनि, 10.28; पातिते, 10.29; निहते तस्मिन्, 10.30). The dialogue gives a prohibition with मा and the imperative (मा … आवह, 10.3), a rhetorical question (द्वितीया का ममापरा, 10.5), present participles as predicates (विशन्त्यः, 10.5) and a past participle with an object (यद् आस्थिता, 10.8). Prefixed absolutives in -य run through the fight (उपादाय, उद्यम्य, प्रगृह्य, उत्पत्य, उत्पात्य, प्राप्य), and bahuvrīhis strip Śumbha of what he had (हताश्वः, छिन्नधन्वा, विसारथिः, 10.18; गतासुः, 10.27).
The teaching (as distinct from the narrative)
Literally, the last asura king is killed in single combat, and the world, freed of him, returns to order. As teaching, the Śākta tradition reads 10.5–8 as the doctrinal heart of the third carita: the many goddesses are one Goddess, her manifestations are her own power and not another’s, and what she has put forth she can take back (संहृतम्, 10.8, the verb of cosmic withdrawal). Śumbha’s error is to see many where there is one (10.3). The duel that strips him of every means of attack, and the restoration of nature that follows his death (10.28–32), are read as the outward side of that truth; reading the fires of 10.32 as the sacrificial fires is traditional interpretation, and the commentary offers it as such. The chapter’s own words are narrative; the non-dual reading is the tradition’s, and it is presented that way.

Themes

  • ‘I alone am here’: the many goddesses as one
  • Vibhūti: manifestation and withdrawal
  • The charge of borrowed strength, answered
  • The duel that strips the asura of everything
  • Lordship as play: the hum-sound and the open hand
  • The world restored to health

6Translation

LiteralO wicked Durgā, do not carry pride out of the arrogance of strength. You who are exceedingly proud fight relying on the strength of other (women).

ReadableWicked Durgā, do not swell with pride in your power! For all your arrogance, you fight leaning on the strength of others.

या अतिमानिनी, literally ‘(you) who (are) exceedingly proud’, is rendered ‘for all your arrogance’.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningŚumbha’s taunt: the Goddess has not fought alone. The Mothers (Brahmāṇī and the others, 8.13 ff.) and Śivadūtī fought beside her in chapters 8–9, and Śumbha himself came to the field after they had fought (9.8). He calls this borrowed strength.

Traditional interpretationThe charge prepares the answer of 10.5: what Śumbha sees as ‘others’ is the Goddess herself. The tradition reads the taunt as the demon’s error in its purest form — he sees many where there is one.

Modern reflectionA modern reading: Śumbha’s accusation is the one we make of anyone whose strength we cannot explain — that it must come from somewhere else.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningThe Goddess answers the charge of 10.3. The ‘others’ (अन्यासाम्) are not others: they are her own vibhūtis, and as she speaks they merge back into her (10.6). Śumbha has been fighting her all along.

Traditional interpretationIn the Śākta tradition this verse is a central statement of the text’s non-dualism: the many goddesses are one Goddess, and the world holds no second power beside her. The narrative first called them the śaktis of the gods, come out of the gods’ bodies (8.13); here the Goddess claims them as her own, and the Kaumārī of 8.17 is already called ‘Ambikā in the form of Guha’ (अम्बिका गुहरूपिणी).

Modern reflectionA modern reading: there is an echo of Śumbha’s own message in 5.111, which claimed that every jewel among gods, gandharvas and serpents rests ‘in me alone’ (मय्येव). The Goddess uses the same words truthfully: what seemed to be many is gathered into one.

Direct textual meaningThe Goddess explains what Śumbha has seen: the many forms were her own power (विभूति) displayed; she has withdrawn them, and now meets him alone. स्थिरो भव is a challenge: ‘hold your ground’.

Traditional interpretationThe verb संहृतम् belongs to the vocabulary of cosmic dissolution (saṃhāra), and in the Śākta tradition the verse is read in that light: what the Goddess projects she can take back, and the manifold is her own, not another’s.   • उपादाय upādāya — Absolutive of उप + आ + √दा ‘to take up’: with a prefix the absolutive ends in -य (ल्यप्), not -त्वा.

6Translation

LiteralThen, taking up a sword and a shining shield marked with a hundred moons, the lord of the Daityas’ chiefs then rushed at the Goddess.

ReadableThen the overlord of the Daityas took up a sword and a gleaming shield adorned with a hundred moons, and charged at the Goddess.

Direct textual meaningWith bow and spear gone, Śumbha closes in with sword and shield. शतचन्द्र, ‘hundred-moon’, is the Epic name for a shield decorated with moon-shaped ornaments; भानुमत् ‘shining’ describes it.

    Literally “maker of the wonder of siddhas and sages”; in the verse: a fight that astonished the siddhas and the sages
    Alternative: सिद्धमुनि may also be a karmadhāraya, ‘perfected sages’ (सिद्धाः मुनयः); the dvandva, two classes of onlookers, fits the Epic idiom better.

C. Morphology

  • चक्रतुः cakratuḥ — Perfect of √कृ, third person dual: reduplicated च-क्र-अतुः. The dual verb takes the two subjects joined by च (दैत्यः चण्डिका च).
  • प्रथमम् prathamam — Taken as an adjective of नियुद्धम्: ‘a first (fight)’, i.e. one never seen before. It could also be adverbial, ‘first’, but no second fight follows, so the first sense fits.

D. Anvaya — prose order

तदा दैत्यः चण्डिका च खे परस्परं प्रथमं सिद्धमुनिविस्मयकारकं नियुद्धं चक्रतुः।

tadā daityaḥ caṇḍikā ca khe parasparaṃ prathamaṃ siddhamunivismayakārakaṃ niyuddhaṃ cakratuḥ |

Two subjects joined by च with a dual verb (चक्रतुः); the object नियुद्धम् carries two qualifiers (प्रथमम्, सिद्धमुनिविस्मयकारकम्).

6Translation

LiteralThen the Daitya and Caṇḍikā waged with each other in the sky a close fight, the first (of its kind), causing wonder to the siddhas and sages.

ReadableThere in the sky the Daitya and Caṇḍikā grappled with each other, a fight such as had never been seen, which filled the siddhas and the sages with wonder.

प्रथमम्, ‘first (of its kind)’, is rendered ‘such as had never been seen’.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningThe duel becomes a wrestling match (नियुद्ध, close combat without weapons) in the open sky, watched by the siddhas and sages.

Modern reflectionA modern reading: at the end, all instruments are gone; the struggle is body to body. The text leaves nothing between the demon and the Goddess.

6Translation

ReadablePierced by the point of the Goddess’s trident, he fell lifeless to the ground, and the whole earth shook — its oceans, its continents and its mountains.

Traditional interpretationThe fires are traditionally understood as the sacrificial fires. In recitation the chapter ends with the bīja ॐ, which belongs to the pāṭha, not to the Purāṇa.

Chapter conclusion

Narrative summary

Śumbha, the last of the asura brothers, sees Niśumbha dead and accuses the Goddess of fighting on the strength of others. She answers that there is no one else: the goddesses who fought beside her are her own powers, and as she speaks they enter her body, and Ambikā stands alone. In single combat she breaks his missiles as if in play, cuts away his bow, spear, sword, shield and mace, fells him with the flat of her hand, fights him in the sky with nothing to stand on, throws him down and pierces him with her trident. He falls and the earth shakes; then the sky, the rivers, the winds, the sun and the fires return to their nature, and the gods rejoice. The battles of the text are over; chapter 11 is the gods’ hymn of thanks.

Śākta insights

  • The Goddess is one without a second: the powers that fought beside her are not others but her own vibhūtis, and they return into her (10.5–6).
  • Manifestation and withdrawal are both her act: what she assumed through her power in many forms she has drawn back (संहृतम्), and she stands alone (10.8).
  • The demon’s error is to see many where there is one; his taunt of borrowed strength (10.3) is answered not by argument but by the visible dissolution of the Mothers into her (10.6).
  • Her sovereignty acts as play: as Parameśvarī she breaks his divine missiles with a cry of ‘huṃ’, as if in sport (10.13), and fells him with the flat of her hand (10.20–21).
  • She depends on nothing: when Śumbha carries the fight into the sky she fights there without support (निराधारा, 10.22).
  • Her victory heals the world itself, not only the gods: sky, rivers, winds, sun and fires return to their nature when the asura falls (10.28–32).

Śrīvidyā connections

The chapter shares names and ideas with the Lalitā Sahasranāma (Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa). The Goddess’s claim to be alone (10.5) is stated there as names: एकाकिनी (665), निर्द्वैता (667) and द्वैतवर्जिता (668). The dissolution of the Mothers into her (10.6) recalls लयकरी (739); her breaking his missiles in play (10.13) recalls परमेश्वरी (396) and लीलाविनोदिनी (966); and निराधारा (132), a metaphysical name there, is a literal word here (10.22). The duel of weapon and counter-weapon (10.12) has its parallel in Lalitā’s war with Bhaṇḍāsura (79, भण्डासुरेन्द्रनिर्मुक्तशस्त्रप्रत्यस्त्रवर्षिणी), but the stories are different. The one specifically Śrīvidyā element is liturgical, not textual: the Gita Press dhyāna before this chapter meditates on Kāmeśvarī, the form of Śiva and Śakti (compare शिवशक्त्यैक्यरूपिणी, 999), holding the bow and arrows, the goad and the noose — the four weapons the Lalitā Sahasranāma gives Lalitā (8–11, रागस्वरूपपाशाढ्या, क्रोधाकाराङ्कुशोज्ज्वला, मनोरूपेक्षुकोदण्डा, पञ्चतन्मात्रसायका) — with a trident added. The chapter itself states no Śrīvidyā doctrine; these are shared Śākta names and images, not evidence that one text depends on the other.

  • 10.6 — Brahmāṇī and the other goddesses dissolve into her body, and Ambikā stands alone.
  • 10.8 — The many forms she had assumed through her power she has withdrawn; now she stands alone in battle — let him stand firm.
  • 10.13 — As Parameśvarī she breaks his divine missiles as if in play, with a fierce cry of ‘huṃ’ and the like.
  • 10.27 — Pierced by the point of her trident, Śumbha falls lifeless, and the whole earth shakes with its oceans, continents and mountains.
  • 10.28 — With the wicked one slain, the whole world grows serene and regains its health, and the sky becomes clear.

Grammatical observations
  • युद्ध्यसे (10.3) is the ordinary युध्यसे of √युध् (divādi, ātmanepada); the doubled dh is the Gita Press orthography.
  • एताः (10.5) is nominative, agreeing with विशन्त्यः and मद्विभूतयः; after पश्य the clause is a direct exclamation.
  • यत् in 10.8 is taken as the relative object of आस्थिता with तत् as correlative; an adverbial यत् = यदा gives the same sense.
  • √धाव् appears in both padas: अभ्यधावत् (10.16, 10.19) and अभ्यधावत (10.25).
  • In the Gita Press text Śumbha’s loss of horses and charioteer is told only by the compounds of 10.18; the line found at 10.17 in other witnesses narrates it.
  • प्रथमम् (10.23) is read as an adjective of नियुद्धम्, ‘a fight never seen before’; ललितम् (10.30) is an adverb, ‘sweetly’.
  • The first शान्ताः of 10.32 is read as ‘(burning) calm’; ‘(the fires) that had been put out’ is the alternative.
