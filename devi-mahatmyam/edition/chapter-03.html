Madhyama Carita · Chapter 3 of 13 · 44 mantras (3.1–3.44)

महिषासुरवधः

mahiṣāsuravadhaḥ

The Slaying of Mahiṣāsura · Complete with Noted Variants

Introduction

Summary
Medhas goes on with the story (3.1). Seeing the army cut down, Mahiṣa’s general Cikṣura goes out to fight Ambikā and rains arrows on her like a cloud on Meru (3.2–3). She cuts his arrows to pieces as if at play, kills his horses and charioteer, breaks his bow and standard and pierces him (3.4–5); on foot with sword and shield he strikes the lion on the head and the Goddess on her arm, and the sword shatters (3.6–8). His flaming spear, hurled at Bhadrakālī, is broken by hers, and he dies with it (3.9–10). Cāmara comes on an elephant: his lance falls to her ‘hum’, his spear to her arrows (3.11–13), and the lion leaps onto the elephant, crashes to the ground with him and strikes off his head (3.14–16). The remaining generals fall in a catalogue — Udagra, Karāla, Uddhata, Bāṣkala, Tāmra, Andhaka, Ugrāsya, Ugravīrya, Mahāhanu, Biḍāla, Durdhara, Durmukha — each with the weapon that kills him (3.17–20). Mahiṣa himself, in his own buffalo shape, tramples the Goddess’s hosts with muzzle, hooves, tail, horns, speed, bellowing and breath (3.21–23), charges her lion (3.24), and in his rage breaks the earth, floods the ocean, tears the clouds and sends mountains crashing from the sky (3.25–27). Caṇḍikā turns her wrath to his death (3.28): she binds him with her noose; he sheds the buffalo and becomes a lion, then a man with a sword, then an elephant, each met by her weapons, and then the buffalo again, shaking the three worlds (3.29–33). She drinks and laughs, he flings mountains, and grinding them to dust she speaks (3.34–36): ‘Roar, fool, for a moment, while I drink; when I have slain you the gods will roar’ (3.37–38). She leaps on him, presses his neck with her foot and strikes him with her spear (3.39–40); caught half out of the buffalo’s mouth, still fighting, he is beheaded with her great sword (3.41–42). The asura army perishes, the gods rejoice and praise her, the Gandharvas sing and the Apsarases dance (3.43–44).
Forms of the Devī
Ambikā, ‘the Mother’, the narrator’s commonest name for her (3.2, 3.12, 3.24, 3.30); Bhadrakālī, ‘the auspicious Kālī’ (3.9); Trinetrā, the three-eyed, and Parameśvarī, the Supreme Sovereign (3.19); Mahādevī (3.24); Caṇḍikā, ‘the fierce one’, from the turning point onward (3.28, 3.34, 3.35); and Jaganmātā, Mother of the world, in the moment of her fiercest act (3.34). Her final act — foot on the buffalo’s neck, spear and sword (3.40–42) — is the image painted and sculpted as Mahiṣāsuramardinī, a name the chapter itself does not use. Her lion and her hosts (gaṇas) fight as hers throughout (3.14–16, 3.21–24).
Significance of the events
This is the battle the middle carita was building to: the Goddess made from the gods’ combined radiance in chapter 2 now does alone what the gods could not. The chapter moves outward in scale — a chariot duel, a wrestling match, a catalogue of deaths, then a buffalo whose rage reaches earth, ocean and sky (3.25–27) — and then inward to a single image: the asura caught halfway out of his own body (3.41). The gods take no part in the fighting; their part is joy and praise (3.43–44), which becomes the hymn of chapter 4 (4.2).
Sanskrit patterns to watch
A long run of reduplicated perfects as the narrative tense (ववर्ष, जघान, चिच्छेद, विव्याध, पफाल, जग्राह, चिक्षेप); periphrastic perfects of causatives (पातयामास, त्रासयामास, प्लावयामास, क्षोभयामास); passive narration with the agent in the instrumental (मृगारिणा … पृथक्कृतम्, 3.16; तया … देव्या … निपातितः, 3.42); locative absolutes (3.11, 3.21, 3.38); यावत् … तावत् with the present for an act about to happen (3.30); duals for the lion and Cāmara (3.15); a verse without a verb that waits for the next (3.22–23); and, in the Goddess’s one speech, the imperative and the future of the same root (गर्ज … गर्जिष्यन्ति, 3.38). Two verbs show the Purāṇic ātmanepada for the active (अमुञ्चत, 3.10; निरकृन्तत, 3.32).
The teaching (as distinct from the narrative)
Literally, the chapter is a battle: generals die one by one, a buffalo-asura changes shape and is killed, and the gods celebrate. As teaching, the Śākta tradition reads the Goddess’s ease as the point — she cuts his arrows ‘as if at play’ (3.4), a sword breaks on her arm (3.8), a lance falls to a sound (3.12), and she drinks and laughs before the kill (3.34), so that the outcome is never in doubt (3.38). Modern readers often add a reflection of their own: an adversary that escapes by changing its shape (3.29–33) is overcome only when it is caught in the middle of the change (3.41–42), and the pride that roars (3.35, 3.38) is given its moment and no more. These readings are interpretations; the text itself states only the battle and the gods’ joy.

Themes

  • The ease (līlā) of the Goddess in battle
  • The lion and the gaṇas: those who fight as hers
  • Mahiṣa’s rage at the scale of the world
  • Shape-changing and the escape that fails
  • Two intoxications: her drink and his pride
  • Victory turned into the gods’ praise

5Vyākaraṇa

A. Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • तत्सैन्यमवलोक्य tatsainyamavalokya → तत् + सैन्यम् + अवलोक्य
    no change; final m before a vowel · खरि च (8.4.55)
    The t of तत् stays unvoiced before s; the final m of सैन्यम् is simply pronounced with the following vowel (सैन्यमवलोक्य). The words are only written together.
  • सेनानीश्चिक्षुरः senānīścikṣuraḥ → सेनानीः + चिक्षुरः
    visarga sandhi · विसर्जनीयस्य सः (8.3.34), स्तोः श्चुना श्चुः (8.4.40)
    Visarga before c becomes s, and s next to c becomes ś.
  • कोपाद्ययौ kopādyayau → कोपात् + ययौ
    consonant sandhi (voicing) · झलां जशोऽन्ते (8.2.39)
    Final t becomes d before the voiced y.
  • योद्धुमथाम्बिकाम् yoddhumathāmbikām → योद्धुम् + अथ + अम्बिकाम्

    a + a → ā (अथ + अम्बिकाम् → अथाम्बिकाम्). Inside the compound महासुरः the same rule joins महा + असुर.

B. Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • महासुरः mahāsuraḥ — karmadhāraya
    Members: महत् + असुर · Vigraha: महान् च असौ असुरः च mahān ca asau asuraḥ ca
    Literally “a great asura”; in the verse: a mighty demon — the standard epithet of Mahiṣa’s captains
  • सेनानीः senānīḥ — upapada tatpuruṣa (with the root-noun नी, affix kvip)
    Members: सेना + नी · Vigraha: सेनां नयति इति senāṃ nayati iti
    Literally “one who leads the army”; in the verse: commander-in-chief; 2.40 calls Cikṣura महिषासुरसेनानीः

C. Morphology

  • निहन्यमानम् nihanyamānam — Passive present participle: the passive stem हन्य- (√हन् + yak) with -मान. ‘Being slain’ — the slaughter of 2.67–69 is still going on as Cikṣura looks.
  • ययौ yayau — Perfect of √या: roots in -ā take the ending -औ in the 3rd singular (य-यौ).
  • योद्धुम् yoddhum — Infinitive of √युध्: dh + t becomes ddh (युध् + तुम् → योद्धुम्), with guṇa of the root vowel. It expresses purpose after a verb of motion: ‘went to fight’.

D. Anvaya — prose order

अथ महासुरः सेनानीः चिक्षुरः तत् सैन्यं निहन्यमानम् अवलोक्य कोपात् अम्बिकां योद्धुं ययौ।

atha mahāsuraḥ senānīḥ cikṣuraḥ tat sainyaṃ nihanyamānam avalokya kopāt ambikāṃ yoddhuṃ yayau |

Subject with two appositions (महासुरः सेनानीः चिक्षुरः); an absolutive clause (सैन्यं निहन्यमानम् अवलोक्य); the main verb ययौ with a purpose infinitive.

6Translation

LiteralHaving seen that army being slaughtered, the great asura, the general Cikṣura, in anger then went to fight Ambikā.

ReadableSeeing his army cut down, the mighty asura Cikṣura, Mahiṣa’s general, went in fury to fight Ambikā.

‘His army’ and ‘Mahiṣa’s general’ make explicit what तत् and सेनानीः imply (Cikṣura is named Mahiṣa’s general at 2.40).

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningThe general steps forward when the army falls. Cikṣura was named first among Mahiṣa’s commanders at 2.40; now he faces the Goddess alone.

Traditional interpretationIn the Gita Press pāṭha, chapters 2–4 form the middle carita, whose presiding form is Mahālakṣmī (a liturgical attribution). Chapter 3 is its battle: the generals fall one by one (3.2–20) before Mahiṣa himself comes forward (3.21).

  • तोयदः toyadaḥ — The verb of the simile is not repeated: (वर्षति) ‘(rains on)’ is supplied from ववर्ष.

6Translation

LiteralHe, the asura, rained on the Goddess in the battle with a rain of arrows, as a cloud (rains) on the peak of Mount Meru with a rain of water.

ReadableIn the battle the asura poured a storm of arrows on the Goddess, as a cloud pours its rain on the summit of Meru.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningThe simile says what the arrows achieve: as much as rain does to Meru. The mountain is wetted, not moved.

6Translation

LiteralThen the Goddess, having cut his masses of arrows merely in play, struck down with arrows the horses and also the driver of the horses.

5Vyākaraṇa

A. Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • कांश्चित्तुण्ड kāṃścittuṇḍa → कांश्चित् + तुण्डप्रहारेण
    no change; n + c inside the pronoun · नश्छव्यप्रशान् (8.3.7), स्तोः श्चुना श्चुः (8.4.40)
    The t of चित् stays before t. Inside the pronoun, कान् + चित् → कांश्चित्: n before c becomes anusvāra with an inserted ś.
  • खुरक्षेपैस्तथापरान् khurakṣepaistathāparān → खुरक्षेपैः + तथा + अपरान्
    visarga sandhi; vowel sandhi (savarṇa-dīrgha) · विसर्जनीयस्य सः (8.3.34); अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः (6.1.101)
    Visarga before t becomes s; ā + a → ā.
  • लाङ्गूलताडितांश्चान्याञ्छृङ्गाभ्यां lāṅgūlatāḍitāṃścānyāñchṛṅgābhyāṃ → लाङ्गूलताडितान् + च + अन्यान् + शृङ्गाभ्याम्
    consonant sandhi (n + c, n + ś); vowel sandhi · नश्छव्यप्रशान् (8.3.7), स्तोः श्चुना श्चुः (8.4.40), शश्छोऽटि (8.4.63); अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः (6.1.101)
    -आन् + च → -आंश्च. च + अन्यान् → चान्यान्. Before ś a final n optionally takes an inserted t (a rule not in our list); the n becomes ñ and the ś after the stop becomes ch: अन्यान् + शृङ्गाभ्याम् → अन्याञ्छृङ्गाभ्याम्. The Gita Press spells this sandhi out; GRETIL and Freund leave it unapplied (चान्यान्शृङ्गाभ्यां) — an orthographic variant (corrections.json).

B. Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • तुण्डप्रहारेण tuṇḍaprahāreṇa — ṣaṣṭhī tatpuruṣa
    Members: तुण्ड + प्रहार · Vigraha: तुण्डस्य प्रहारः, तेन tuṇḍasya prahāraḥ, tena
    Literally “by a blow of the snout”; in the verse: butting with his muzzle
  • खुरक्षेपैः khurakṣepaiḥ — ṣaṣṭhī tatpuruṣa
    Members: खुर + क्षेप · Vigraha: खुराणां क्षेपाः, तैः khurāṇāṃ kṣepāḥ, taiḥ
    Literally “by flingings of the hooves”; in the verse: kicks and stamps
  • लाङ्गूलताडितान् lāṅgūlatāḍitān — tṛtīyā tatpuruṣa
    Members: लाङ्गूल + ताडित · Vigraha: लाङ्गूलेन ताडिताः, तान् lāṅgūlena tāḍitāḥ, tān
    Literally “struck by the tail”; in the verse: lashed with his tail

C. Morphology

  • कांश्चित् kāṃścit — The indefinite pronoun declines in its first part (कश्चित्, कांश्चित्, केचित्): accusative plural ‘some’. कांश्चित् … अपरान् … अन्यान् distribute the victims: ‘some … others … others’.
  • विदारितान् vidāritān — Past participle of the causative दारय- (वि + √दॄ): ‘made to burst, ripped open’.
  • शृङ्गाभ्याम् śṛṅgābhyām — Instrumental dual: the buffalo’s pair of horns.

D. Anvaya — prose order

कांश्चित् तुण्डप्रहारेण, तथा अपरान् खुरक्षेपैः, अन्यान् च लाङ्गूलताडितान्, (अन्यान्) च शृङ्गाभ्यां विदारितान् (पातयामास, 3.23)।

kāṃścit tuṇḍaprahāreṇa, tathā aparān khurakṣepaiḥ, anyān ca lāṅgūlatāḍitān, (anyān) ca śṛṅgābhyāṃ vidāritān (pātayāmāsa, 3.23) |

A verse without a verb: four groups of victims in the accusative, each with how Mahiṣa struck them. The verb पातयामास comes in 3.23.

6Translation

LiteralSome with blows of his muzzle, likewise others with kicks of his hooves, and others lashed by his tail, and (others) torn open with his two horns —

ReadableSome he struck down with blows of his muzzle, others with his hooves; others he lashed with his tail, and others he ripped open with his horns —

The verse continues into 3.23, whose verb पातयामास (‘he felled’) is anticipated here as ‘struck down’.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningMahiṣa fights as a buffalo, with every part of the animal: muzzle, hooves, tail, horns. The sentence runs on into 3.23, where the verb (‘he felled them on the ground’) finally comes.

5Vyākaraṇa

A. Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • कांश्चिदपरान् kāṃścidaparān → कांश्चित् + अपरान्
    consonant sandhi (voicing) · झलां जशोऽन्ते (8.2.39)
    Final t becomes d before a vowel. Inside कांश्चित् itself the n of कान् meets the c of चित् and becomes ṃś (नश्छव्यप्रशान्, 8.3.7), as in कांश्चित् at the head of 3.22.
  • अपरान्नादेन aparānnādena → अपरान् + नादेन
    no change · —
    Final n before the n of नादेन simply stands; the Gita Press writes the two words together.
  • निःश्वासपवनेनान्यान् niḥśvāsapavanenānyān → निःश्वासपवनेन + अन्यान्
    vowel sandhi (savarṇa-dīrgha) · अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः (6.1.101)
    a + a → ā.

B. Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • निःश्वासपवनेन niḥśvāsapavanena — ṣaṣṭhī tatpuruṣa
    Members: निःश्वास + पवन · Vigraha: निःश्वासस्य पवनः, तेन niḥśvāsasya pavanaḥ, tena
    Literally “by the wind of (his) out-breath”; in the verse: by the gale of his snorting breath
    Alternative: Also possible as a karmadhāraya, निःश्वासः एव पवनः ‘the breath which is a wind’; the sense is the same. निःश्वास is itself a prefixed noun (निस् + √श्वस् + घञ्), not a compound.
  • भूतले bhūtale — ṣaṣṭhī tatpuruṣa
    Members: भू + तल · Vigraha: भुवः तलम्, तस्मिन् bhuvaḥ talam, tasmin
    Literally “on the surface of the earth”; in the verse: on the ground

C. Morphology

  • पातयामास pātayāmāsa — Periphrastic perfect of the causative: पातय- + आम् + the perfect of √अस् (आस). Causatives and other derived stems form their perfect this way. ‘Made to fall’ = ‘felled’.
  • कांश्चित् kāṃścit — The indefinite pronoun: किम् with the particle चित्, declined in its first part (कः → कश्चित्, कान् → कांश्चित्). With अपरान् and अन्यान् it gives the distributive ‘some … others … others again’.
  • भ्रमणेन bhramaṇena — Action noun in -अन (ल्युट्) from √भ्रम् ‘to wander, whirl’; neuter.

D. Anvaya — prose order

(महिषासुरः) कांश्चित् वेगेन, अपरान् नादेन भ्रमणेन च, अन्यान् निःश्वासपवनेन भूतले पातयामास।

(mahiṣāsuraḥ) kāṃścit vegena, aparān nādena bhramaṇena ca, anyān niḥśvāsapavanena bhūtale pātayāmāsa |

पातयामास is the verb for the whole list begun in 3.22: the accusatives there (कांश्चित्, अपरान्, अन्यान्, the participles ताडितान्, विदारितान्) and here all depend on it, each with its own instrumental of means.

6Translation

LiteralSome by (his) charge, others by (his) bellowing and whirling, and others by the wind of (his) breath, he felled on the ground.

ReadableSome he threw to the ground by the force of his charge, others with his bellowing and his wheeling about, and others again with the blast of his breath.

The verb completes the sentence begun in 3.22; the subject, Mahiṣāsura, is named in 3.21.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningThe buffalo’s rampage through the Goddess’s gaṇas ends: every part of the animal — speed, voice, motion, breath — is a weapon. The single verb पातयामास gathers the long list of 3.22–23.

Traditional interpretationThe gaṇas he fells were born from the Goddess’s own breath in battle (निःश्वासान् मुमुचे … त एव सद्यः सम्भूता गणाः, 2.52–53). Here the buffalo fells them with the wind of his breath: breath against breath, before the duel itself begins.

  • प्रमथानीकम् pramathānīkam — ṣaṣṭhī tatpuruṣa

    Members: महत् + देवी · Vigraha: महती चासौ देवी च, तस्याः mahatī cāsau devī ca, tasyāḥ
    Literally “of the great Goddess”; in the verse: of the Goddess (whose mount is the lion)

C. Morphology

  • अभ्यधावत abhyadhāvata — Imperfect ātmanepada of √धाव् (धावति / धावते), with the augment between prefix and root: अभि + अ + धाव + त; इ before अ becomes य (इको यणचि, 6.1.77). The same form stands in 3.6 (अभ्यधावत तां देवीम्).
  • निपात्य nipātya — Absolutive of the causative stem पातय-: a prefixed verb takes -य (ल्यप्) for -त्वा, and the causative -अय- is dropped before it.
  • चक्रे cakre — Perfect ātmanepada of √कृ. कोपं चक्रे, ‘made anger’, is the regular idiom for ‘became angry’; 3.28 has the imperfect कोपं … अकरोत्.

D. Anvaya — prose order

सः असुरः प्रमथानीकं निपात्य महादेव्याः सिंहं हन्तुम् अभ्यधावत। ततः अम्बिका कोपं चक्रे।

saḥ asuraḥ pramathānīkaṃ nipātya mahādevyāḥ siṃhaṃ hantum abhyadhāvata | tataḥ ambikā kopaṃ cakre |

Two sentences: the asura’s charge (absolutive, object, infinitive of purpose, verb) and the Goddess’s answer (कोपं चक्रे).

6Translation

LiteralHaving struck down the army of the Pramathas, that asura rushed forward to kill the great Goddess’s lion. Then Ambikā became angry.

ReadableHaving cut down the Goddess’s host, the demon charged to kill the great Goddess’s lion. At that, Ambikā grew angry.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningWith the gaṇas down, Mahiṣa goes for the lion, the Goddess’s mount (given her by Himavān, 2.29). The attack on her vāhana is what turns her anger on him; from here the duel is between the two of them.

Traditional interpretationप्रमथ ‘tormentor’ is a name used elsewhere for Śiva’s troops; here it names the gaṇas the Goddess produced from her breath (2.53).

  • चूर्णयन्ती cūrṇayantī — Present participle of a curādi root (stem चूर्णय-) with the feminine suffix -ई: चूर्णयत् → चूर्णयन्ती. Her action runs while she speaks: ‘pulverising … she said’.
  • प्रहितान् prahitān — प्रहित ‘sent, hurled’ is the past participle of प्र + √हि ‘to send’; the identical-looking हित ‘placed’ belongs to √धा.
  • मदोद्धूतमुखरागाकुलाक्षरम् madoddhūtamukharāgākulākṣaram — A neuter accusative singular can describe the manner of an action (kriyāviśeṣaṇa): ‘she spoke in such a way that …’. उद्धूत is the past participle of उद् + √धू ‘to shake up, stir’.

A present participle of accompanying action (चूर्णयन्ती) with its own object, then the main verb उवाच with its person addressed (तम्) and an adverbial compound of manner.

6Translation

LiteralAnd she, pulverising with volleys of arrows those (mountains) hurled by him, spoke to him in a manner in which (her) syllables were confused by the flush of (her) face stirred up by intoxication.

ReadableGrinding to dust with volleys of arrows the mountains he hurled, she spoke to him, her face flushed with the drink and her words slurred:

The long compound is taken as an adverb describing her speech; the alternative (describing the asura) is given in Samāsa.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningThe verse introduces the Goddess’s only direct speech in the chapter (3.38). She speaks while still destroying his missiles: the words and the arrows come together. Readings differ over whose face is flushed: the compound fits the Goddess, who has just drunk, and can grammatically describe the asura instead (see Samāsa).

Textual Verification Required / uncertain: The reference of मदोद्धूतमुखरागाकुलाक्षरम् (the Goddess’s speech, adverbial; or the asura, agreeing with तम्) is an interpretive choice; the adverbial reading is preferred.

  • निजमुखात् nijamukhāt — karmadhāraya

    Literally “from (his) own mouth”; in the verse: out of the buffalo’s mouth — his own
  • अर्धनिष्क्रान्तः ardhaniṣkrāntaḥ — karmadhāraya (an adverbial first member with a participle; also classed as a supsupā compound)
    Members: अर्ध + निष्क्रान्त · Vigraha: अर्धं निष्क्रान्तः ardhaṃ niṣkrāntaḥ

C. Morphology

  • पदा padā — Instrumental of the consonant stem पद् ‘foot’, which supplies the weak cases of पाद (पदा, पद्भ्याम् …). The Goddess’s foot was पादेन in 3.40.
  • अर्धनिष्क्रान्तः ardhaniṣkrāntaḥ — निष्क्रान्त is the past participle of निस् + √क्रम् (s → ṣ after i, the root vowel lengthened before -त: क्रान्त).
  • संवृतः saṃvṛtaḥ — Past participle of सम् + √वृ: ‘covered, enclosed’, hence ‘held, restrained’.

D. Anvaya — prose order

ततः तया पदा आक्रान्तः सः अपि ततः निजमुखात् अर्धनिष्क्रान्तः एव आसीत्, देव्याः वीर्येण संवृतः (सन्)।

tataḥ tayā padā ākrāntaḥ saḥ api tataḥ nijamukhāt ardhaniṣkrāntaḥ eva āsīt, devyāḥ vīryeṇa saṃvṛtaḥ (san) |

The first ततः is ‘then’, the second ‘from there’ (from the buffalo body), defined by निजमुखात्. देव्याः may also be read as देव्या (instrumental, ‘by the Goddess’) with संवृतः; the spelling देव्या वीर्येण fits both.

6Translation

LiteralThen he too, pinned by her foot, from there, from his own mouth, had only half come out, held fast by the power of the Goddess.

ReadablePinned beneath her foot, the asura began to come forth from the buffalo’s mouth in his own form, but got only halfway, held fast by the Goddess’s power.

‘In his own form’ and ‘the buffalo’s mouth’ interpret निजमुखात् ततः: he emerges from the mouth of the body he is in. ‘Began to’ renders the sense of एव ‘only (half)’.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningThe last transformation fails. Pinned by her foot, the asura tries to leave the buffalo body through its mouth and is caught halfway, held by the Goddess’s power.

Traditional interpretationMany images of Mahiṣāsuramardinī show exactly this moment: the asura in human form half emerged from the body of the buffalo, the Goddess’s foot or lion upon him.

Modern reflectionA modern reading: the asura is caught between the shape he has worn and the one he is trying to escape into. Every earlier escape worked by changing form; this one is stopped in the middle of the change.

Textual Verification Required / uncertain: देव्या वीर्येण: genitive देव्याः (‘by the Goddess’s power’, chosen) or instrumental देव्या (‘by the Goddess, with her power’); the printed text allows both.

C. Morphology

  • तुष्टुवुः tuṣṭuvuḥ — Perfect third plural of √स्तु: the reduplicated syllable keeps only the stop (तु-), the root’s s becomes ṣ after the u of the reduplication (ष्टु, with t → ṭ beside ṣ), and u → uv before the vowel ending.
  • जगुः jaguḥ — Perfect third plural of √गै, treated as गा: ज-ग्-उः, the ā lost before the vowel ending (like ययुः, 3.27).
  • ननृतुः nanṛtuḥ — Perfect third plural of √नृत्: reduplication न-, the root ṛ unchanged in the weak (plural) forms.

Three clauses, each with its own subject and perfect verb: the gods praise, the Gandharvas sing, the Apsarases dance.

6Translation

LiteralThe gods, together with the heavenly great seers, praised that Goddess; the lords of the Gandharvas sang, and the troupes of Apsarases danced.

ReadableThe gods, with the heavenly great sages, sang the Goddess’s praise; the Gandharva lords sang and the Apsarases danced.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningThe chapter closes in celebration: praise, song and dance. तुष्टुवुः ‘they praised’ is taken up in the next chapter, where the gods led by Indra sing their hymn to the Goddess (तां तुष्टुवुः, 4.2).

Traditional interpretationIn the Gita Press pāṭha this is the last mantra of the third chapter and carries the closing bīja ॐ, which belongs to the recitation, not to the verse.

Chapter conclusion

Narrative summary

Mahiṣa’s generals come out one at a time and die one at a time — Cikṣura in a chariot duel, Cāmara under the lion’s paw, the rest in a single catalogue. Then Mahiṣa himself fights as a buffalo, and his rage reaches earth, sea and sky. Caṇḍikā binds him; he slips from form to form — lion, man, elephant, buffalo again — until, with her foot on his neck, he is caught halfway out of the buffalo and beheaded. The chapter ends with what she promised in her one speech: the gods rejoicing, and the sound of praise (3.43–44), which chapter 4 takes up as a hymn.

Important grammatical constructions

  • The reduplicated perfect as the tense of narrative, singular and plural (जघान, चिच्छेद, विव्याध; ययुः, निपेतुः, तुष्टुवुः, ननृतुः).
  • The periphrastic perfect in -आमास for causatives and class-10 stems (पातयामास, त्रासयामास, चूर्णयामास, क्षोभयामास).
  • Passive narration: the past participle as verb, the agent in the instrumental (करप्रहारेण शिरश्चामरस्य पृथक्कृतम्, 3.16; देव्या … हतः, 3.17; तया … निपातितः, 3.42).
  • The locative absolute for the time of an action (हते तस्मिन् … चमूपतौ, 3.11; स्वसैन्ये संक्षीयमाणे, 3.21; मया त्वयि हते, 3.38).
  • यावत् … तावत् with the present for an act on the point of happening (3.30), and यावत् alone with the present for an act that runs alongside (3.38).
  • Distributive pronouns (कांश्चित् … अपरान् … अन्यान्) with instrumentals of means, gathered by one verb across two verses (3.22–23).
  • A chain of absolutives and a participle leading to one finite verb (उक्त्वा, समुत्पत्य, आरूढा, आक्रम्य … अताडयत्, 3.40).
  • The imperative and the future of one root set against each other (गर्ज गर्ज … गर्जिष्यन्ति, 3.38).

Śākta insights

  • Nothing the asuras bring reaches her: arrows are cut ‘as if at play’ (3.4), a sword shatters on her arm (3.8), a lance falls to a single sound (3.12).
  • The Goddess fights through what is hers: the lion kills Cāmara (3.14–16), the gaṇas born from her breath bear Mahiṣa’s first assault (3.21–23), and it is the attack on her lion that turns her anger on him (3.24, 3.32).
  • The asura’s disorder is cosmic, not private: earth, ocean, clouds and mountains suffer (3.25–27), and the three worlds tremble again when he resumes the buffalo (3.33) — which is why the one who acts is named Mother of the world (3.34).
  • Escape by changing form is answered form by form (3.29–33) and ends when he is caught between forms (3.41–42).
  • Her intoxication and his stand face to face: she drinks and laughs (3.34, 3.38), he is ‘drunk with pride’ (बलवीर्यमदोद्धतः, 3.35) and cannot see what he fights.
  • The victory belongs to the gods only as joy and praise (3.38, 3.43–44); the praise becomes the next chapter’s hymn (4.2).

Śrīvidyā connections

The chapter shares a few names and images with the Lalitā Sahasranāma (Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa): Parameśvarī (3.19; name 396, परमेश्वरी), Caṇḍikā (3.28, 3.34, 3.35; name 755, चण्डिका), the red eyes of the drinking Goddess (3.34; compare name 432, मदघूर्णितरक्ताक्षी), her taste for madhu (3.38; compare name 510, मधुप्रीता), and the noose (3.29), which the Lalitā Sahasranāma gives its own meaning, attachment (name 8, रागस्वरूपपाशाढ्या). These are a shared Śākta vocabulary, not evidence that one text depends on the other. The battle with Mahiṣa belongs to the Devī Māhātmya alone, and no specifically Śrīvidyā doctrine is stated in this chapter.

Verses for study and memorisation

  • 3.4 — She cuts Cikṣura’s arrows to pieces ‘as if at play’ (लीलयैव) and kills his horses and charioteer.
  • 3.12 — Ambikā brings Cāmara’s lance to the ground, its fire gone, with the sound ‘hum’ alone.
  • 3.29 — She binds the great asura with her noose; caught, he sheds his buffalo form — the transformations begin.
  • 3.34 — Caṇḍikā, the Mother of the world, in fury drinks the supreme drink again and again and laughs, her eyes red.
  • 3.38 — ‘Roar, fool, roar for a moment while I drink’ — the Goddess’s only speech: the gods will roar when he is slain.
  • 3.41 — Pinned by her foot, the asura is caught half-emerged from the buffalo’s mouth, held by her power.
  • 3.42 — Still half emerged and still fighting, he is struck down by the Goddess, who cuts off his head with her great sword.

Sandhi patterns
  • Visarga sandhi is the commonest junction: -aḥ → -o before voiced sounds (ततो देवी), -ḥ → s/ś before t/c (ततस्तौ, सेनानीश्चिक्षुरः), -ḥ → r before vowels and voiced sounds (बाणैर्यन्तारं), and the special rule for सः (स देवीं, 3.3; सच्छिन्नधन्वा, 3.6).
  • -aḥ before a vowel other than a loses its visarga and leaves the vowels apart (तत एवाशु, 3.31; अर्धनिष्क्रान्त एवासीद्, 3.41; अर्धनिष्क्रान्त एवासौ, 3.42).
  • Pūrvarūpa with avagraha (समरेऽसुरः, सोऽभून्महागजः), once on a dual verb that strict grammar would leave pragṛhya (युयुधातेऽतिसंरब्धौ, 3.15, kept as ārṣa); ā + ā written with the double avagraha (साऽऽरूढा, 3.40; पदाऽऽक्रान्तः, 3.41).
  • Consonant sandhi: t + ś → cch (तच्छूलं, 3.10), ch doubled after a short vowel (तस्यच्छित्त्वा, 3.4), t → n before nasals (तस्मान्नागान्महीं, 3.15), and n before c and ś (-आंश्च, -आञ्छृ-, 3.22, where the Gita Press spells out what the witnesses leave unapplied).
  • Optional visarga kept before a sibilant (मेरुगिरेः शृङ्गं, 3.3; ततः सिंहः, 3.14); savarṇa-dīrgha, guṇa and vṛddhi across words and inside compounds (लीलयैव, शरोत्करान्).
