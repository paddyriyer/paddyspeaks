Uttama Carita · Chapter 7 of 13 · 27 mantras (7.1–7.27)

चण्डमुण्डवधः

caṇḍamuṇḍavadhaḥ

The Slaying of Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa · Complete

Introduction

Summary
Medhas takes up the story (7.1). Commanded by Śumbha (6.21–24), the daityas march out with Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa at their head, an army of all four arms with weapons raised (7.2). They find the Devī, a faint smile on her face, seated on her lion on a great golden peak of the king of mountains (7.3), and move to seize her, others closing in with bows drawn and swords bared (7.4). Ambikā’s anger rises against them and her face turns black as ink (7.5); from her frowning brow springs Kālī, terrible of face, bearing sword and noose (7.6), with a skull-staff, a garland of human heads, a leopard’s hide and withered flesh, a gaping mouth, a lolling tongue, sunken red eyes and a roar that fills the quarters (7.7–8). She falls upon the army and devours it (7.9): the elephants with their riders, drivers and bells flung into her mouth with one hand (7.10), horsemen and chariots with their drivers crunched between her teeth (7.11), foot soldiers seized by the hair or the throat, trampled or crushed against her breast (7.12); even the weapons and missiles hurled at her she catches in her mouth and grinds to pieces (7.13). The asura host perishes by her sword, her skull-staff and her teeth (7.14–15). Seeing the army laid low in a moment, Caṇḍa rushes at her (7.16); he buries her under arrows, and Muṇḍa under discuses by the thousand (7.17), which vanish into her mouth like suns into a dark cloud (7.18). Kālī laughs a terrible laugh (7.19), mounts the great lion, charges Caṇḍa, seizes him by the hair and strikes off his head with her sword (7.20); Muṇḍa charges and she fells him too (7.21), and what is left of the army scatters in terror (7.22). Kālī takes the two heads to Caṇḍikā with peals of savage laughter (7.23) and offers them as two great victims in the sacrifice of battle, leaving Śumbha and Niśumbha for the Goddess herself (7.24). Medhas continues (7.25): gracious Caṇḍikā speaks gently to Kālī (7.26) and gives her a name — because she came bearing Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa, she will be known in the world as Cāmuṇḍā (7.27).
Forms of the Devī
The Devī (देवी, 7.3), smiling and still on her lion upon the golden peak; Ambikā (7.5), whose anger darkens her face — the Ambikā who came forth from Pārvatī’s body (5.87); Kālī (काली, 7.6, 7.16, 7.19, 7.23, 7.26), born from Ambikā’s frowning brow, terrible of face, with sword and noose (असिपाशिनी, 7.6), skull-staff, garland of human heads and leopard’s hide (7.7), gaping mouth, lolling tongue and sunken red eyes (7.8) — ‘the dread-eyed’ (भीमाक्षी, 7.17), ‘she of the terrible roar’ (भैरवनादिनी, 7.19), and the देवी who rides the lion against Caṇḍa (7.20); Caṇḍikā (चण्डिका, 7.23, 7.26), the Goddess to whom the heads are brought, called ‘the auspicious’ (कल्याणी, 7.26); and Cāmuṇḍā (चामुण्डा, 7.27), the name Caṇḍikā gives to Kālī. This Kālī is not the Kālikā of 5.88, the name Pārvatī bears when she turns dark: here Kālī comes out of Ambikā. Mātaṅgī, of the dhyāna, is the form of the liturgy, not of the text.
Significance of the events
This is the second embassy of force sent against the Devī, after Dhūmralocana (chapter 6), and it closes a thread begun in 5.89: Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa were the servants who first saw Ambikā and told Śumbha of her beauty, and they are the first of his great asuras to die. The chapter brings a new figure into the war: for the first time in the third episode a separate goddess comes out of the Devī’s own body, and it is her wrath (7.5–6) that takes form. Kālī fights by devouring, and the chapter ends with her naming as Cāmuṇḍā (7.27) — the name she bears in chapter 8, where Caṇḍikā asks her to open her mouth wide (8.53) and she drinks Raktabīja’s blood (8.59–61), and in the hymn of chapter 11 (11.21). Two reversals frame the battle: Śumbha ordered the Devī dragged by the hair (6.23), and Caṇḍa is seized by the hair and beheaded (7.20); the asuras came to bring her back, and their own heads are brought to her (7.23–24).
Sanskrit patterns to watch
A narrative chapter of perfects — reduplicated (ययुः, ददृशुः, चक्रुः, चिक्षेप, जग्राह, ममर्द, जग्मुः, अभिदुद्राव, बभुः, जहास), the weak perfect भेजे (7.22) and the periphrastic छादयामास (7.17) — beside imperfects (अभक्षयत, अपोथयत्, अताडयत्, अच्छिनत्, अपातयत्). The same verb appears in both voices: अभक्षयत (7.9) and अभक्षयत् (7.14), अधावत (7.20) and अभ्यधावत् (7.21). The portrait of Kālī (7.6–8) is a string of bahuvrīhis in the feminine nominative, with no finite verb in 7.7 and 7.8; in 7.11 a participle (चर्वयन्ती) carries the action. Absolutives with -य after a prefix (समादाय, निक्षिप्य, आक्रम्य, उत्थाय, अभ्येत्य) stand beside -त्वा (दृष्ट्वा, गृहीत्वा); there is an infinitive of purpose (समादातुम्, 7.4), passive participles with an instrumental agent (7.13, 7.15), a simile with यथा (7.18), distributive pronouns (एकम् … अपरम् … अन्यम्, 7.12; केचित् … केचित्, 7.15), simple futures (हनिष्यसि, 7.24; भविष्यसि, 7.27), a causal यस्मात् … ततः (7.27), and the closing name explained by its own verse (7.27).
The teaching (as distinct from the narrative)
Literally, a goddess grows angry, a second and terrible goddess springs from her brow, devours an army, kills its two generals and brings their heads back, and is given a name. As teaching, the chapter shows that the fierce form is not a stranger to the gracious one: Kālī comes out of Ambikā (7.6) and returns to Caṇḍikā (7.23), and the same Goddess who smiles in 7.3 and speaks gently in 7.26 is the one whose anger becomes Kālī. Her wrath is directed — ‘against those enemies’ (7.5) — and it leaves nothing over: army, mounts and weapons all go into her mouth (7.10–13, 7.18). The tradition reads that mouth as the place where every form ends, and Kālī’s ‘sacrifice of battle’ (7.24) as the offering of the pride and ignorance the asuras stand for; both are readings of the tradition, not statements of the verses, which speak only of an army eaten and a battle called a sacrifice. A modern reflection: the power that does the work lays its result at the feet of the gracious one (7.24), and is honoured for it with a name (7.27).

Themes

  • Wrath made form: Kālī from the Devī’s brow
  • The devouring mouth: nothing left over
  • The hair-dragging order turned back on Caṇḍa
  • Battle as sacrifice (yuddhayajña)
  • The terrible and the gracious as one Goddess
  • Cāmuṇḍā: a name won by a deed

6Translation

LiteralThen those Daityas, commanded, with Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa at their head, furnished with a fourfold army, with weapons raised, went.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningThe order of 6.21–24 is carried out. The army is चतुरङ्ग, the four divisions of the classical Indian army; the verses that follow show each of them ending in Kālī’s mouth: elephants (7.10), then warriors, horses and chariots with their drivers (7.11).

Traditional interpretationCaṇḍa and Muṇḍa are the generals of Śumbha and Niśumbha. Their names (caṇḍa ‘fierce’, muṇḍa ‘shaven, a head’) will give the Goddess one of her best-known names at the end of this chapter (7.27).

  • व्यवस्थिताम् vyavasthitām — Past participle of वि + अव + √स्था: स्था weakens to स्थित; i + a gives vy (इको यणचि, 6.1.77). Active in sense: ‘(she who was) stationed’.

D. Anvaya — prose order

6Translation

LiteralThen they saw the Goddess, gently smiling, seated upon the lion, on a great golden peak of the king of mountains.

ReadableThere they saw the Goddess, a faint smile on her face, seated on her lion upon a great golden summit of the king of mountains.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningThe king of mountains is the Himālaya, where the gods praised her (5.7) and where Śumbha’s servants first saw her ‘lighting up the Himālaya’ (5.90). Her faint smile before an advancing army is the same composure she showed Śumbha’s messenger (5.104 onward).

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningKālī is born from the Goddess’s frowning brow. This is a different event from 5.88, where Pārvatī herself turns dark and is called Kālikā after Ambikā has come out of her body; here Kālī comes out of Ambikā.

Traditional interpretationThe Śākta tradition reads the sequence of 7.5–6 as anger taking form: the Devī’s wrath does not stay a mood but becomes a goddess who acts. Sword and noose are her first attributes; 7.7–8 complete the picture.

Modern reflectionA modern reading: the fierce form does not come from outside the gracious one. The same Goddess who smiled in 7.3 frowns here, and what she sends out is still herself.

6Translation

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningBoth generals now attack together: Caṇḍa with arrows, Muṇḍa with discuses. ‘Covered’ (छादयामास) is the image of a storm of weapons that hides the target from sight.

Textual Verification Required / uncertain: महासुरः could also be read in apposition to मुण्डः (‘the great asura Muṇḍa covered her with arrows and discuses’). Since 7.16 makes Caṇḍa the attacker, he is taken as the subject of the first line.

Direct textual meaningKālī presents the two heads as sacrificial victims (पशु) and calls the battle a sacrifice (युद्धयज्ञ). She leaves the last two victims, Śumbha and Niśumbha, to the Goddess herself — which is how the story will end (Niśumbha in 9.34–36, Śumbha in 10.27).

Chapter conclusion

Narrative summary

Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa, the two who first reported the Devī’s beauty to Śumbha (5.89), come with a full army to take her by force. Her anger darkens her face, and out of her brow comes Kālī, who eats the army whole — elephants, horses, chariots, soldiers and even their weapons — laughs at the discuses that vanish into her mouth, and beheads the two generals. She brings their heads to Caṇḍikā as victims in the sacrifice of battle, leaving Śumbha and Niśumbha to the Goddess herself (7.24). The chapter ends with a name: Caṇḍikā, speaking gently, calls her Cāmuṇḍā (7.27).

Important grammatical constructions

  • A portrait in bahuvrīhis: a string of feminine nominatives with the verb ‘to be’ understood (करालवदना, नरमालाविभूषणा, द्वीपिचर्मपरीधाना, 7.6–8).
  • The same verb in both voices within a few verses: अभक्षयत (7.9) / अभक्षयत् (7.14); अधावत (7.20) / अभ्यधावत् (7.21).
  • The periphrastic perfect with two subjects sharing one verb: महासुरः … मुण्डः च … छादयामास (7.17).
  • Passive participles with an instrumental agent: तैः … असुरैः मुक्तानि (7.13); असिना निहताः, खट्वाङ्गताडिताः (7.15).
  • Distributive pronouns giving the pace of the slaughter: एकम् … अपरम् … अन्यम् … अन्यम् (7.12); केचित् … केचित् (7.15).
  • A simile with यथा matching colour to colour: discuses into her mouth as suns into a cloud (7.18).
  • The genitive of the person offered to (मया तव उपहृतौ, 7.24) and the simple future of a promise (हनिष्यसि, 7.24; भविष्यसि, 7.27).
  • A causal pair यस्मात् … ततः and a name explained by the verse that gives it (चामुण्डा, 7.27).

Śākta insights

  • The fierce form is the Devī’s own: Kālī comes out of Ambikā’s frowning brow (7.5–6) and comes back to Caṇḍikā with the victory (7.23–24). One Goddess acts in both forms.
  • Her wrath is not blind: it rises ‘against those enemies’ (7.5), and what it destroys it takes in completely — the army, its mounts and even its weapons (7.10–13, 7.18).
  • What the asuras intended for her falls on them: Śumbha ordered her dragged by the hair (6.23), and Caṇḍa is seized by the hair and beheaded (7.20); they came to bring her, and their heads are brought to her (7.23–24).
  • Kālī calls the battle a sacrifice and the generals its victims, and leaves the last victims to Caṇḍikā (7.24); the Śākta tradition reads this as the offering of pride and ignorance — an interpretation, not the verse’s own words.
  • The terrible and the gracious are set side by side without contradiction: Kālī’s savage laughter (7.23) and Caṇḍikā’s gentle words (7.26), the smile of 7.3 and the black face of 7.5.
  • A name is given for a deed: Kālī becomes Cāmuṇḍā (7.27), the name by which she acts in chapter 8 and is saluted in chapter 11 (11.21).

Śrīvidyā connections

Two names this chapter gives the Goddess are also names in the Lalitā Sahasranāma (Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa): Ambikā (295, अम्बिका), at 7.5, and Kalyāṇī (324, कल्याणी), at 7.26. One connection is specific: the Lalitā Sahasranāma names the Goddess Caṇḍikā (755, चण्डिका) and, immediately after, Caṇḍamuṇḍāsuraniṣūdinī (756, चण्डमुण्डासुरनिषूदिनी, ‘slayer of the asuras Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa’) — the deed of this chapter, there ascribed to the Goddess herself, where here it is done by Kālī, who came out of her (7.6). The ललितम् of 7.26 (‘gentle’ words) is a coincidence of vocabulary, not a reference to Lalitā. The dhyāna the pāṭha sets before the chapter is of Mātaṅgī, the dark goddess of the vīṇā and the parrot; in Śrīvidyā practice Mātaṅgī is identified with Śyāmalā, the Mantriṇī or minister of the Goddess, whom the Lalitā Sahasranāma names in 69 (गेयचक्ररथारूढमन्त्रिणीपरिसेविता), 75 (मन्त्रिण्यम्बाविरचितविषङ्गवधतोषिता) and 786 (मन्त्रिणीन्यस्तराज्यधूः) — but that link belongs to the liturgy, not to the Purāṇa text. Name 75, the Goddess pleased by a slaying done by her Mantriṇī, resembles Caṇḍikā receiving Kālī’s victory (7.24–27) in pattern only; the myths are different and neither text depends on the other. No specifically Śrīvidyā doctrine is stated in this chapter.

Sandhi patterns
  • -aḥ before voiced sounds → -o (ततो दैत्याः, ततो देवीम्, ततो जहास, दिशो भेजे, ततो लोके), and before a → -o with avagraha (चण्डोऽभिदुद्राव, मुण्डोऽभ्यधावत्).
  • -āḥ before a voiced sound → -ā (चतुरङ्गबलोपेता ययुः, अस्या वदनम्, असुरा दन्ता-); visarga → s/ś before t/c (आज्ञप्तास्ते, ददृशुस्ते, दैत्याश्चण्ड-, शिरश्चण्डस्य, दशनैश्चर्वयन्ती).
  • -iḥ, -uḥ, -aiḥ → r before voiced sounds (ऋषिरुवाच, चक्रुरुद्यताः, जग्मुर्विनाशम्, बभुर्यथा, तैर्मुक्तानि, शरवर्षैर्महाभीमैर्भीमाक्षीम्); visarga dropped before r (तुरगै रथम्, 7.11); kept before kṣ (मुण्डः क्षिप्तैः, 7.17), as the Gita Press prints it.
  • Savarṇa-dīrgha everywhere inside compounds (खट्वाङ्ग, महासुरान्, दन्ताग्राभिहताः); guṇa (सिंहस्योपरि, चकारोच्चैः, घनोदरम्, चामुण्डेति), vṛddhi (समादायैकहस्तेन, तथैव, चैवान्यम्), yaṇ (चर्वयन्त्यतिभैरवम्, चक्राण्यनेकानि, तमप्यपातयत्) and ayādi (तावानीतौ, 7.26).
  • Consonants: t → d before voiced sounds (तद्बलम्, ललाटफलकाद्द्रुतम्, अपातयद्भूमौ; ईषद्धासाम् with h → dh), t → n and k → ṅ before m (तन्मुखम्, दिङ्मुखा), t + c → cc (यस्माच्चण्डम्, अभक्षयच्चान्यान्), n + c → ṃśc (अन्यांश्च, 7.14), and ch doubled after the augment (अच्छिनत्, 7.20).
Samāsa constructions
  • Bahuvrīhis of the portrait of Kālī (करालवदना, नरमालाविभूषणा, द्वीपिचर्मपरीधाना, अतिविस्तारवदना, निमग्नारक्तनयना), one with an instrumental relative (नादापूरितदिङ्मुखा, ‘by whom the quarters were filled’), and others of description: ईषद्धासा, मषीवर्ण (with an implied comparison), भीमाक्षी (-अक्ष stem), दुरात्मन्, सुमहावीर्य, अभ्युद्यतायुध.
  • Upapada tatpuruṣas with agent endings -धर, -ग, -ग्राह, -इन्: विचित्रखट्वाङ्गधरा, आकृष्टचापासिधराः, तत्समीपगाः, पुरोगम, पार्ष्णिग्राह, भैरवनादिनी.
  • Tṛtīyā tatpuruṣas of means and cause: खट्वाङ्गताडित, दन्ताग्राभिहत, खड्गाभिहत, भयातुर, भ्रुकुटीकुटिल, जिह्वाललनभीषणा, शुष्कमांसातिभैरवा, प्रचण्डाट्टहासमिश्र; one pañcamī tatpuruṣa (हतशेष, 7.22).
  • Itaretara dvandvas: चण्डमुण्डौ (7.24, 7.26; inside the bahuvrīhi चण्डमुण्डपुरोगमाः, 7.2), असिपाशिनी (a dvandva with the possessive -इन्, 7.6), and the four-member list inside पार्ष्णिग्राहाङ्कुशग्राहियोधघण्टासमन्वितान् (7.10).
  • Karmadhārayas with महा- (महासुर, महास्त्र, महापशु, महासिंह) and a rūpaka (युद्धयज्ञ, ‘battle that is a sacrifice’, 7.24); prādi tatpuruṣas with अति- (अतिभैरव, अतिभीषण, अतिरुष्); a nañ tatpuruṣa (अनेक).
  • The chapter’s longest compound describes Kālī’s laughter-lit teeth: कालीकरालवक्त्रान्तर्दुर्दर्शदशनोज्ज्वला (7.19).
Grammatical observations
  • Voice varies within a verb: अभक्षयत (ātmanepada, 7.9) / अभक्षयत् (7.14); अधावत (7.20) / अभ्यधावत् (7.21); and an ātmanepada participle विशमानानि from a parasmaipada root (7.18).
  • 7.7 and 7.8 have no finite verb, and in 7.11 the participle चर्वयन्ती carries the action; the anvayas supply (आसीत्).
  • उत्थाय with the accusative महासिंहम् means ‘mounting’ the lion (7.20); देवी there is Kālī, the subject since 7.16.
  • One verb serves two subjects in 7.17 (छादयामास) and two objects in 7.12 (अपोथयत्); महासुरः in 7.17 is taken as Caṇḍa.
  • मुण्डम् एव च (7.23) means Muṇḍa’s head as well; तव (7.24) is a genitive in place of the dative of the one offered to; महापशू is a nominative dual.
  • भेजे (7.22) is the weak perfect of √भज्, singular with the collective सैन्यम्; चामुण्डा (7.27) is a nirvacana, not a regular compound.
