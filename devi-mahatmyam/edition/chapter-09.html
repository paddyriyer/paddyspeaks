Uttama Carita · Chapter 9 of 13 · 41 mantras (9.1–9.41)

निशुम्भवधः

niśumbhavadhaḥ

The Slaying of Niśumbha · Complete

Introduction

Summary
King Suratha calls the slaying of Raktabīja wonderful and asks what Śumbha and the furious Niśumbha did next (9.1–3). Medhas answers: both brothers flew into a rage (9.4–5). Niśumbha charged with the pick of the asura army, his champions guarding him on every side, and Śumbha, who had been fighting the Mothers, came on too (9.6–8). The brothers rained arrows on the Devī like two storm-clouds; Caṇḍikā cut them down and wounded both (9.9–10). Niśumbha struck her lion on the head, and one after another his weapons were destroyed — sword and shield by a razor-headed arrow, his śakti by her discus, his spear by her fist, his mace burnt to ash by her trident — until, coming on axe in hand, he was felled by her arrows (9.11–16). Śumbha took his place, eight-armed in his chariot, filling the sky (9.17–18). The Goddess answered with sound: her conch and bowstring, her bell that drains the Daityas’ tejas, the lion’s roar, Kālī striking sky and earth so that every earlier sound was drowned, and Śivadūtī’s laughter, at which the asuras trembled (9.19–23). She cried ‘Stand, wicked one, stand!’ and the gods in the sky cried ‘Victory!’ (9.24). Śumbha’s flaming dart was struck aside by a great firebrand, his roar was overcome by a crash like thunder, and the two cut each other’s arrows by the thousand (9.25–27), until Caṇḍikā struck him with her spear and he fell senseless (9.28). Niśumbha, recovering, shot at the Goddess, Kālī and the lion, made himself ten thousand arms and buried Caṇḍikā under discuses; Durgā cut them all down (9.29–31). He charged with a mace, which she cut through with her sword before it reached her; he took up a spear, and she pierced his heart with hers (9.32–34). From his heart came another being, mighty and crying ‘Stop!’; laughing aloud, the Goddess struck off its head, and he fell (9.35–36). The lion, Kālī and Śivadūtī devoured the asuras, and the Mothers — Kaumārī, Brahmāṇī, Māheśvarī, Vārāhī, Vaiṣṇavī and Aindrī — destroyed the rest with their own weapons; some perished, some fled (9.37–41).
Forms of the Devī
The Devī (9.2, 9.7, 9.9 and throughout the duels); Caṇḍikā, ‘the fierce one’, the name the battle verses use most (9.8, 9.10, 9.15, 9.28, 9.30, 9.32–34); Ambikā, ‘Mother’, even in battle (9.17, 9.24); Bhagavatī Durgā, ‘who destroys distress in hard places’ (दुर्गार्तिनाशिनी, 9.31). Beside her fight her own powers: Kālī, who struck sky and earth (9.22, 9.29, 9.37, 9.41); Śivadūtī, whose laughter shook the asuras (9.23, 9.37, 9.41); and the Mothers (मातृ, 9.8) named with their weapons — Kaumārī and Brahmāṇī (9.38), Māheśvarī and Vārāhī (9.39), Vaiṣṇavī and Aindrī (9.40); Nārasiṃhī is not named here. Her lion fights throughout (9.11, 9.21, 9.29, 9.37, 9.41).
Significance of the events
The chapter is named for the first of the two brothers to die (colophon: निशुम्भवधः). It is built as a series of answers: every weapon Niśumbha takes up is destroyed by a different weapon of hers (9.11–16, 9.31–33), and Śumbha’s show of eight arms is answered by sound before any blow (9.18–23). The Goddess still fights with her companions — Kālī, Śivadūtī, the lion and the Mothers — and the rout at the end is theirs as much as hers (9.37–41). That sets up chapter 10, where Śumbha, the brother kept alive by a swoon (9.28), taunts her for fighting by the strength of others (10.3), and she draws them all back into herself (10.5–6).
Sanskrit patterns to watch
A battle narrative in the perfect: reduplicated forms (चिच्छेद, चिक्षेप, जग्राह, विव्याध, अभिजघान), the weak perfects with e and no reduplication (त्रेसुः, 9.23; नेशुः, 9.38; पेतुः, 9.39; विनेशुः, 9.41), and the periphrastic perfect of causative stems (ताडयामास, 9.10; पूरयामास, 9.20–21; छादयामास, 9.30). Locative absolutes set each new turn (रक्तबीजे निपातिते, 9.3, 9.5; ताडिते वाहने, 9.12; छिन्ने चर्मणि खड्गे च, 9.13; the long one of 9.17). Past participles often stand as the finite verb (आगता, 9.15; निरस्ता, 9.25; निःसृतः, 9.35), and 9.26 has the past active participle in -तवत् (जितवान्). The learner also meets an impersonal passive of speech (जयेत्यभिहितं देवैः, 9.24), causatives in both padas (अपातयत, 9.16; अभ्यधावत beside अभ्यधावत्, 9.32, 9.6), samprasāraṇa in √व्यध् (आविध्य, 9.15; विव्याध, आविद्ध, 9.34), the distributive -शस् (शतशः, सहस्रशः, 9.27), the च्वि construction (चूर्णीकृताः, 9.39) and repetition for distribution (खण्डं खण्डम्, 9.40).
The teaching (as distinct from the narrative)
Literally, Niśumbha attacks, is felled, recovers and is killed; Śumbha is struck senseless; the asura army is devoured, slain or scattered. As teaching, the Śākta tradition reads the chapter for what it shows of the Goddess: nothing aimed at her reaches her (each weapon is undone, 9.11–16, 9.25, 9.31–33); her presence alone, as sound, drains the enemy’s strength before the fight (9.20, 9.23); and the Mothers, Kālī and Śivadūtī are her own powers acting as many (9.37–41). The figure that leaves Niśumbha’s pierced heart still crying ‘Stop!’ (9.35–36) is not explained by the text; reading it as the force that drove him, which must be cut down where it emerges, is interpretation, and is offered as such in the commentary.

Themes

  • Weapon answered by weapon: nothing aimed at her reaches her
  • Sound as power: the bell, the roar and Kālī’s blow
  • The Mothers and companions as her own powers
  • Durgā, who ends distress in hard places
  • The second self from the pierced heart
  • Two brothers, two deaths: one now, one kept for chapter 10

B. Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • चरितमाहात्म्यम् caritamāhātmyam — ṣaṣṭhī tatpuruṣa
    Members: चरित + माहात्म्य · Vigraha: चरितस्य माहात्म्यम् caritasya māhātmyam
    Literally “the greatness of the deeds”; in the verse: the glory shown in what the Devī did

    Literally “resting on the slaying of Raktabīja”; in the verse: the episode of Raktabīja’s death
  • रक्तबीज raktabīja — bahuvrīhi

    Literally “he whose seed is blood”; in the verse: the asura from each drop of whose blood another like him arose (chapter 8)

C. Morphology

  • आख्यातम् ākhyātam — Past passive participle used as the main verb, with the agent in the instrumental (भवता): ‘has been told by you’.
  • भगवन् bhagavan — Vocative of भगवत्; the nominative is भगवान्. A form of address to a sage or god.
  • भवता bhavatā — Instrumental of भवत्, the polite ‘you’, which takes third-person verbs; here the agent of a passive participle.

D. Anvaya — prose order

भगवन्, भवता मम इदं रक्तबीजवधाश्रितं देव्याः चरितमाहात्म्यं विचित्रम् आख्यातम्।

bhagavan, bhavatā mama idaṃ raktabījavadhāśritaṃ devyāḥ caritamāhātmyaṃ vicitram ākhyātam |

A passive sentence: the subject चरितमाहात्म्यम् with its qualifiers, the participle आख्यातम् as verb, भवता the agent and मम the hearer; विचित्रम् is predicative.

6Translation

LiteralWonderful is this that has been told to me by you, O venerable one: the greatness of the Devī’s deeds, connected with the slaying of Raktabīja.

ReadableHoly sir, what you have told me is wonderful: the glory of the Goddess’s deeds in the slaying of Raktabīja.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningSuratha sums up chapter 8 — the Devī and the Mothers against Raktabīja, whose every drop of blood became a new asura until Kālī drank it — and calls it विचित्र, ‘marvellous’.

Traditional interpretationThe question keeps the frame of the text alive: the third (uttama) carita, like the first two, is told as Medhas’s answer to the king. The Gita Press pāṭha assigns this carita to Mahāsarasvatī as its presiding form; that is liturgical tradition, not a statement of the verse.

C. Morphology

  • इच्छामि icchāmi — Present of √इष् ‘to wish’: the tudādi present stem is इच्छ-. With the infinitive श्रोतुम्: ‘I wish to hear’.
  • निपातिते nipātite — Past participle of the causative पातय- with नि: ‘made to fall, felled’. In the locative with रक्तबीजे it forms a locative absolute: ‘Raktabīja having been felled’.
  • चकार cakāra — Perfect of √कृ (reduplicated च-कार). Singular with two subjects: it agrees with शुम्भः, and निशुम्भश्च is added after, a common pattern.

6Translation

LiteralAnd further I wish to hear what deed Śumbha did, and the exceedingly wrathful Niśumbha, when Raktabīja had been felled.

The relative clause is turned into a question for natural English.

6Translation

Direct textual meaningNiśumbha changes weapons and attacks not the Devī but her lion. The lion fights throughout the uttama carita and returns in 9.21 and 9.37.

Textual Verification Required / uncertain: The agent of निरस्ता is unexpressed; supplying the Goddess (देव्या) follows the context, not the words. महोल्का may be a weapon of hers or a meteor-like missile; the text does not say which.

5Vyākaraṇa

A. Sandhi and sandhi-viccheda

  • शूलेनाभिजघान śūlenābhijaghāna → शूलेन + अभिजघान
    vowel sandhi (savarṇa-dīrgha) · अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः (6.1.101)
    a + a → ā.
  • स तदाभिहतो sa tadābhihato → सः + तदा + अभिहतः
    visarga sandhi (special rule for सः), then vowel sandhi · एतत्तदोः सुलोपोऽकोरनञ्समासे हलि (6.1.132); अकः सवर्णे दीर्घः (6.1.101)
    The visarga of सः is dropped before the consonant t; तदा + अभिहतः gives ā; अभिहतः before the voiced bh becomes अभिहतो (हशि च, 6.1.114).
  • मूर्च्छितो निपपात mūrcchito nipapāta → मूर्च्छितः + निपपात
    visarga sandhi · ससजुषो रुः (8.2.66), हशि च (6.1.114), आद्गुणः (6.1.87)
    -aḥ before a voiced consonant becomes -o.

C. Morphology

  • अभिजघान abhijaghāna — Perfect of अभि + √हन्: the reduplicated syllable is ज (h → j), and the root’s h becomes gh after it (ज-घान); vṛddhi of the root vowel in the 3rd singular.
  • निपपात nipapāta — Perfect of नि + √पत्: reduplication प-पात with vṛddhi. ‘Fell down’.
  • मूर्च्छितः mūrcchitaḥ — Past participle of √मूर्च्छ् ‘to faint, lose consciousness’: Śumbha is stunned, not killed. He returns in chapter 10.

D. Anvaya — prose order

ततः क्रुद्धा सा चण्डिका शूलेन तम् अभिजघान। तदा अभिहतः सः मूर्च्छितः (सन्) भूमौ निपपात ह।

tataḥ kruddhā sā caṇḍikā śūlena tam abhijaghāna | tadā abhihataḥ saḥ mūrcchitaḥ (san) bhūmau nipapāta ha |

Two sentences: the blow (subject चण्डिका, instrument शूलेन, object तम्, verb अभिजघान) and its result (subject सः with the participles अभिहतः and मूर्च्छितः, verb निपपात).

6Translation

LiteralThen that Caṇḍikā, enraged, struck him with her spear. Struck then, he fell senseless on the ground.

ReadableThen Caṇḍikā in her anger struck him with her spear, and Śumbha, struck down, fell senseless to the earth.

‘Śumbha’ is supplied for सः.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningŚumbha is struck down and swoons. The verse says मूर्च्छितः, ‘fainted’: he is out of the fight for now, which is why Niśumbha, who had himself been felled in 9.16, rises again in the next verse.

Traditional interpretationThe narrative is careful with the two brothers: Niśumbha’s death comes first, in this chapter; Śumbha’s is kept for chapter 10, after the Goddess has drawn all her Śaktis back into herself.

  • सम्प्राप्य samprāpya — Absolutive of सम् + प्र + √आप् with ल्यप् (-य), the form used after prefixes: ‘having (re)gained’.
  • आजघान ājaghāna — Perfect of आ + √हन्, formed like अभिजघान in 9.28 (ज-घान).

Subject (निशुम्भः) with an absolutive phrase (चेतनां सम्प्राप्य) and an epithet (आत्तकार्मुकः); verb आजघान with three objects and the instrument शरैः.

6Translation

LiteralThen Niśumbha, having regained consciousness, bow taken up, struck with arrows the Goddess, Kālī and the lion as well.

ReadableThen Niśumbha came to his senses, seized his bow, and shot his arrows at the Goddess, at Kālī, and at the lion.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningNiśumbha, felled by the Goddess’s arrows in 9.16, recovers and returns to the fight while his brother lies stunned. He aims at all three: the Goddess, Kālī and the lion.

Traditional interpretationThe lion is named here by an epithet, केसरिन् ‘the maned one’. It fights throughout as a warrior in its own right (9.21, 9.37).

    Literally “born of Diti”; in the verse: a daitya — here Niśumbha

  • छादयामास chādayāmāsa — Periphrastic perfect: the causative/curādi stem छादय- takes -आम् and the perfect of √अस् (आस): छादयाम् + आस. Stems of more than one syllable form their perfect this way.
  • कृत्वा kṛtvā — Absolutive of √कृ with क्त्वा (no prefix, so not ल्यप्). With अयुतं बाहूनाम् it means ‘having made (for himself) ten thousand arms’ — Niśumbha multiplies his body.

6Translation

LiteralAnd again the lord of the Dānavas, the son of Diti, having made ten thousand arms, covered Caṇḍikā with discus-weapons.

ReadableAgain the Dānava lord took on ten thousand arms, and the son of Diti buried Caṇḍikā under a hail of discuses.

‘Took on’ renders कृत्वा, ‘having made’; ‘a hail of’ renders the collective singular चक्रायुधेन.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningNiśumbha’s second assault: he multiplies his arms to ten thousand and hurls discuses from all of them at once. Śumbha had shown eight arms in 9.18; the brothers’ powers of self-multiplication answer the Goddess’s many arms.

Traditional interpretationदनुज (son of Danu) and दितिज (son of Diti) are used interchangeably of the asuras in the text; both name Niśumbha here.

    Literally “split open by the spear of Kaumārī”; in the verse: pierced by Kaumārī’s spear (she came शक्तिहस्ता, ‘spear in hand’, 8.17)

    Literally “great asuras”; in the verse: the mightiest of the asuras

  • नेशुः neśuḥ — Perfect 3rd plural of √नश्. A root with a between two single consonants and an unchanged initial forms its weak perfect with e and no reduplication: नेशुः (like पेतुः from √पत्, 9.39).
  • केचित् kecit — Nominative plural masculine के of the interrogative किम् with the particle चित्, making it indefinite: ‘some’.
  • निराकृताः nirākṛtāḥ — Past passive participle of निर् + आ + √कृ ‘to drive away, repudiate’; it is the predicate of the second sentence: ‘were routed’.

6Translation

LiteralSome great asuras, pierced by Kaumārī’s spear, perished; others were driven off by water purified by Brahmāṇī’s mantras.

ReadableSome of the great asuras perished, pierced by Kaumārī’s spear; others were routed by water hallowed with Brahmāṇī’s mantras.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningThe Mothers (Mātṛs) who came out of the gods in 8.12–21 join the slaughter, each with the weapon she brought: Kaumārī the spear (8.17), Brahmāṇī the water of her kamaṇḍalu (8.15, 8.33).

Traditional interpretationThe word शक्ति means both the spear Kaumārī carries and the ‘power’ she herself is (the Mothers are the gods’ शक्तयः, 8.13; देवशक्तिभिः, 8.22). The verse uses it in the first sense, but the two are never far apart in this chapter.

B. Samāsa and samāsa-vigraha

  • महाहवात् mahāhavāt — karmadhāraya
    Members: महत् + आहव · Vigraha: महान् आहवः, तस्मात् mahān āhavaḥ, tasmāt
    Literally “from the great battle”; in the verse: from the field of the great battle
  • कालीशिवदूतीमृगाधिपैः kālīśivadūtīmṛgādhipaiḥ — itaretara dvandva (its last member मृगाधिप is a ṣaṣṭhī tatpuruṣa: मृगाणाम् अधिपः)
    Members: काली + शिवदूती + मृगाधिप · Vigraha: काली च शिवदूती च मृगाधिपः च, तैः kālī ca śivadūtī ca mṛgādhipaḥ ca, taiḥ
    Literally “by Kālī and Śivadūtī and the lord of beasts”; in the verse: by Kālī, Śivadūtī and the lion — the three devourers of 9.37

C. Morphology

  • विनेशुः vineśuḥ — Perfect 3rd plural of वि + √नश् with e and no reduplication, as नेशुः in 9.38. वि- strengthens the sense: ‘perished utterly’.
  • नष्टाः naṣṭāḥ — Past participle of √नश् (श् + त → ष्ट). Beside विनेशुः ‘perished’ it has its other sense, ‘were lost, vanished’: these asuras fled from the field.
  • भक्षिताः bhakṣitāḥ — Past passive participle of √भक्ष् (curādi) with the connecting vowel इ: भक्षित ‘eaten’.

D. Anvaya — prose order

केचित् असुराः विनेशुः, केचित् महाहवात् नष्टाः, अपरे च कालीशिवदूतीमृगाधिपैः भक्षिताः।

kecit asurāḥ vineśuḥ, kecit mahāhavāt naṣṭāḥ, apare ca kālīśivadūtīmṛgādhipaiḥ bhakṣitāḥ |

Three clauses, each with its own subject (केचित् … केचित् … अपरे): one finite verb (विनेशुः) and two participial predicates (नष्टाः, भक्षिताः).

6Translation

LiteralSome asuras perished; some vanished from the great battle; and others were devoured by Kālī, Śivadūtī and the lord of beasts.

ReadableSome of the asuras perished, some fled from the great battle, and others were devoured by Kālī, Śivadūtī and the lion.

‘Fled’ renders नष्टाः, literally ‘were lost (to the battle)’; set against विनेशुः ‘perished’, it means they escaped.

7Commentary

Direct textual meaningThe closing verse of the chapter sums up the rout: death, flight, and the devouring by Kālī, Śivadūtī and the lion. Śumbha alone remains, lying stunned (9.28), for the final battle of chapter 10.

Traditional interpretationThe Gita Press pāṭha closes the ninth chapter here, with the colophon that names it निशुम्भवधः, ‘the slaying of Niśumbha’; its 41 mantras include the two uvāca lines. Chapter 10 opens with Śumbha seeing his brother dead and his army being cut down (10.2).

Chapter conclusion

Narrative summary

Suratha asks what the asura brothers did after Raktabīja’s death, and Medhas tells of their last army and their duels with the Goddess. Niśumbha loses weapon after weapon and falls; Śumbha, towering in his chariot, is answered by the sounds of the Goddess and her companions, matches her arrows for a moment, and is struck senseless. Niśumbha rises, multiplies his arms, charges again and is pierced through the heart; a second figure comes out of him crying ‘Stop!’ and is beheaded by the laughing Goddess. Her lion, Kālī, Śivadūtī and the Mothers destroy or scatter the rest. The chapter ends with Śumbha still alive, lying stunned (9.28), for the last battle of chapter 10.

  • The periphrastic perfect of causative and curādi stems (ताडयामास, 9.10; पूरयामास, 9.20–21; छादयामास, 9.30).
  • Participles as the main verb: the past participle of motion (आगता, 9.15; निःसृतः, 9.35), the passive participle (निरस्ता, 9.25) and the past active participle in -तवत् (जितवान्, 9.26).
  • An impersonal passive of speech with its agent in the instrumental, in a यदा … तदा frame (जयेत्यभिहितं देवैः, 9.24).
  • One singular verb serving two or three subjects (चकार, 9.3, 9.5; चिच्छेद, 9.27; चखाद, 9.37).

  • Bahuvrīhis of the weapon in hand (परशुहस्तम्, 9.16; आत्तकार्मुकः, 9.29; शूलहस्तम्, 9.34).

Śākta insights

  • Nothing aimed at the Goddess reaches her: each of Niśumbha’s weapons is undone by a different one of hers, and once by her fist alone (9.11–16, 9.31–33).
  • Her power acts as sound before it acts as a blow: the bell destroys the tejas of the Daitya hosts (9.20), Kālī’s blow drowns every other sound (9.22), and at Śivadūtī’s laughter the asuras tremble (9.23).
  • Kālī, Śivadūtī and the Mothers are her own powers fighting as many; each Mother wields her god’s weapon, so the gods’ powers win as forms of the Goddess (8.13, 9.38–40), and in 10.5–6 she draws them back into herself.
  • Durgā is named here by what she does: she destroys the distress of those in hard straits (दुर्गार्तिनाशिनी, 9.31).
  • The gods do not fight in this carita; their part is to cry ‘Victory!’ from the sky as she challenges the asura (9.24), as later they will praise her in chapter 11.
  • The asura’s force is not ended by the first wound: something comes out of Niśumbha’s pierced heart still defiant, and only when it is cut down does he fall (9.35–36); the text leaves the figure unexplained.

Śrīvidyā connections

The chapter shares names with the Lalitā Sahasranāma (Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa): Caṇḍikā (755, चण्डिका), Ambikā (295, अम्बिका), Durgā (190, दुर्गा) — followed there by दुःखहन्त्री (191), much as 9.31 glosses Durgā as दुर्गार्तिनाशिनी — and Śivadūtī (405, शिवदूती), a name whose origin the Devī Māhātmya itself tells (8.23–28) and which the Lalitā Sahasranāma lists as a name. The battle images have parallels in the Lalitā Sahasranāma’s war with Bhaṇḍāsura: the Goddess answering the asura’s weapons with her own (79, भण्डासुरेन्द्रनिर्मुक्तशस्त्रप्रत्यस्त्रवर्षिणी; compare 9.10–16, 9.27) and accompanied by an army of śaktis (65, भण्डासुरवधोद्युक्तशक्तिसेनासमन्विता; compare the Mothers, 9.38–40). The stories are different, and these are shared Śākta names and images, not evidence that one text depends on the other; no specifically Śrīvidyā doctrine is stated in this chapter.

  • 9.22 — Kālī leaps up and strikes sky and earth with both hands, and every earlier sound is drowned in the din.
  • 9.24 — Ambikā cries ‘Stand, wicked one, stand!’, and the gods in the sky cry ‘Victory!’
  • 9.31 — Bhagavatī Durgā, destroyer of distress in hard straits, cuts down Niśumbha’s discuses and arrows.
  • 9.35 — From Niśumbha’s pierced heart comes another being, mighty and valiant, crying ‘Stop!’
  • 9.36 — Laughing aloud, the Goddess cuts off its head with her sword, and he falls: the slaying that names the chapter.

Sandhi patterns
  • Visarga sandhi is the commonest junction: -aḥ before voiced sounds → -o (शुम्भो यत्कर्म, ततो युद्धम्), visarga → ś/s before c/t (निशुम्भश्च, शिरश्चिच्छेद, पेतुस्तथापरे), and -aḥ + a → -o’ (निशुम्भोऽथ, 9.6; सोऽसुरः, 9.13; शतशोऽथ, 9.27; ततोऽसावपतद्भुवि, 9.36).
  • सः drops its visarga before consonants (स रथस्थः, 9.18; स तदाभिहतो, 9.28; स च, 9.33).
  • Final n before ś, t and c: n + ś → ñch (चिच्छेदास्ताञ्छरान्, 9.10; शुम्भमुक्ताञ्छरान्, 9.27); n → ṃs before t (शरांस्ताभ्याम्, 9.10; दुरात्मंस्तिष्ठ, 9.24; असुरांस्तान्, 9.37); n → ṃś before c (सायकांश्च, 9.31).
  • Final t assimilates: before a nasal → n (अभ्यधावन्निशुम्भो, 9.6; केचिन्नेशुः, 9.38; हृदयान्निःसृतः, 9.35), before c → c (केचिच्चूर्णीकृताः, 9.39), voiced before a voiced sound (केचिद्विनेशुः, 9.41; अपतद्भुवि, 9.36).

Grammatical observations
  • Weak perfects with e and no reduplication are taken as regular for their roots: त्रेसुः (9.23), नेशुः (9.38), पेतुः (9.39), विनेशुः (9.41).
  • √धाव् appears in both padas: parasmaipada अभ्यधावत् (9.6) and ātmanepada अभ्यधावत (9.32); the causative अपातयत (9.16) is ātmanepada too.
  • निर्घातनिःस्वनो … जितवान् (9.26) has no expressed object or source; the reading ‘overcame Śumbha’s roar’ follows the context, as does supplying the Goddess as the agent of निरस्ता (9.25).
  • Several sentences carry an unstated subject over from the previous verse (the Devī in 9.13 and 9.20; Śumbha in 9.17–18, where the verse names him only as the brother).
  • वैष्णव्या (9.40) is divided as the instrumental; the genitive वैष्णव्याः gives the same printed text.
  • In 9.9 शरवर्षम् is taken as the object of वर्षतोः; it can also stand in apposition to युद्धम्.
