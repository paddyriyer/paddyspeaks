surathavaiśyayorvarapradānam

29 mantras · 13.1–13.29

❁

Overview

Medhas ends his account: this is the greatness of the Goddess, by whom the world is upheld (13.2). He gives the answer to the question the king asked in chapter 1: knowledge too is the work of Viṣṇu’s Māyā, and by her the king, the merchant and the discerning are deluded, have been and will be (13.3–4). His counsel is a single imperative: take refuge in her, for, worshipped, she gives enjoyment, heaven and liberation (13.4–5). Mārkaṇḍeya takes up the story again (13.6). Suratha bows to the seer and, sick of his own clinging and of the loss of his kingdom, goes at once to practise austerity with the merchant, on the sandy bank of a river, to see the Mother (13.7–9). There they recite the hymn to the Goddess, make an image of her from the earth of the bank, and worship it with flowers, incense, fire-offerings and water, fasting or eating little, their minds fixed on her; they offer even an oblation sprinkled with their own blood (13.10–12). After three years Caṇḍikā, pleased, appears before them in person and offers each whatever he asks (13.13–15). The king chooses a kingdom that will not be lost in another birth and his own kingdom back in this one; the merchant, turned from the world, chooses the knowledge that frees from ‘mine’ and ‘I’ (13.16–18). The Goddess grants both: in a few days the king will regain his kingdom and rule unshaken, and after death, born of the Sun-god Vivasvat, he will be the Manu called Sāvarṇi; the merchant will have knowledge, for his final fulfilment (13.19–25). She vanishes as they praise her, and Mārkaṇḍeya closes with the promise of 1.2–3 kept: Suratha will be born of the Sun and become Sāvarṇi, the Manu (13.26–29).

About this chapter
Forms of the Devī
The Goddess by whom the world is upheld (यया इदं धार्यते जगत्, 13.2); the Māyā of the Lord Viṣṇu that works both delusion and knowledge (भगवद्विष्णुमाया, 13.3); the supreme Sovereign in whom one takes refuge (परमेश्वरी, 13.4); the giver of enjoyment, heaven and release (भोगस्वर्गापवर्गदा, 13.5); the Mother whom the two men go to see (अम्बा, 13.9); an image of clay on a river bank (मूर्तिः महीमयी, 13.10); and Caṇḍikā, mother and sustainer of the world, who appears in person (जगद्धात्री … चण्डिका, 13.13) and speaks her last words in the text (13.15, 13.20–25). The pāṭha’s dhyāna adds the form of the rising sun with noose, goad, boon and fearlessness.
Significance of the events
The chapter closes two stories at once. Medhas’s teaching, begun when the king asked why a man who knows better still clings (1.40–45), ends in counsel, not in argument: take refuge (13.4). And Mārkaṇḍeya’s frame, begun with the promise to tell how Sāvarṇi became the eighth Manu (1.2–3), ends with that promise kept (13.22–23, 13.29). Between the two lies the step the earlier chapters only describe: here the human hearers of the three stories worship for themselves, and the Goddess, who has so far appeared to gods and fought demons, appears to a king and a merchant and grants what each asks. The two requests, a kingdom and knowledge, are read in the tradition as the two goals named in 13.5, enjoyment and release.
Sanskrit patterns to watch
A short chapter with a clear grammar of narrative and promise. The perfect carries the story, including duals for the two worshippers (चक्रतुः 13.11, ददतुः 13.12) and the ātmanepada perfects तेपे (13.10) and वव्रे (13.17); a genitive absolute gives the circumstance (समाराधयतोः, 13.12). The Goddess speaks in futures: simple (प्राप्स्यते, भविष्यति, 13.20–25) and, in the last line, periphrastic (भविता, 13.29). Passives recur where the speaker keeps the agent in the background (धार्यते 13.2, क्रियते 13.3, मोह्यन्ते 13.4, प्रार्थ्यते and प्राप्यताम् 13.15). Absolutives link the actions (श्रुत्वा, प्रणिपत्य, हत्वा, सम्प्राप्य, दत्त्वा, लब्ध्वा, समासाद्य); भवान् takes a third-person verb (13.20, 13.23); and the merchant’s request quotes ‘mine’ and ‘I’ with इति (ममेत्यहमिति, 13.18).
The teaching, as distinct from the narrative
Literally, a sage finishes his story, a king and a merchant worship the Goddess for three years, she appears, and each receives what he asked for: a kingdom now and a Manu’s reign hereafter for the king, knowledge for the merchant. As teaching, the Śākta tradition reads the chapter as the practical end of the whole text: the power that deludes the wise is also the giver of knowledge (13.3), and the way out of delusion is not more argument but refuge in her (13.4). Worship is shown in its fullness, outward rite and inward collection together (13.10–12), and its fruit is direct vision (प्रत्यक्षम्, 13.13). The two boons are read as bhoga and apavarga (13.5); the Goddess grants both without judging either, but the text names precisely what the merchant’s knowledge undoes: the thoughts ‘mine’ and ‘I’ (13.18), the very mamatā Medhas diagnosed in chapter 1. These readings are the tradition’s and the reader’s; the chapter itself tells the story without comment.
Carita
Uttama Carita (the third episode, chapters 5–13), of which this is the last chapter, and the last chapter of the whole Devī Māhātmya. In the recitation tradition the presiding form of the third carita is Mahāsarasvatī; this is the Gita Press viniyoga (printed before chapter 5), not a statement of the Purāṇa text. In the Gita Press pāṭha chapter 13 has no viniyoga of its own, only a dhyāna: the auspicious one (शिवा), shining like the disc of the rising sun, four-armed and three-eyed, holding the noose and the goad and showing the gestures of boon and fearlessness (बालार्कमण्डलाभासां … शिवां भजे). The pāṭha also closes the carita with the bīja it opened with: क्लीं stands at the head of chapter 5 and after the last line here (13.29), as ऐं frames chapter 1 and ह्रीं chapters 2–4. The dhyāna and the bījas are liturgy; the chapter itself names none of them.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

Medhas closes his narrative. The compound देवीमाहात्म्यम् here names what he has told — the greatness of the Goddess — and it is the name the whole text bears in its colophons (देवीमाहात्म्ये).

Traditional interpretation

The verse joins two of her works: she has shown her power in the three stories, and she sustains (धार्यते) the world. Medhas had said the same in 12.36: she, though eternal, comes forth again and again to protect the world.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

Medhas returns to the question with which the king began (1.40–45): why do men who know better remain bound? The answer is the one given in chapter 1: Viṣṇu’s Māyā. Here he adds the other side — knowledge too (विद्या) is her work.

Traditional interpretation

The verse restates 1.57–58: she is the supreme knowledge (विद्या परमा), the cause of release, and also the cause of the bondage of saṃsāra. Delusion and knowledge come from one power; that is why the remedy proposed in 13.4 is to take refuge in her.

Comparative Śrīvidyā notes

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

The king bows to his teacher and goes. Two causes of his weariness are named together: the inner one (excessive ममत्व, the sense of ‘mine’) and the outer one (the loss of the kingdom). The first is what the teaching diagnosed (1.41: ममत्वं गतराज्यस्य).

Modern reflection

A modern reading: the verse puts the private cause before the public one. Suratha’s grief came less from what he had lost than from how tightly he had held it.

Direct textual meaning

Direct textual meaning

The worship reaches its height: an offering (बलि) sprinkled with the worshippers’ own blood. The text states it without comment; it marks how far their devotion went. Three years of such worship pass before the Goddess appears.

Traditional interpretation

The offering is of themselves, not of another life: the vow costs the worshippers their own blood. Their wholeness of intent (यतात्मनोः, ‘self-controlled’) is what the verse stresses as the condition of the Goddess’s appearing in the next line.

Modern reflection

A modern reading: what is offered is the body that had served attachment. Their worship spends what they had spent their lives protecting.

Direct textual meaning

The tradition reads the merchant’s choice as apavarga, liberation — the last of the gifts in 13.5. The two men, who came to the seer with the same affliction, leave with the two great goals: enjoyment and release.

Modern reflection

A modern reading: the merchant had more reason than the king to ask for his property back. He asks instead to be free of needing it.

Śākta commentary

Direct textual meaning

The last line of the Devī Māhātmya returns to its first subject. Mārkaṇḍeya had promised to tell how Sāvarṇi, son of the Sun, became the eighth Manu (1.2–3); the answer is this story — a king who lost everything, took refuge in the Goddess, and will be born of the Sun as Sāvarṇi.

Traditional interpretation

In the Gita Press pāṭha the mantra is followed by the bījas क्लीं ॐ, which close the recitation and belong to the pāṭha, not to the Purāṇa. The colophon then names the chapter ‘the granting of boons to Suratha and the merchant’ (सुरथवैश्ययोर्वरप्रदानम्) and completes the count of seven hundred.

Modern reflection

A modern reading: the text ends not with the Goddess but with a human future. Its last verb is ‘will be’ (भविता) — the story of her power closes as a promise about what a person will become.

Reflecting on the chapter

The Devī Māhātmya ends where it began. In chapter 1 a king who had lost his kingdom and a merchant thrown out by his family asked why they still clung to what had rejected them, and Medhas answered with a name, Mahāmāyā, and with three stories to show who she is. In the first (chapter 1) she does not fight at all: as Yoganidrā she withdraws from the sleeping Viṣṇu, who wakes and kills Madhu and Kaiṭabha. In the second (chapters 2–4) she takes form from the gods’ own radiance, destroys Mahiṣa and his armies, and when the gods praise her promises to come whenever she is remembered. In the third (chapters 5–13) she comes forth from Pārvatī’s body, destroys Śumbha, Niśumbha and their generals, declares that the many goddesses are herself alone, and, praised as Nārāyaṇī, promises to return whenever the world is oppressed. Chapter 12 names the fruits of hearing these stories. Chapter 13 turns the hearers into worshippers. Medhas’s last word is ‘take refuge’ (13.4); the two men go to a river bank, worship an image of clay for three years, and see the Goddess face to face (13.13). The king asks for his kingdom and receives more than he knew to ask for, a Manu’s age (13.17, 13.20–23); the merchant asks for the knowledge that ends ‘mine’ and ‘I’ and receives it (13.18, 13.25). She vanishes, as she vanished from the gods after the second story (बभूवान्तर्हिता, 4.39 and 13.28), and the last line answers the first promise of the text: Suratha will be born of the Sun and become Sāvarṇi, the Manu (13.29; compare 1.2–3).

Important grammatical constructions

  • The perfect dual for the two worshippers acting together (चक्रतुः, 13.11; ददतुः, 13.12).
  • The genitive absolute for a circumstance (एवं समाराधयतोः … यतात्मनोः, ‘while they were worshipping thus’, 13.12), with the main clause in the next line (13.13).

Śākta insights

  • The power that deludes is the power that gives knowledge: Medhas’s last teaching (13.3) restates 1.57–58, so the text ends on the doctrine with which it began.
  • No one is outside her delusion, not even the discerning, in any time (13.3–4); the remedy offered is not insight but refuge (13.4).
  • Worship is both outward and inward: flowers, incense, fire and water, and also fasting, a mind fixed on her and collected (13.10–11). Its fruit is to see her (13.9, 13.13).
  • She gives what is asked, and gives each seeker his own goal: enjoyment to the king, release to the merchant (13.5, 13.17–18, 13.20–25). Medhas had said as much: asked, and pleased, she gives knowledge and prosperity (12.37).
  • Her gift exceeds the request: the king asks for an imperishable kingdom and is made a Manu, ruler of an age (13.17, 13.22–23).
  • The knowledge the merchant receives is named exactly: the end of ‘mine’ and ‘I’ (13.18), the mamatā that bound both men in chapter 1 (1.41, 1.53).

Śrīvidyā connections

The chapter shares names with the Lalitā Sahasranāma (Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa): Viṣṇumāyā (339, विष्णुमाया) and Vidyā (549, विद्या) with 13.3; Parameśvarī (396, परमेश्वरी) with 13.4; स्वर्गापवर्गदा (764), ‘giver of heaven and release’, closely parallel to भोगस्वर्गापवर्गदा in 13.5; Caṇḍikā (755, चण्डिका) and Jagaddhātrī (935, जगद्धात्री) with 13.13; and ज्ञानदा (643) and मुक्तिदा (736), giver of knowledge and of liberation, the gifts of 13.25. The pāṭha’s dhyāna for this chapter, the Goddess bright as the rising sun with noose and goad, recalls the Lalitā Sahasranāma’s उद्यद्भानुसहस्राभा (6), रागस्वरूपपाशाढ्या (8) and क्रोधाकाराङ्कुशोज्ज्वला (9); but its other hands show boon and fearlessness, not Lalitā’s sugarcane bow and flower arrows, and the dhyāna is liturgy, not the Purāṇa text. These are shared Śākta names and images, not evidence that one text depends on the other; no specifically Śrīvidyā doctrine is stated in this chapter.

Sandhi patterns
  • Visarga sandhi is the commonest junction: -aḥ before voiced consonants → -o (सद्यो भक्त्या, सावर्णिको नाम), visarga → s/ś before t/c (ददतुस्तौ, यश्च), visarga → r after other vowels (ऋषिरुवाच, मनुर्भवान्, सावर्णिर्भविता), and the special rule for सः and एषः (स नराधिपः, त्वमेष वैश्यश्च).
  • Pūrvarūpa: -o + a- → avagraha (निर्विण्णोऽतिममत्वेन, सोऽपि, वरोऽस्मत्तोऽभिवाञ्छितः).

  • Ṣaṣṭhī tatpuruṣas name the text and its persons (देवीमाहात्म्यम्, नराधिपः, जगद्धात्री, कुलनन्दन, क्षत्रियर्षभः).
  • Bahuvrīhis describe the worshippers (शंसितव्रतम्, निर्विण्णमानसः, and the duals निराहारौ, यताहारौ, तन्मनस्कौ, यतात्मनोः).
  • An itaretara dvandva lists the gifts and the offerings (भोगस्वर्गापवर्ग, पुष्पधूपाग्नितर्पणैः); the first stands inside the upapada compound भोगस्वर्गापवर्गदा.
  • Nested tatpuruṣas: निजगात्रासृगुक्षितम्, सङ्गविच्युतिकारकम्, भगवद्विष्णुमायया, नदीपुलिनसंस्थितः.
  • Nañ tatpuruṣas (अविभ्रंशि, अस्खलितम्) and avyayībhāvas (प्रत्यक्षम्, यथाभिलषितम्).
Grammatical observations
  • प्राप्स्यते (13.20) has an ātmanepada ending on a parasmaipada root, read as a licence of Epic and Purāṇic verse, not as a passive.
  • तेपे (13.10) is the ātmanepada perfect of √तप् with तपस् as object; वव्रे (13.17) the ātmanepada perfect of √वृ ‘to choose’.
  • The singulars जगाम and नदीपुलिनसंस्थितः (13.9) and तपस्तेपे (13.10) agree with the nearest subject, though both men act; the dual returns with तौ (13.10).
  • हत्वा (13.21) has its agent only in the genitive तव, a loose but common Purāṇic construction; the recipient of a gift stands in the genitive (तयोः, 13.27).
  • संसिद्ध्यै (13.25) is the optional dative in -ऐ of an i-stem, with the dh doubled before y as the Gita Press prints it.
  • The last line uses the periphrastic future भविता (13.29), the luṭ of a future not of today.

Open questions
  • 13.11: निराहारौ यताहारौ (Gita Press) / निराहारौ यतात्मानौ (GRETIL, Freund) — Textual Verification Required; the Gita Press reading is analysed.
  • 13.29: the digital source repeats 13.28cd–13.29 as a block numbered 30; the repetition is removed on the colophon’s count of 29 mantras (700 in all), and the closing क्लीं ॐ placed after 13.29 (a structural correction in corrections.json, not a variant).
  • Interpretive doubts, none about the reading itself, are flagged on 13.3 (the terse विद्या तथैव क्रियते), 13.9 (singular नदीपुलिनसंस्थितः; the listener addressed as महामुने), 13.10 (स च वैश्यः: the king and the merchant, or the merchant too) and 13.25 (which clause संसिद्ध्यै goes with); 13.11 carries the variant above.
