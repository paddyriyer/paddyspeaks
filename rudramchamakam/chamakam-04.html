चमकम् · Chamakam · Anuvāka 4

चतुर्थोऽनुवाकःFood, plenty and the grains

Taittirīya Saṃhitā 4.7.4 · 6 sense-groups for study · 114 words

✲

The worshipper asks for nourishment and its kinds — milk, its essence, ghee and honey — for meals and drink taken together, for ploughing and rain, for wealth and the body’s nourishment, for seven rising measures of plenty, and then names twelve grains and pulses, from rice to wild rice. Sāyaṇa glosses each: payas and what follows are particular foods, vibhu to akṣiti are seven increasing measures of grain, and even poor barley (kūyava) is asked for. Every item is followed by ca me, and the verb kalpatām is supplied from the tenth anuvāka.

Samāsa & samāsa-vigraha

Vyākaraṇa

  • ऊर्क् ūrk

    Nominative singular of the feminine root noun ūrj; a root noun takes no ending in the nom. sg., and the final j becomes k (P. 8.2.30). Sāyaṇa begins his comment with the mantra’s opening words, «ऊर्क् च म इति».

  • मे me

    Enclitic me (for mama / mahyam). As in every Chamakam anuvāka before the tenth, the verb is not written here: Sāyaṇa supplies kalpatām from anuvāka 10 to every item.

  • च ca

    Every item is followed by ca me; the refrain is part of the text and is recited with each item.

Translation

LiteralNourishment and for me, gladness and for me, milk and for me, essence and for me, ghee and for me, honey and for me [may they be ready].

In readable EnglishMay nourishment be mine, and kindly speech, milk and its essence, ghee and honey.

Uncertain: Sāyaṇa’s gloss on ūrj is damaged in the witness (ऊर्गंन्नसामान्यम्); the sense ‘food in general’ (ūrk + anna-sāmānyam) is clear, but the gloss is not quoted for that reason.

Interpretation

Traditional interpretation · after Sāyaṇa

Sāyaṇa takes ūrj as food in general, and sūnṛtā as «प्रियोक्तिः», pleasant speech. Milk and the items after it are particular kinds of food: «पयः प्रभृतयोऽन्नविशेषाः». Payas is «क्षीरम्», milk; rasa is «तत्रत्यं सारम्», the essence found in it; ghee and honey he calls well known («घृतमधुनी प्रसिद्धे»).

Vyākaraṇa

  • सग्धिः sagdhiḥ

    Formed with sa- ‘together’ and gdhi, a short form of jagdhi ‘eating’ (Monier-Williams). The dictionaries do not settle its compound class, so it is not analysed here as a samāsa.

  • सपीतिः sapītiḥ

    sa- ‘together’ + pīti ‘drinking’ (√pā); a pair with sagdhiḥ. As with sagdhiḥ, it is not analysed as a samāsa.

  • जैत्रम् jaitram

    Vṛddhi derivative connected with √ji ‘conquer’; neuter, used as a noun.

  • औद्भिद्यम् audbhidyam

    Vṛddhi derivative of udbhid ‘breaking through, sprouting’ (ud + √bhid); a neuter abstract.

Translation

LiteralEating together and for me, drinking together and for me, ploughing and for me, rain and for me, victory and for me, breaking forth and for me [may they be ready].

In readable EnglishMay meals eaten together be mine, and drink shared, ploughing and rain, a fruitful field, and the springing up of plants.

Reading: corrections.json, C4.2: the second witness reads शपीति; it is a W2 error. The text keeps सपीतिः, which Sāyaṇa’s gloss सहपानम्, beside सग्धिः ‘eating together’, confirms.

Interpretation: The dictionaries give jaitra as ‘victory, victorious’ and audbhidya as ‘breaking through, success, victory’. Sāyaṇa reads both in the setting of food and harvest: a good field, and the coming up of plants. The word table gives both; the readable translation follows Sāyaṇa.

Interpretation

Traditional interpretation · after Sāyaṇa

Sāyaṇa: sagdhi is eating together with one’s kin («सग्धिर्बन्धुभिः सह भोजनमित्यर्थः»), and sapīti likewise drinking together («सहपानम्»). Ploughing and rain are well known as the causes of food («कृषिवृष्टी अन्नस्य हेतुत्वेन प्रसिद्धे»). Jaitra he explains as «जयशीलं सुक्षेत्रमित्यर्थः», ‘winning’, that is, a good field; audbhidya as the springing up of what breaks through the ground, «तरुगुल्मादीना मुत्पत्तिः», trees, shrubs and the like.

Contemplative reflection · modern, not a translation

Before grain and wealth, the list asks for meals and drink taken in company: food is wanted as something shared.

Samāsa & samāsa-vigraha

Vyākaraṇa

  • रायः rāyaḥ

    Nom. pl. of rai (m.), the stem that stands beside rayi; rai becomes rāy before a vowel ending (rāy-aḥ).

  • विभु vibhu

    vibhu, prabhu and bahu are neuter adjectives standing as nouns, with no ending in the nom. sg.; hence vibhu ca, not vibhuś ca. The pañcāśat mark [7] stands after this ca: no sandhi across it.

  • भूयः bhūyaḥ

    Comparative of bahu, bhūyas (P. 6.4.158 bahor lopo bhū ca bahoḥ), neuter nom. sg.

Translation

LiteralWealth and for me, riches and for me, abundance and for me, nourishment and for me, the ample and for me, the abundant and for me, much and for me, more and for me [may they be ready].

In readable EnglishMay wealth be mine, and riches, abundance and nourishment; the ample, the abundant, much and more.

Interpretation

Traditional interpretation · after Sāyaṇa

Sāyaṇa: rayi is gold («रयिः सुवर्णम्»), rāyaḥ gems, pearls and the like («रायो मणिमुक्तादिः»), puṣṭa the same gold in great abundance («पुष्टं पूर्वोक्तमेव सुवर्णमतिसमृद्धम्»), and puṣṭi the nourishment of the body («शरीरपोषः»). From vibhu to akṣiti (in the next unit) he reads seven measures of grain, each larger than the one before: «विभ्वादयोऽक्षित्यन्ता धान्यविषया उत्तरोत्तराभिवृद्धयः सप्तापि तारतम्येन द्रष्टव्याः».

Vyākaraṇa

  • पूर्णतरम् pūrṇataram

    pūrṇa with the comparative suffix -tara (P. 5.3.57).

  • कूयवाः kūyavāḥ

    Masculine plural, like the grain names that follow. The first member is kū-, with a long vowel, for ku- ‘bad, poor’.

  • अक्षुत् akṣut

    Nom. sg. of the feminine root noun a-kṣudh; the final dh becomes t at the end of the word and c before ca (akṣuc ca).

Translation

LiteralFull and for me, fuller and for me, imperishableness and for me, poor barley and for me, food and for me, freedom from hunger and for me [may they be ready].

In readable EnglishMay I have fullness, and greater fullness, and what does not run out; poor grain too, and food, and freedom from hunger.

Interpretation

Traditional interpretation · after Sāyaṇa

Pūrṇa, pūrṇatara and akṣiti close Sāyaṇa’s seven measures of grain. Then, he says, not only abundant grain is asked for but small amounts too, and the text names kūyavāḥ, «कूयवाः कुत्सितयवाः», poor barley. Food he calls well known («अन्नं प्रसिद्धम्»); akṣut is the end of hunger that food achieves: «अक्षुदन्नसाध्यः क्षुत्परिहारः».

Vyākaraṇa

  • व्रीहयः vrīhayaḥ

    Nom. pl. of the i-stem vrīhi, with guṇa before the ending (vrīhay-aḥ). The grains are named in the plural, as kinds of seed.

Translation

LiteralRice and for me, barley and for me, beans and for me, sesame and for me, mung beans and for me, khalva grains and for me [may they be ready].

In readable EnglishMay rice be mine, and barley, beans, sesame, mung beans and khalva.

Uncertain: khalva is only ‘a kind of grain or leguminous plant’ in the dictionaries, and Sāyaṇa says only that its seeds are larger than mudga. No closer identification is given here.

Interpretation

Traditional interpretation · after Sāyaṇa

Sāyaṇa calls rice, barley, beans, sesame and mung beans well known («प्रसिद्धाः»; the witness spells the list «व्रीहियवमापतिलमुद्राः», with damaged letters for māṣa and mudga). Of khalva he says only that its seeds are larger even than those of mudga: «खल्वा मुद्गेभ्योऽपि स्थूलवीजाः».

Vyākaraṇa

  • मसुराः masurāḥ

    The text has short u; the dictionaries give both masura and masūra.

  • प्रियङ्गवः priyaṅgavaḥ

    Nom. pl. of the u-stem priyaṅgu, with guṇa before the ending (-av-aḥ); so also aṇavaḥ from aṇu.

Translation

LiteralWheat and for me, lentils and for me, panic seed and for me, fine grains and for me, śyāmāka millet and for me, wild rice and for me [may they be ready].

In readable EnglishMay wheat be mine, and lentils, millet, fine rice, śyāmāka, and wild rice.

Interpretation: aṇu as a grain is a millet (Panicum miliaceum) in the dictionaries; Sāyaṇa takes aṇavaḥ as fine-grained rice (सूक्ष्मशालयः). The word table gives both; the translation follows Sāyaṇa in the readable version.

Interpretation

Traditional interpretation · after Sāyaṇa

Sāyaṇa calls wheat and priyaṅgu well known («प्रसिद्धाः»). Lentils, like mung beans, are the source of pulse broth: «मसुरा मुद्गवत्सूपहेतवः». Aṇavaḥ he takes as fine-grained rice («सूक्ष्मशालयः»). Of the last two, śyāmāka is the grain of the village and nīvāra the grain of the forest: «श्यामाका ग्राम्या धान्यविशेषाः । नीवारा आरण्याः».

Contemplative reflection · modern, not a translation

The list of what is wanted ends in the plainest things: twelve kinds of grain, down to the wild rice no one sowed.
