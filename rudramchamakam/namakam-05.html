नमकम् · Namakam · Anuvāka 5

पञ्चमोऽनुवाकःHomage to Bhava and Rudra: his names, his forms, his waters

Taittirīya Saṃhitā 4.5.5 · 15 yajus · 75 words

✲

Fifteen yajus, each opening with namaḥ and naming two aspects of Rudra in the dative, each followed by ca. Sāyaṇa notes that the yajus with homage on both sides ended with the fourth anuvāka and that from the fifth to the ninth each yajus opens with homage alone. The names move from Bhava, Rudra, Śarva and Paśupati, through his appearance (blue neck and white throat, braided or shaven hair, a thousand eyes, a hundred bows), his size, age and priority, to his swiftness and his presence in the current, the wave, still water, the stream and the island. Sāyaṇa explains the two ca of each yajus as letting namaḥ go with each name separately.

    visarga sandhi

    -aḥ before a voiced consonant becomes -o (P. 6.1.114)

Samāsa & samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this unit; every word is a simple word.

Vyākaraṇa

  • नमः namaḥ

    From this anuvāka to the ninth every yajus opens with namaḥ and does not close with it, as those of the third and fourth did. ‘Be’ is understood.

  • भवाय bhavāya

    bhava, from √bhū ‘to be, to come to be’. Dative with namaḥ (P. 2.3.16).

  • च ca

    Each yajus of this anuvāka has two datives, each followed by ca. Sāyaṇa explains the pair at the end of the anuvāka: the ca are conjunctive and are read so that namaḥ may be construed with each dative on its own — ‘homage to A, and [homage] to B’.

Translation

LiteralHomage to Bhava and to Rudra.

In readable EnglishHomage to Bhava, and to Rudra.

Interpretation

Traditional interpretation · after Sāyaṇa

Sāyaṇa marks the turn in the hymn: the yajus with homage on both sides ended with the fourth anuvāka («चतुर्थेऽनुवाक उभयतोनमस्काराणि यजूंषि समापितानि»), and from the fifth the yajus open with homage alone («नमस्कारोपक्रमाण्येव»). He takes Bhava as the one from whom living beings come to be («भवन्ति प्राणिनोऽस्मादिति भवः»), and Rudra as the one who drives away sorrow, the cause of weeping («रुद्रोदनहेतुभूतं दुःखं द्रावयतीति रुद्रः»).

Vyākaraṇa

  • शर्वाय śarvāya

    Monier-Williams derives śarva from śaru ‘arrow’ and describes Śarva as a god who kills with arrows; Sāyaṇa derives it from √śṝ ‘to crush’ (शृणाति).

  • पशुपतये paśupataye

    pati as the last member of a compound declines like an i-stem (patiḥ samāsa eva, P. 1.4.8): dative paśupataye.

Translation

LiteralHomage to Śarva and to the lord of cattle.

In readable EnglishHomage to Śarva, and to Paśupati, the lord of beasts.

Interpretation: The dictionaries connect śarva with śaru ‘arrow’ (‘the archer’); Sāyaṇa explains it from √śṝ, ‘he who crushes sin’. The word table gives both; the translation keeps the name.

Interpretation

Traditional interpretation · after Sāyaṇa

Sāyaṇa: Śarva is the one who crushes and destroys sin («शृणाति हिनस्ति पापमिति शर्वः»). Paśupati is the one who protects men («पुरुषान्पालयतीति पशुपतिः») who are like cattle for want of knowledge (the printed text, broken by a page number, reads «पशुसमानानज्ञा» … «निनः», that is paśusamānān ajñāninaḥ).

Vyākaraṇa

  • नीलग्रीवाय nīlagrīvāya

    In the bahuvrīhi the feminine nīlā (agreeing with grīvā) takes its masculine form nīla (P. 6.3.34), and the whole compound is masculine, agreeing with Rudra.

  • शितिकण्ठाय śitikaṇṭhāya

    śiti ‘white’; Monier-Williams also records ‘black, dark-blue’ from later literature. Sāyaṇa takes ‘white’.

  • च ca

Translation

LiteralHomage to the blue-necked one and to the white-throated one.

In readable EnglishHomage to him whose neck is blue, and to him whose throat is white.

Interpretation

Traditional interpretation · after Sāyaṇa

Sāyaṇa reconciles the two names. Part of the neck is blue from holding the Kālakūṭa poison («कालकूटधारणेन नीलवर्णो ग्रीवैकदेशो यस्यासौ नीलग्रीवः»); the rest of the throat is white («शितिः श्वेतवर्णोऽवशिष्टः कण्ठप्रदेशो यस्यासौ शितिकण्ठः»).

Contemplative reflection · modern, not a translation

Two colours are named on one throat, side by side. Sāyaṇa does not choose between them; he places both.

Interpretation

Traditional interpretation · after Sāyaṇa

He resolves the contrast by guise: the matted locks in the dress of the Pāśupatas and others («पाशुपतादिवेषेण कपर्दित्वम्»), the shaven head in the dress of ascetics («यत्यादिवेषेण मुण्डितकेशत्वम्»).

Sāyaṇa: śīghriya is the one who goes quickly («शीघ्रियः शीघ्रगामी»); for śībhya he takes the base word (printed शीम) as denoting a current of water («उदकप्रवाहवाची») and śībhya as the one present in it.

Sandhi & sandhi-viccheda

  • नम ऊर्म्याय nama ūrmyāya → नमः + ऊर्म्याय

    visarga sandhi (before a vowel)

    -aḥ before a vowel other than a: the visarga becomes y and is dropped (P. 8.3.17, 8.3.19); the two vowels then stay apart

  • चावस्वन्याय cāvasvanyāya → च + अवस्वन्याय

    vowel sandhi (savarṇa-dīrgha)

    a + a → ā (akaḥ savarṇe dīrghaḥ, P. 6.1.101)

Samāsa & samāsa-vigraha

No compound in this unit; every word is a simple word.

Vyākaraṇa

  • ऊर्म्याय ūrmyāya

    ūrmi ‘wave’ with the suffix -ya, ‘being in’; Monier-Williams ‘undulating, wavy’.

  • अवस्वन्याय avasvanyāya

    ava + svana ‘sound’ with -ya. Sāyaṇa takes avasvana as soundless, still water; Monier-Williams gives avasvanya ‘roaring’.

  • च ca

    Each yajus of this anuvāka has two datives, each followed by ca. Sāyaṇa explains the pair at the end of the anuvāka: the ca are conjunctive and are read so that namaḥ may be construed with each dative on its own — ‘homage to A, and [homage] to B’.

Translation

LiteralHomage to the one in the wave and to the one in still water.

In readable EnglishHomage to him who is in the waves, and to him who is in the still water.

Interpretation: Monier-Williams gives avasvanya ‘roaring’; Sāyaṇa takes ava-svana as soundless, still water (ध्वनिरहिते). The translation follows Sāyaṇa; the word table gives both.

Interpretation

Traditional interpretation · after Sāyaṇa

Sāyaṇa: ūrmya is the one present in the wave («ऊर्मौ तरङ्गे स्थित ऊर्म्यः»); avasvanya the one present in still water without sound («अवस्वने ध्वनिरहिते स्थिरजलेऽवस्थितोऽवस्वन्यः»).

Contemplative reflection · modern, not a translation

In Sāyaṇa’s reading the pair is the moving wave and the silent pool; both are places where Rudra is named.

LiteralHomage to the one in the stream and to the one on the island.

In readable EnglishHomage to him who is in the stream, and to him who is on the island.

Interpretation

Traditional interpretation · after Sāyaṇa

Sāyaṇa: srotasya is the one present in the current («प्रवाहे स्थितः»); dvīpya the one present on the island, the land in the midst of the water («द्वीपे वारिमध्यवर्तिभूमौ स्थितो»). He closes the anuvāka with the rule for every yajus in it: the two ca are read as conjunctive so that namaḥ is construed with each of the two datives separately («अत्रैकैकस्मिन्यजुषि चतुर्थ्यन्ताभ्यां पदाभ्यां नमःशब्दं पृथगन्वेतुं समुच्चयार्थौ चकारौ पठितौ»).
